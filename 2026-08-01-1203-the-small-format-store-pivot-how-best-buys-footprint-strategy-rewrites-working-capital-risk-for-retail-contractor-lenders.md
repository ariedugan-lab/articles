Best Buy's incoming CEO Jason Bonfig wants to expand by opening smaller stores. That is not a merchandising detail. For small business lenders, it is a structural signal: the retailer is moving capital from giant showrooms to compact, service-heavy hubs. Every square foot removed from a store plan changes where inventory sits, who gets paid, and when.

Funders should read this as a working capital event, not a consumer electronics story.

## The Buildout Cycle Is a Lending Vertical

Smaller stores require buildout. Electrification, HVAC, low-voltage wiring, shelving, signage, flooring, fire safety. General contractors and subcontractors work on multiple sites. They get progress payments, but material costs outpace advances. Payment terms from general contractors can stretch 60 to 90 days. A regional contractor with a Best Buy remodel contract needs working capital before the first draw. Lenders who underwrite based on last year's tax returns miss this.

Funder action: create a specific retail buildout working capital product. Underwrite contract value, not just bank balances. Ask for a schedule of values, percentage of completion, and change orders. Use invoice-level data from general contractors. Brokers should build a target list of electrical, HVAC, and low-voltage contractors licensed in counties where Best Buy is opening small stores. The quote pipeline is the credit signal.

## Inventory Moves Downstream

Small-format stores carry fewer SKUs. Best Buy will rely on its supply chain for ship-to-store and buy-online-pickup-in-store. That shifts inventory from store shelves to distribution centers. For small businesses that supply Best Buy directly, smaller stores mean smaller but more frequent replenishment orders. A vendor used to one bulk order per quarter now has multiple weekly purchase orders. That changes the cash conversion cycle. The vendor must finance more frequent picking, packing, and freight without a matching change in payment terms.

Lenders need to adjust inventory financing models. In a small-format retail model, purchase order volume does not equal inventory value at risk. Underwrite to weeks of supply, not revenue multipliers. If a borrower's customer concentration in Best Buy increases, haircut the advance rate or require a covenant on days sales outstanding. A broker should not pitch a merchant cash advance to a vendor just because sales are up. Ask whether the increased sales are tied to lower-margin fulfillment costs.

## The Service Network Is the New Storefront

Best Buy's smaller stores are built around services: installation, repair, home theater setup, delivery. The physical store becomes a dispatch point. That creates demand for independent technicians and local service providers. Many are small businesses with two to ten vehicles. They invoice Best Buy under national service agreements. Payment cycles are long. Seasonal spikes are severe.

This is a strong MCA and term loan vertical: service contractors with purchase orders, a vehicle fleet, and predictable chargebacks. But underwriters must verify the actual relationship with Best Buy. A third-party servicer might subcontract to a local company. The local company's revenue is dependent on a sub-agreement that can be cancelled at will. Underwrite to contract longevity and average ticket size. A single large job from a retail service network can mask an otherwise fragile cash position.

Brokers and ISO networks should target appliance repair, TV mounting, and audio installation businesses. The signal is not Geek Squad growth. The signal is that a small-format expansion shifts labor to local subcontractors. If a broker can source a business that already holds a vendor code with a major retailer, the funding angle is clear.

## Anchor Economics Are Changing

Small-format stores also change the anchor-tenant risk in strip centers and power centers. A 50,000-square-foot Best Buy closing leaves a hole. A 20,000-square-foot Best Buy opening in the same market may not fill the same parking lot. Small businesses adjacent to old big-box locations lose foot traffic. New small-format locations can create a new micro-economy in a different strip center.

Lenders with real estate exposure need to compare the retailer's old and new footprints at the local level. Do not treat a Best Buy location as a stable credit proxy. The company is intentionally shrinking its real estate footprint while increasing site count. For a landlord borrower, an empty former big-box is a vacancy risk, and a nearby new small store is not the same tenant quality. Update underwriting models for store format migration.

Brokers should look for commercial mortgage brokers handling retail properties. They need to know whether a current Best Buy lease is at risk of replacement with a smaller format. Ask the landlord whether Best Buy has exercised lease modification rights. That question changes valuation.

## What This Means for Underwriting Models

Best Buy's strategy is not a one-off headline. It is part of a broader retail movement: capital-light store portfolios, smaller footprints, and service-embedded locations. For small business lenders, the implications are threefold.

First, sources of repayment are shifting from product sales to service attachments and delivery contracts. Cash flow is real but delayed. Use working capital products that match invoice timing, not daily revenue smoothing.

Second, asset-based lending needs to treat inventory differently. Small-format retail shrinks store inventory, pushes stock to central distribution centers, and makes local vendors part-time suppliers. A vendor's balance sheet will have more receivables and less inventory. A lender that underwrites inventory will miss both the risk and the opportunity.

Third, concentration risk is rising. The more Best Buy centralizes services and supply chains, the more small businesses become dependent on one payer. A policy change from Best Buy can cut a small business's revenue overnight. Underwriters must run what-if scenarios for payer concentration. This is not speculative. The company is making a strategic pivot, and every subcontractor in that ecosystem is exposed.

## Operator Moves

For funders: create a standalone underwriting addendum for retailers and their vendor ecosystems. Include a large-payer concentration schedule. Require borrowers to disclose top three customers as a percentage of revenue. For borrowers above 30 percent concentration, structure a cash reserve or a lower maximum advance.

For brokers: source contractors in the retail buildout supply chain. Search for licensed electricians, HVAC contractors, and signage companies in counties with new small-format store permits. Use permit data to build a target list. Send a direct message about a pre-underwritten working capital line. The building permit is the lead generator.

For ISO networks: train agents on the distinction between inventory-based borrowers and receivables-based borrowers. A small-format retail vendor is a different credit profile than a big-box vendor. Use merchant cash advance underwriting that uses daily settlement data, but validate the repayment source with contract invoices. Do not rely on historical bank statements alone when a strategy shift is underway.

The go small strategy will be watched as a margin experiment. For small business lenders, it is already a useful early warning system. The retailers that downsize are not becoming less dependent on their ecosystem. They are becoming more dependent on it. That turns every contractor, vendor, and local service provider into a potential borrower with a cash flow gap.

Funders that move quickly can underwrite a new vertical: the small-format retail supply chain. The demand for working capital is there. The real risk is not in the stores. It is in the payment terms, concentration levels, and inventory velocity of the small businesses that serve them.

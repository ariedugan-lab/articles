## The Tap-to-Pay Fraud Trap: Why Small Business Lenders Must Underwrite for Chargeback Risk in Contactless Payment Environments

Chinese organized crime rings are making up to $1 billion annually from tap-to-pay fraud schemes targeting retailers and banks. That is not a headline for law enforcement alone. For small business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks, it is a direct signal that a new underwriting risk has matured under the radar.

Tap-to-pay fraud exploits the very convenience that drives merchant revenue. Fraudsters use stolen card data to make high-velocity, low-value purchases at contactless terminals. When the legitimate cardholder disputes the charges, the merchant absorbs the chargeback. Repeated chargebacks trigger account freezes, reserve holds, and termination by payment processors. The cash flow impact hits small businesses immediately. The hit to creditworthiness hits lenders later.

Most small business lending models treat revenue as a smooth input. They assume that a merchant’s processing volume reflects genuine customer demand. Tap-to-pay fraud breaks that assumption. A bakery with $100,000 in monthly card volume could see $20,000 of that wiped out by chargebacks in a single fraud wave. The lender who funded against that volume now holds a note secured by air.

### How the Fraud Rings Operate

The schemes documented by CNBC are not random. They are systematic operations using counterfeit cards, NFC relay devices, and stolen card-not-present credentials converted to contactless tokens. Rings target small merchants with high foot traffic and low fraud detection budgets: convenience stores, fast-casual restaurants, bodegas, food trucks, small retail shops. Merchants who accept tap-to-pay with minimal authentication — under $100 thresholds, no PIN — are prime targets.

Fraudsters make purchases in rapid succession across multiple locations. By the time the merchant processes the batch and the first chargeback arrives, the ring has already moved on. The merchant is left with fees, lost inventory, and a damaged processing record.

### The Underwriting Blind Spot

Standard underwriting for MCAs, revenue-based financing, and short-term loans relies on bank statements, processing volume, and time in business. Chargeback ratios are rarely reviewed unless the merchant is already at risk of processor termination. Even when lenders pull processing data, they look at total volume and average ticket size. They do not segment revenue by chargeback exposure.

This matters because tap-to-pay fraud is not evenly distributed. Merchants in dense urban areas, near transit hubs, or with high tourist traffic face elevated risk. Restaurants with high dinner-hour contactless volume are more vulnerable than B2B service providers. A lender who treats all processing volume as equal is underwriting blind.

Consider a concrete scenario. A coffee shop chain applies for a $50,000 MCA. Its processor reports $60,000 in monthly card volume. Standard debt-service coverage looks adequate. But the lender fails to notice that 15% of June’s volume came from tap-to-pay transactions under $25 — the sweet spot for fraud rings. By the time the chargebacks hit, the coffee shop’s cash account is drawn down to cover refunds and processor penalties. The MCA repayment stalls. The funder is left chasing a merchant whose revenue quality collapsed overnight.

### Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISO Networks

**For funders:** The primary risk is adverse selection. Funders who ignore chargeback exposure will attract merchants who have been terminated by processors or flagged by acquiring banks. Those merchants need cash urgently because their processing revenue is disappearing. Lending to them is funding a wind-down. Funders must add chargeback ratio to their credit box, not as a secondary flag but as a primary filter. Any merchant with a chargeback-to-volume ratio above 1.5% should require manual review and a clear explanation. Above 3% should be an auto-decline unless the merchant has a documented remediation plan from its processor.

**For brokers:** Your reputation depends on deal quality. A funder who takes a loss on a fraud-ridden merchant will remember the broker who submitted it. Brokers need to ask merchants about chargeback history, processor warnings, and recent reserve holds. This is a due diligence conversation that separates trusted brokers from volume chasers. If a merchant reports a surge in contactless payments without a corresponding increase in foot traffic, that is a red flag. Brokers who proactively flag these merchants retain funder trust.

**For ISO networks:** Your portfolio-level risk is tied to the aggregate chargeback exposure of your merchants. ISO networks that monitor merchant processing data across their partner base can identify geographic or vertical concentration of fraud risk. If 20% of your merchants are quick-service restaurants in a city where fraud rings are active, you are overexposed. ISO networks should negotiate with payment facilitators for real-time chargeback data feeds. That data is now as important as bank statement analysis.

### Adjusting Your Underwriting Framework

Lenders can take five concrete actions:

1. **Pull processor statements with chargeback line items.** Many processors provide monthly reports with chargeback counts and dollar amounts. Require these in every funding package. Do not rely on bank statements alone.

2. **Segment revenue by payment method.** Ask merchants for a breakdown of contactless vs. chip vs. swiped transactions. A rapid shift toward contactless without a business reason (e.g., new terminal deployment) is a warning sign.

3. **Use transaction velocity alerts.** Monitor for spikes in average transaction frequency. If a merchant’s daily transaction count jumps 30% without a promotion or holiday, investigate.

4. **Build a chargeback decay curve.** Model how chargebacks erode usable revenue over a 60- to 90-day window. Use that adjusted number for debt-service calculations.

5. **Collaborate with payment processors.** Some processors offer risk-scoring APIs that flag merchants with elevated fraud exposure. Integrate those scores into your underwriting decision engine.

### A New Risk Signal for the Industry

The $1 billion tap-to-pay fraud figure is a floor, not a ceiling. As contactless adoption grows and fraud rings refine their methods, the chargeback burden on small merchants will increase. Lenders who ignore this signal will see their loss rates rise silently — buried in “unexpected” defaults that trace back to fraud events they never measured.

Funders, brokers, and ISO networks have an opportunity to differentiate. By incorporating chargeback risk into underwriting, you not only protect your portfolio but also become the lender of choice for merchants who manage their payment hygiene carefully. Those merchants are the high-quality borrowers everyone wants.

Tap-to-pay fraud is a liability that now sits on your side of the balance sheet. Treat it as one.

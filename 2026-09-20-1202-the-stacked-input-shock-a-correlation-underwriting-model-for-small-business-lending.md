# The Stacked Input Shock: A Correlation Underwriting Model for Small Business Lending

The market keeps treating tariffs, fuel prices, and interest rates as separate stories. They are not. For many small businesses, they land on the same profit and loss statement in the same quarter. A manufacturer pays more for imported components, more to ship them, and more to finance the inventory. An auto supplier absorbs tariffed parts, diesel surcharges, and a higher floorplan rate. A retailer sees landed cost rise, delivery cost rise, and the cost of its working capital line reset higher.

Most small business lending models still score these as isolated sector factors. That creates a blind spot. The borrower does not experience three separate shocks. The borrower experiences one stacked input shock. Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that underwrite the stack will price risk better and avoid adverse selection.

## The mistake: one variable at a time

A conventional underwriting file might flag a retailer as tariff-exposed. It might flag a trucking company as fuel-exposed. It might flag a manufacturer as rate-sensitive. Each flag triggers a modest adjustment. The deal moves forward.

The problem is correlation. When tariffs, fuel, and rates rise together, the adjustments compound. Gross margin compresses. Working capital needs expand. Debt service coverage falls. The borrower's ability to pass through cost increases becomes the deciding credit factor, not the original risk flag.

Small business lenders need a correlation lens because small borrowers lack the hedges that large companies use. They do not run fuel hedges. They do not have treasury teams renegotiating tariff clauses. They do not lock SOFR swaps. They absorb the shock or pass it to customers. Many cannot pass it quickly.

## The stacked input shock score

Build a simple score into pre-qualification and underwriting. Call it the stacked input shock score. It does not replace cash flow underwriting. It forces the file to show how three inputs interact.

Score three exposures.

Tariff exposure. Look at imported cost of goods sold as a percentage of total COGS. Review supplier concentration. Check whether customer contracts allow pass-through. Check inventory turns. A borrower with 40 percent imported COGS, four turns, and no price adjustment clause is not the same as a domestic retailer with 10 percent imported COGS and monthly repricing.

Fuel exposure. Measure fuel and freight as a percentage of revenue. For transportation businesses, look at route density and empty miles. For retailers, look at inbound freight and last-mile delivery. For restaurants, look at delivery fees and distribution surcharges. A fuel surcharge program with weekly resets reduces risk. A fixed-price delivery contract increases it.

Rate exposure. Calculate variable-rate debt as a percentage of total liabilities. Check debt service coverage under a 200 basis point increase. For merchant cash advance borrowers, check stacking and renewal frequency. MCA factor rates do not reset like a credit line, but the funder's cost of capital does. That cost shows up in renewal terms, holdback pressure, and default risk when the borrower cannot absorb a lower advance.

Then apply a correlation multiplier. If one exposure is high, adjust. If two or more are high, cut advance rates, shorten maturities, increase reserves, or decline. The exact calibration depends on portfolio data. The discipline is what matters.

## What funders must change

Funders should stop treating tariff risk, fuel risk, and rate risk as separate line items in a credit memo. Combine them into a stress case. Run a simultaneous 10 percent tariff increase, 10 percent fuel increase, and 200 basis point rate increase. Model the impact on monthly cash flow. Then ask how many months the borrower can cover fixed charges before pass-through or cost cuts take effect.

Use bank data to verify the story. Look for rising inventory purchases, higher freight payments, increasing interest expense, and slower customer payments. A tariff shock often shows up first as inventory build. A fuel shock shows up as higher card and ACH outflows. A rate shock shows up as higher debt service or lower advances on renewals.

Reprice for correlation. A borrower with one high exposure may earn standard pricing. A borrower with two or three high exposures needs a different structure. Consider shorter terms, weekly remittances, tighter covenants, and collateral monitoring. If the borrower sells to customers with strong credit, consider receivables financing with verified invoices. If the borrower holds inventory, consider inventory financing with conservative advance rates and a clear liquidation path.

For merchant cash advance funders, watch renewal behavior. A merchant that renews at a higher factor rate or takes a second position is not growing. It is plugging a working capital gap created by stacked inputs. That is a default signal, not a loyalty signal.

## What brokers and ISO networks must do

Brokers and ISO networks sit closest to the borrower. They can collect the three data points that matter: import exposure, fuel exposure, and rate exposure. Add them to the application. Ask for the last three months of freight invoices, a supplier list with country of origin, and the current debt schedule with rates and maturities.

Do not push maximum approval on a stacked-risk file. A larger advance may solve this month's cash flow and destroy next quarter's. Position fixed-rate term debt for rate-sensitive borrowers. Position working capital lines for tariff-exposed retailers that can reprice quickly. Position equipment financing for fuel-efficient upgrades when the savings are measurable.

ISO networks should build vertical playbooks. Auto suppliers need contract-level pass-through analysis. Restaurants need delivery and distribution cost review. Retailers need landed cost and inventory turn analysis. Transportation businesses need fuel surcharge and route density analysis. The playbook does not need to be complex. It needs to route the deal to the right funder with the right structure.

## The operator playbook

1. Add a stacked input shock score to intake. Score tariff, fuel, and rate exposure on a 1 to 5 scale. Flag any file with two exposures at 4 or higher.

2. Require a pass-through memo. Document contract terms, repricing cadence, customer concentration, and competitor price pressure. If the borrower cannot pass through, underwrite the full shock.

3. Stress test simultaneously. Do not model tariffs alone. Do not model fuel alone. Do not model rates alone. Model the combined hit to gross margin, cash conversion cycle, and debt service coverage.

4. Adjust structure, not just price. Correlation risk is a structure problem. Shorter maturities, smaller advances, weekly collections, and tighter monitoring reduce loss given default.

5. Monitor portfolio clusters. Group borrowers by import intensity, fuel intensity, and rate sensitivity. If a cluster shows rising delinquency or slower renewals, tighten appetite before losses appear.

The source headline is a warning. Tariffs, fuel, and rates are squeezing American companies. For small business lenders, the opportunity is not to avoid every affected borrower. The opportunity is to separate borrowers that can pass through the shock from those that cannot. That separation starts with underwriting the stack, not the headline.

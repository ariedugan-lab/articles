The hard seltzer boom is over. Non-carbonated ready-to-drink (RTD) cocktails—brands like Surfside and BeatBox—are capturing share of throat at a pace that caught distributors, retailers, and bar owners off guard. For small business lenders, this is not a consumer trend story. It is a cash-flow event with direct implications for portfolio risk.

Gen Z is driving the shift. Nielsen data shows RTD cocktail volume grew 32% year-over-year while hard seltzer declined 18% in the same period. Bars and liquor stores that loaded up on seltzer inventory during the prior wave now face write-downs, stale stock, and margin pressure. The businesses that win are those that pivot quickly—but pivoting requires capital and a tolerance for inventory churn that most small operators lack.

This creates a new underwriting signal: trend lifecycle risk. Standard cash-flow models look backward. They measure historical revenue, EBITDA, and debt service. They do not measure whether a business’s product mix is about to become obsolete. Lenders who ignore that blind spot will fund the next default before the trend fully hits.

## Why the shift matters for funders

If you fund beverage distributors, liquor stores, or bars, the seltzer-to-RTD transition is a leading indicator of working capital stress. Consider a distributor that has 60 days of hard seltzer on hand and faces a 20% drop in reorder rates. That inventory becomes a cash trap. The business needs to discount to move it, then rebuy RTD cocktails at higher per-unit cost. The result: a temporary spike in days payable outstanding and a squeeze on current ratios.

Funders should adjust advance rates for businesses with heavy exposure to declining categories. Require a covenant on inventory turnover by product family—not just total inventory. If seltzer turnover drops below a threshold, trigger a review. This is not punitive; it is protective. A distributor that can transition inventory quickly will survive. One that cannot will burn cash.

The same logic applies to bars and restaurants. Many added seltzer taps and can coolers during the peak. Now they need to retool. The cost of swapping out a tap system or sourcing new glassware is small, but the disruption to working capital is real. A bar that loses 15% of seltzer sales overnight and replaces them with lower-margin cocktails may see a 200-basis-point EBITDA drop. Your underwriting model should account for that.

## What brokers should tell clients

Brokers who serve the beverage and hospitality space have an opportunity to position themselves as trend advisors—not just capital brokers. When you speak to a client who distributes seltzer, ask two questions:

1. What percentage of your revenue comes from carbonated vs. non-carbonated products?
2. Are you actively shifting shelf space or marketing spend toward RTD cocktails?

If the answer to the second is no, warn them. Then offer a solution: a working capital line to fund the inventory transition. The capital is not for growth—it is for survival. The client needs to buy new product, discount old product, and manage a temporary dip in margins. A short-term bridge loan with flexible repayment terms can preserve their credit profile.

Brokers should also look for businesses that are already ahead of the curve. A distributor that saw the trend early and shifted 40% of shelf space to RTD cocktails is a lower-risk borrower. Their inventory turnover is higher, their margin mix is improving, and they are less exposed to the seltzer cliff. Those are the accounts to prioritize for funder matchmaking.

## ISO networks: Lead generation angle

This trend creates a clear lead generation opportunity. Run a targeted campaign for beverage distributors, liquor stores, and bar owners in regions with high Gen Z density—college towns, urban centers, coastal markets. The message: “Is your seltzer inventory turning into a liability? We offer capital for inventory repositioning.”

Use data points to personalize outreach. If you have access to POS or SKU-level data, identify businesses where seltzer sales declined more than 10% quarter-over-quarter. Those are the businesses most likely to need capital. If you do not have that data, use public signals: social media mentions of seltzer discounting, local news about store closures, or even foot traffic trends from location data providers.

The conversion rate on this type of lead can be higher than generic “working capital” offers because the need is specific and time-sensitive. The business owner knows the seltzer problem exists. Your offer provides a concrete solution.

## How to underwrite trend lifecycle risk

Traditional lending models treat all revenue as equal. That is the flaw. Revenue from a declining product category is lower-quality revenue. It will not persist. To underwrite for trend lifecycle risk, add two steps to your process:

1. **Category concentration analysis.** Compute what percentage of a borrower’s revenue or cost of goods sold comes from categories with negative trend momentum. Use industry reports, Google Trends, or point-of-sale data to assign a momentum score: positive, neutral, or negative. If negative categories exceed 30% of revenue, reduce your advance rate or require a personal guarantee.

2. **Inventory velocity segmentation.** Do not rely on aggregate turnover. Split inventory into three buckets: growing categories, stable categories, and declining categories. Measure turnover for each. If declining-category turnover drops below the borrower’s cost of capital-adjusted threshold, flag the account for review.

These steps are not complex. They require accessing data that is already available from the borrower’s accounting system or distributor portal. Most lenders do not ask because they do not think about product lifecycle. That is the competitive gap.

## The bottom line for small business lending operators

Gen Z’s move from seltzer to RTD cocktails is one data point in a broader pattern. Consumer tastes shift faster than small businesses can adjust. Lenders who build trend lifecycle risk into their underwriting will originate higher-quality loans, spot trouble early, and capture market share from competitors who rely on backward-looking models.

The next trend—whatever it is—will hit. Be ready to underwrite it.

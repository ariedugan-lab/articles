# The GLP-1 Prescriber Concentration Trap: Novo-Lilly Share Shifts Reprice Small Business Lending Risk

Novo's effort to reset its GLP-1 franchise while Eli Lilly gains ground is not a pharma story for lenders to watch from the sidelines. It is a working capital signal for any small business that prescribes, dispenses, administers, or finances obesity drugs. The credit question is no longer whether GLP-1 demand exists. It is whether a borrower's cash flow survives a product mix shift.

Most GLP-1 lending commentary focuses on demand, reimbursement, or advertising. That misses the operating risk. The real exposure is prescriber concentration. When Novo and Lilly trade share, small businesses do not adjust instantly. They carry inventory, retrain staff, rewrite protocols, and wait for reimbursements. Each step consumes cash before revenue catches up. Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that underwrite the category as one demand signal will misprice risk.

## Why market share is a working capital event

A share shift changes three cash-flow variables at once. First, inventory. Pharmacies and DME suppliers buy in batches. If a wholesaler or rebate agreement pushes them toward one manufacturer, a later shift leaves slow-moving SKUs. Second, receivables. PBM reimbursements and patient financing payments already have long tails. A product switch can trigger prior authorization resets, denials, and patient churn. Third, acquisition cost. Clinics and telehealth platforms must spend to retain patients who switch products or lose coverage.

For small business lending, those variables matter more than the headline obesity market size. A borrower can operate in a growing category and still miss payroll because its revenue depends on one prescriber, one payer, or one manufacturer's supply.

## The credit blind spot: prescriber and product concentration

Existing underwriting often asks about revenue concentration by customer. GLP-1 adjacent businesses need a second layer. Ask how much revenue depends on one prescriber, one telehealth partner, one pharmacy network, or one drug. A clinic with 60% of scripts from a single physician is not diversified. A digital pharmacy with 70% of fills from one referral source is not diversified. A supplier whose inventory is weighted to one manufacturer's pens is not diversified.

When Novo works to turn itself around and Lilly gains ground, those concentrations become volatility. Lenders should treat them as leading indicators, not historical trivia.

## Segment implications

### Independent pharmacies and DME suppliers

Inventory financing is the first exposure. Pharmacies may overstock a product based on rebates, wholesaler incentives, or expected demand. If Lilly gains share or Novo changes commercial terms, the borrower can face excess inventory, expiry risk, and reduced reimbursement. A business line of credit sized on peak inventory can become over-advanced. A merchant cash advance with a daily holdback can mismatch PBM reimbursement cycles. Funders should monitor inventory composition, days on hand, return rights, and wholesaler terms.

### Weight-loss clinics and medspas

These businesses depend on protocol consistency and patient retention. A product switch forces new clinical workflows, prior authorization support, and marketing. Cash-pay patients may pause treatment. Insurance patients may face coverage changes. Patient financing receivables can age faster than expected. Underwrite by payer mix, treatment completion rates, and prescriber concentration. Do not rely on consult volume alone.

### Telehealth and digital pharmacy platforms

Subscription and refill models look stable until supply or formulary changes. A platform that acquired patients around one drug's availability can see churn when patients switch. Funders should test 90-day retention, refill rates, and concentration by prescriber and product. Revenue-based financing needs a lower advance rate when retention is unproven. A high growth rate can hide a fragile cohort.

### Marketing and fulfillment vendors

Agencies serving GLP-1 clinics face campaign cancellation risk. Fulfillment vendors face order volatility. These borrowers are one step removed from the drug, but they are not insulated. Lenders should map contract length, client concentration, and recurring revenue before approving working capital.

## Underwriting checklist for funders

Add these questions to the application and renewal file. Top three prescribers as a percentage of scripts or revenue. Product mix across Novo, Lilly, compounded alternatives, and other categories. Payer mix across commercial, Medicare, cash-pay, and discount cards. Inventory turns and shelf life. PBM aging and denial rates. Patient financing aging. Wholesaler terms and rebate contracts. Prescriber contract terms and non-compete exposure.

Use bank data to detect wholesaler payments and PBM deposits. A sudden change in either can signal a mix shift before financial statements show it. Stress test a 20% product shift and a 10% reimbursement cut. If debt service coverage falls below policy, adjust the structure.

## Implications for brokers and ISO networks

Brokers should not sell generic growth capital to a borrower with heavy GLP-1 concentration. Package the request with script data, payer contracts, and inventory reports. Position the loan as inventory pivot capital or receivables bridge. ISO networks should train representatives to ask product and prescriber questions during intake. Better submissions reduce funder friction and improve close rates.

For funders, the pricing response is structural. Shorten terms where product concentration is high. Set borrowing base exclusions for slow-moving GLP-1 inventory. Require monthly script reporting. Use tighter holdbacks on MCA deals with patient financing exposure. For business lines of credit, monitor concentration covenants at renewal. For term loans, require a prescriber diversification plan if one relationship exceeds a threshold.

## The signal to watch

Novo's next chapter and Lilly's gains are not a single event. They are a cadence. Every formulary change, supply update, and direct-to-consumer campaign shifts cash through small businesses. Lenders that track prescriber and product concentration will price risk earlier. Those that treat GLP-1 as one demand curve will fund the wrong borrowers at the wrong advance rates.

The operator takeaway is simple. Underwrite the borrower's dependence, not the category's growth. In small business lending, concentration is the risk that survives a good headline.

General Motors and Ford have taken their century-old rivalry into new terrain. Both automakers are pursuing military contracts and energy storage opportunities, according to CNBC. For a small business lender, this is not another story about the auto industry. It is a warning about how customer concentration gets measured.

A concentration problem is not always visible on an accounts receivable aging report.

If a supplier borrower lists GM and Ford as customers, the typical funding file reads that as diversification. Two large, recognized counterparties. Spread across two corporate giants. That view rests on a false assumption: that different buyer names mean independent revenue streams.

GM and Ford are now chasing the same U.S. defense market and the same energy storage market. Their strategic outcomes are tied to shared demand drivers. A supplier that sells to both OEMs can still be concentrated in one end customer category, one federal procurement budget, or one energy policy cycle.

The invoices look different. The cash-flow source may be the same.

## The Old Concentration Test No Longer Holds

Most small business underwriting standards classify concentration by revenue share. If one customer accounts for more than 30% of revenue, the file gets flagged. If two customers each account for 20%, the file gets treated as balanced.

That framework works when buyers compete in independent end markets. It breaks down when two large buyers sell into the same programs.

Take a small metal fabricator that does 45% of its revenue with GM and 35% with Ford. On paper, the exposure is split. But if both automakers are building defense vehicles for the same U.S. military procurement cycle, the fabricator may actually have 80% of its revenue tied to defense spending.

The names are different. The underlying appropriation is not.

The same logic applies to energy storage. If a supplier ships components to both GM and Ford energy projects, its revenue depends on utility-scale storage deployment. Tariff policy, interconnection rules, and tax credit changes hit both buyers at the same time.

Multiple customer names do not create diversification when those customers share one demand curve.

## Build a Customer-Demand Map

Lenders should move beyond the customer list and build a customer-demand map. For every material customer, identify the actual end market, the distribution chain, and the demand driver.

A GM invoice could mean consumer vehicle production, defense vehicle production, or energy storage hardware. Those are different risk classes.

A Ford invoice could mean internal combustion vehicle parts, electric vehicle components, or grid storage modules. Those are also different risk classes.

The invoice header alone does not tell you which risk you are financing.

Ask the borrower to segment revenue by what the end product does and who ultimately pays for it. The borrower often knows whether it is stamping a part for a commercial pickup or an armored military vehicle. Use that knowledge in the underwriting file.

For each material customer segment, document the following:

- Customer name and contract type
- End product and end user
- Government program, utility project, or commercial program attached to the work
- Program expiration or renewal date
- Whether revenue recurs monthly or is tied to a project milestone

This mapping turns a simple customer concentration ratio into an end-market concentration analysis.

## Apply a Correlation Overlay

Once the customer-demand map is complete, apply a correlation overlay. If two large customers share the same end-market driver, treat their combined revenue as a single concentration for stress testing.

For example, if a borrower has 50% of its revenue going to GM and 25% going to Ford, but all of it supports defense platform work, combined defense-related revenue is 75%. That is not diversified. That is high concentration.

Run a category-level stress test. What happens to the borrower if U.S. defense procurement is cut by 20%? What happens if energy storage deployment slows because of a tariff change or grid interconnection backlog?

Because GM and Ford are entering the same new sectors, their suppliers should not be underwritten on the assumption that one OEM’s loss is the other OEM’s gain. In defense and energy, both may decline together.

The correlation overlay changes eligibility, advance rates, and loan structure. A diversified-looking book with a hidden shared dependency should not qualify for the same working capital line as a borrower with genuinely independent end markets.

## What This Means for Funders

Funders should stop treating a second large corporate customer as automatic risk reduction. Review the customer-demand map before approving the deal.

For asset-based and invoice-based facilities, separate receivables by end-market category. Apply concentration limits at the category level, not just the customer level. If combined correlated revenue exceeds a reasonable threshold, reduce the advance rate or require a guarantor cushion.

For revenue-based financing and merchant cash advances, examine deposit patterns relative to program timing. Large deposits from GM or Ford can look stable when viewed on a monthly statement, but they may come from a milestone-based defense or energy program. If the program is delayed, the repayment capacity disappears. A fixed daily ACH based on average revenue becomes a collection risk.

Funders that rely on bank statements should ask whether the largest deposits are recurring production payments or one-off milestone payments. That distinction matters more when both customers are pivoting into the same program-heavy sectors.

## What This Means for Brokers

Brokers need to sharpen their discovery process. When a borrower says it sells to both GM and Ford, the next question should be: What are they buying from you, and where does it end up?

Ask whether the customer work is commercial auto production or new defense and energy programs. Ask whether the GM or Ford contracts are tied to a single government project. Ask which programs expire in the next twelve months.

These questions determine the right financing product. A borrower with recurring auto production volume can support a traditional working capital facility. A borrower with defense or energy program revenue needs a structure that can handle milestone timing, contract assets, and non-repeating cash flow.

Brokers who identify the correlation early can direct the file to a lender with the right risk appetite. That protects the deal and the borrower relationship.

## What This Means for ISO Networks

ISO networks should train agents on correlated customer risk. Too many files are sold as diversified simply because the borrower has multiple invoice headers.

Sales agents need a simple rule: multiple buyers in the same end market are not diversification. If the buyers all depend on the same federal budget, the same utility buildout, or the same regulatory calendar, the borrower carries a concentrated exposure.

Networks should embed this into the origination conversation. A short checklist works:

- Which end markets does this borrower serve?
- Do the largest customers depend on the same demand driver?
- Is any of this revenue tied to a non-recurring program?

That checklist keeps reps from presenting hidden concentration as strength.

The GM and Ford rivalry is now a shared expansion into defense and energy. The two automakers still compete, but they are competing for the same contracts, the same program dollars, and the same policy tailwinds.

For small business lenders, the risk is not the rivalry. It is the correlation. A supplier that sells to both GM and Ford may look protected. The credit file should prove that the end markets are actually independent before the lender prices it that way.

Name recognition is not credit quality. Concentration analysis must go beyond logos and measure the shared demand beneath them.

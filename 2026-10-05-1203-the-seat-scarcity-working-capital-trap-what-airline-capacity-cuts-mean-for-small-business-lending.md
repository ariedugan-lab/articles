# The Seat-Scarcity Working Capital Trap: What Airline Capacity Cuts Mean for Small Business Lending

Airlines are trimming capacity while jet fuel prices climb. Travelers keep booking. Fares rise. Profits do not.

That headline looks like an airline story. For funders, brokers, and ISO networks, it is a small business lending signal. The industry is trading volume for price. Fewer seats, higher fares, tighter schedules. That shift changes the cash-flow profile of every small business that depends on passenger traffic or travel spend.

Most lenders still underwrite these borrowers on gross revenue. That approach breaks when unit volume falls and price per unit rises. A merchant can show flat or higher top-line deposits while its operating cash flow deteriorates. The airline capacity cut is the leading indicator.

## Fewer seats, higher fares, weaker volume

Capacity discipline means airlines are not adding flights to meet demand. They are protecting fares. Fuel costs are eating the upside. The result is a market with higher ticket prices and fewer available seats.

For a small business, that creates two competing forces:

- Revenue per customer can rise. Premium cabins, ancillary fees, and higher fares lift spend per traveler.
- Customer count can fall. Fewer seats mean fewer passengers moving through airports, hotels, parking lots, and ground transportation.

If the business sells a flat-rate service, volume loss hits directly. If it sells a percentage-based service, higher fares can mask volume loss. That is the trap.

A travel agency can report higher gross bookings because tickets cost more. But its commission per ticket may not rise at the same rate. An airport parking operator can report higher revenue per car if rates rise, but fewer cars mean the fixed cost base gets harder to cover. An off-airport hotel can fill rooms with fewer business travelers but higher leisure rates, then miss its commercial demand forecast.

Underwriting on gross revenue misses the difference between pass-through and operating cash.

## Trap 1: Gross bookings are not net revenue

Travel agencies, corporate travel managers, and online booking resellers generate revenue on gross bookings. When airfare rises, gross bookings rise. Many bank statements look stronger. But the agency may only keep a flat transaction fee or a small commission.

Funders must separate:

- Gross client payments
- Supplier payables
- Net commission and fee income
- Refund and chargeback reserves

If a borrower presents a 20 percent increase in deposits driven by higher airfare, do not treat that as 20 percent more repayment capacity. Ask for net revenue reports. Recalculate debt service coverage on net commission, not gross bookings. For merchant cash advance, use the true operating account deposits after supplier sweep-outs.

Brokers should request booking engine reports, supplier payment terms, and cancellation rates. ISO networks should train reps to spot pass-through deposits. A travel agency with high gross volume and thin net margin is not a strong MCA candidate just because the bank account is busy.

## Trap 2: Prepaid travel is a liability, not working capital

Higher fares push travelers to book earlier. That creates prepaid deposits. For a small business, those deposits look like cash. They are not.

A prepaid booking creates a future obligation. The business must deliver the trip or refund the customer. When airlines cut capacity, schedule changes and cancellations rise. Refunds hit the same account that looked strong in underwriting.

Lenders should treat prepaid travel balances as restricted or liability-backed cash. Do not include them in free cash flow. For working capital loans, subtract refund reserves and supplier deposits from available liquidity. For MCA, exclude months with large prepaid spikes from baseline revenue unless the borrower can show consistent conversion.

This matters for destination tour operators, event planners, and niche travel agencies. A 10 percent cancellation rate on a 30 percent deposit base can wipe out the working capital cushion that supported the advance.

## Trap 3: Volume-dependent fixed costs do not reprice

Airport-adjacent small businesses carry fixed costs: leases, shuttles, parking lots, insurance, and labor. When passenger volume drops, those costs remain. Higher fares do not automatically flow to the parking operator, the hotel shuttle, or the airport restaurant.

Underwrite these borrowers on transaction count, not just ticket size. Check:

- Passenger enplanements at the relevant airport
- Airline route cuts and frequency changes
- Hotel occupancy by segment, not just average daily rate
- Parking and ground transportation trip counts
- Concession sales per passenger versus total passengers

A restaurant near a hub airport may see higher spend per traveler but fewer business travelers. If its model depends on weekday lunch volume, a capacity cut can hurt even when average check rises. A black car service may see higher fares from surge pricing, but fewer trips mean lower driver utilization and weaker loan repayment.

## What funders should do now

Segment your travel-adjacent portfolio. Separate volume-driven businesses from price-driven businesses. Then re-underwrite limits.

Use a simple stress test:

- 10 percent capacity cut
- 15 percent fare increase
- 5 percent cancellation rate
- 30-day delay in supplier refunds

Calculate net operating cash flow after that stress. If the borrower still covers debt service, the risk is manageable. If not, reduce exposure or require more frequent remittances.

Review bank statements for pass-through deposits. Large deposits followed by supplier payments are not revenue. Use average net operating deposits over six to twelve months. Exclude prepaid travel balances and refund reserves.

For portfolios with airport-adjacent exposure, monitor airline schedule changes. A route cut is a leading indicator of lower foot traffic. It precedes a revenue decline by 30 to 90 days. Funders can tighten renewals before defaults appear.

## Broker and ISO playbook

Brokers should ask three questions before submitting a travel-dependent file:

1. What percentage of gross revenue is pass-through to suppliers?
2. What is the cancellation and refund rate over the last 12 months?
3. How many transactions, not just dollars, does the business process per month?

If the broker cannot answer, the file is not ready. Funders will price the uncertainty or decline.

ISO networks should build a travel-adjacent checklist. Include booking reports, supplier aging, refund reserve balances, and airport traffic data. Train reps to avoid stacking offers on borrowers with high prepaid liabilities. Stacking increases default risk when refunds hit. It also creates compliance exposure if the borrower uses one advance to cover another.

For MCA renewals, look at remittance consistency. A travel agency with seasonal prepaid spikes may show strong months followed by refund months. Use a trailing 12-month average, not the peak season. For business loans, require monthly financials if the borrower has material supplier payables.

## The operator takeaway

The airline capacity cut is not a reason to avoid travel-adjacent small business lending. It is a reason to underwrite it correctly. Higher airfares can inflate gross revenue while reducing net cash flow. Funders that separate pass-through revenue from operating revenue will see the risk earlier. Brokers that document net commission and transaction volume will place more files. ISO networks that train on refund liability will reduce early defaults.

The signal is simple: when an industry trades volume for price, stop underwriting on volume. Start underwriting on the cash the borrower actually keeps.

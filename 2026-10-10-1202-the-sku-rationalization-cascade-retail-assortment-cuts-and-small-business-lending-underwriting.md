## Retail assortment cuts are a working capital signal
Retailers from BJ's to Lululemon are trimming assortments. The merchandising story is simple: fewer SKUs, faster turns, less markdown risk. For small business lenders, the signal is not merchandising. It is a working capital event that hits suppliers before it shows up in bank statements.

When a large retailer culls products, it does not cut revenue evenly. It cuts specific vendors, specific SKUs, and specific purchase orders. A small supplier can lose 20 to 40 percent of its retail revenue in one reset while its trailing financials still look stable. That gap between reported revenue and future cash flow is where underwriting mistakes happen.

Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that treat this as a retail headline will miss the credit risk. Operators that map SKU concentration, reset calendars, and retailer deductions will price the risk correctly.

## The cascade runs from shelf to supplier
Large retailers use assortment cuts to stabilize business. They reduce duplicate SKUs, improve inventory turns, and negotiate better terms with remaining vendors. That is rational for the retailer. It is brutal for small suppliers that depended on broad distribution.

The first hit is purchase order volume. A supplier may keep a listing but see unit orders fall. The second hit is chargebacks and markdown allowances. Retailers push unsold inventory risk back to vendors. The third hit is payment timing. Deductions and disputes extend the cash conversion cycle. By the time the supplier's bank statement shows the damage, the lender is already exposed.

This is not a single-sector issue. Apparel, home goods, consumer electronics, beauty, and packaged food all face SKU rationalization. BJ's and Lululemon are examples, not exceptions. The same playbook appears in mass retail, grocery, club, and specialty channels.

## Underwrite SKU concentration, not just customer concentration
Most small business lending models ask about customer concentration. That is no longer enough. A supplier can have ten retailers and still face existential risk if one retailer drives 60 percent of revenue and that retailer is cutting the category.

Funders should add SKU-level concentration to underwriting. Ask:
- What percentage of revenue comes from the top five SKUs?
- Which SKUs are tied to a single retailer?
- Has the retailer issued a reset, category review, or vendor scorecard in the last two quarters?
- What is the chargeback trend by retailer?
- How much raw material or packaging is dedicated to at-risk SKUs?

The answers change advance rates. Inventory that cannot be redirected to another buyer is not good collateral. A purchase order from a retailer that is cutting the category is not the same as a purchase order from a retailer expanding it. Loan brokers and ISO networks should capture this data at application. It improves placement with funders that understand retail supply chains.

## Inventory and receivables risk shifts before revenue does
SKU cuts strand inventory. A supplier may hold finished goods, components, and packaging for products that no longer have shelf space. That inventory may look valuable on a balance sheet. In practice, it is often illiquid. Lenders should exclude retailer-specific inventory from borrowing bases or apply a deep advance-rate discount.

Receivables risk also changes. Retailers may pay slower, dispute more, or take deductions. A clean accounts receivable aging can hide this if the supplier is still shipping against old POs. Funders should monitor dilution, chargebacks, and deduction aging. Factoring and receivables financing programs need retailer-specific concentration limits.

For merchant cash advance and short-term working capital products, the risk is timing. Daily or weekly remittances can mask a revenue cliff for weeks. When the reset lands, the supplier's bank account drains, and the MCA becomes a collections problem. ISO networks should flag merchants with recent retail vendor changes before stacking new advances.

## The barbell: winners need growth capital, losers need rescue capital
Assortment cuts create a barbell. Some suppliers lose shelf space and need wind-down or bridge capital. Others gain SKUs as retailers consolidate vendors. Those winners need working capital to scale production, buy inventory, and meet higher fill rates.

Lenders that only see the downside will miss the opportunity. Lenders that only chase growth will fund suppliers that are about to be cut. The key is to separate SKU expansion from SKU contraction. Ask whether the retailer is adding items, expanding distribution, or increasing shelf space. Ask for the vendor scorecard. Ask for EDI 852 or POS data if available.

Brokers and ISO networks can build a retail supply chain niche. Position the loan as a working capital diagnostic, not a commodity. Merchants in this niche value speed, but they also need a funder that understands reset cycles. A broker who asks the right SKU questions will place better files and reduce early defaults.

## Operator playbook for funders, brokers, and ISO networks
Funders:
- Add SKU concentration and retailer reset questions to application and renewal reviews.
- Require retailer-level revenue, chargeback, and deduction reports for retail-dependent borrowers.
- Set concentration limits by retailer and by SKU.
- Use inventory advance rates that reflect resale value outside the original retailer.
- Monitor retailer earnings calls, vendor scorecards, and assortment news as leading indicators.

Brokers and ISO networks:
- Ask merchants about recent SKU cuts, canceled POs, and markdown requests.
- Document which retailers are expanding and which are contracting.
- Match files to funders with retail supply chain expertise and appropriate concentration tolerance.
- Avoid stacking when a merchant has a pending reset or unresolved chargebacks.

The retail assortment story is not about shopping. It is about cash flow. Retailers trim to stabilize. Suppliers absorb the shock. Small business lenders that underwrite the SKU-level cascade will protect capital and find better borrowers. Those that wait for bank statements to show the problem will be late.

TV will look different in three years. For small business lenders, the useful response is not to predict the winner. It is to separate revenue that is dying from revenue that is being rebuilt.

Media insiders expect pay TV subscribers to keep falling, streaming ad tiers to keep growing, and sports rights to hold pricing power. Each shift changes cash flow quality for small businesses in the video supply chain. Lenders that treat media as one vertical will misprice risk. Use two models.

## Linear TV exposure is a decline curve, not a flat line

The most direct credit signal is the pay TV subscriber trajectory. As households cancel cable and satellite packages, retransmission fees shrink, local affiliates lose carriage revenue, and networks cut vendor budgets. The small businesses that feel this first are not production houses. They are the local station equipment vendors, broadcast engineering contractors, set-top box logistics providers, and cable field service subcontractors.

For these borrowers, revenue is not stable; it is on a decline curve. Underwrite that curve. Apply a 10 to 15 percent annual revenue haircut for businesses with more than half of revenue tied to linear TV operations. Stress test accounts receivable days. A network that paid in 45 days now pays in 75. A local affiliate that renewed a maintenance contract in December now delays until March. Advance rates need to fall for receivables from linear TV customers.

Do not rely on contract backlog for these borrowers. A multi-year maintenance deal with a declining broadcaster still has cash flow, but the renewal will be smaller. The right question is not whether the current contract is valid. It is whether the borrower can survive a 30 percent step-down at renewal.

## Streaming ad-tier growth creates a different risk

Streaming is growing, but it is not a stable cash flow machine. Subscription revenue is recurring, yet the ad-tier portion is more volatile. Advertisers commit quarterly. CPMs shift with data privacy rules, measurement changes, and platform competition. Small businesses that sell into streaming ad operations see revenue grow fast and then compress when a platform insources the work or changes its ad stack.

Underwrite streaming-adjacent borrowers on contract quality, not growth rate. Ask how many platforms they serve. If one platform is more than 40 percent of revenue, treat that as concentration. Ask whether the ad inventory is sold through direct insertion orders or programmatic pipes. Direct IOs have clearer payment timing; programmatic revenue relies on platform payout schedules that can extend beyond 60 days. Ask about chargebacks and makegood liability. If a campaign underdelivers, the borrower may owe work, not cash. That creates a hidden labor cost that hits gross margin.

For working capital structures, revenue-based financing fits these borrowers only if the settlement process is clean. If revenue is recognized at campaign start but cash arrives after reconciliation, the borrower needs a line that matches the settlement cycle, not a fixed daily debit.

## Sports rights are an event signal, not a trend signal

Media insiders agree that live sports will remain the anchor of linear TV. That creates a temptation to treat sports-adjacent small businesses as safe. They are not safe; they are event-dependent. Sports bars, event production crews, venue maintenance firms, and licensed merchandise suppliers all benefit from the broadcast calendar. But their cash flow is lumpy, and the lulls are expensive.

Lenders need event-adjusted cash flow. Strip out playoff, final, and season-opening surges. If the base cash flow cannot cover rent and debt service, the borrower needs a seasonal working capital product with a repayment schedule that matches cash flow, not a level-payment term loan. Also watch rights fragmentation. When a sports property moves from broadcast to streaming, the audience shifts and the venue economics change. A bar may still show the event, but the ad dollars follow the broadcaster. Small businesses that sold sponsorship-adjacent services to the old rights holder can lose that recurring piece without losing the event itself.

## Brokers and ISO networks should segment the vertical now

The practical move for brokers is to stop sending media-adjacent borrowers into a generic business loan path. Create three checklists and use them to pre-qualify.

For linear-exposed businesses, collect the list of top customers, the renewal dates, and the share of revenue from pay TV-related services. If the top customer is a legacy broadcaster, discount the revenue.

For streaming-adjacent businesses, ask for platform concentration, contract term, and payment settlement days. If the borrower has no contract renewal in writing, treat the revenue as spot work.

For sports-adjacent businesses, ask for monthly cash flow by month from the last 24 months. Identify the high-event months and the base months. Build a repayment schedule that flexes with the season.

Funders can use this segmentation to set advance rates. A streaming ad supplier with a two-year platform contract and 30-day settlement may support a higher advance than a linear TV vendor with a declining contract book. A sports bar with strong peak months and weak base months does not belong in a vanilla working capital product.

## The three-year takeaway

TV in three years will be smaller in linear, larger in streaming, and still concentrated in sports. The credit implications are not about the industry's survival. They are about which small business cash flows are improving and which are in persistent decline.

Lenders that blend all media revenue into one forecast will underwrite declining assets as if they were growing and fast-growing assets as if they were stable. Both errors create losses. Update the underwriting checklist. Segment by revenue source. Tie repayment to actual cash flow events. Re-underwrite every quarter based on customer concentration and payment behavior.

The TV market will not look like it does today. Your book should not either.

Late Sunday, Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) told NBC's *Meet the Press* he was prepared to advance Kevin Warsh's nomination to chair the Federal Reserve, ending a stalemate that had threatened to drag past Jerome Powell's May 15 term expiration. The Senate Banking Committee scheduled a vote for Wednesday — the same day the Federal Open Market Committee is widely expected to hold its policy rate steady for the third consecutive meeting.

For non-bank lenders — MCA funders, revenue-based financing providers, equipment lessors, factoring shops, and the broker and ISO networks that feed them — the operative question is not whether Warsh gets the job. It is what kind of Fed he intends to run, and how quickly the rate environment changes if he does.

## The setup: a chair Trump expects to cut

Warsh told the Banking Committee at last week's hearing that he had made no promises on rate policy and would act as "an independent actor" if confirmed. Hours earlier, President Trump told CNBC he would be "disappointed" if Warsh did not move quickly to lower rates. Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.), the committee's ranking Democrat, called Warsh "nothing more than President Trump's sock puppet."

Strip the politics out and the structural read is simple: the next chair takes office under intense, public, and repeated pressure to cut. Even modest dovish signaling from a new chair moves long-end yields, repricing every credit product downstream — including the small-business credit that drives MCA, RBF, and commercial origination.

## Why this matters for non-bank business lending

Banks set the headline. Non-bank lenders set the price small businesses actually pay. Three transmission channels matter most:

**1. Cost of capital.** Most MCA and RBF balance sheets are funded by warehouse lines, securitization, family-office credit, or institutional forward-flow. Each one is priced off some flavor of SOFR or a short-Treasury benchmark. A 50–100 bp move in the front end compresses the all-in cost of funding originators carry — which either expands net spread or, more commonly in competitive cycles, gets passed through to merchants as cheaper factor rates.

**2. Borrower demand.** Lower rates do not just improve unit economics for lenders. They reset the demand curve. Small businesses that deferred capex, refinanced revolvers, or paused expansion through 2024–2025 typically re-enter the market within a quarter or two of a clear easing signal. Origination teams that wait for that demand to walk in the door lose the deal to someone who was already on the relationship.

**3. Stack dynamics.** When credit gets cheaper, more capital flows toward the same merchants. Stacking risk goes up. The funders that win the next cycle will not be the ones with the most aggressive pricing — they will be the ones with the cleanest view of who already has paper outstanding, who is broker-shopping, and which broker submissions actually translate into funded, performing deals.

## The independence question is the lending question

Tillis spent most of his interview defending Fed independence — pushing back on what he called a "vindictive prosecution" of Powell over the central bank's $2.5 billion building renovation. The political theater is real. But for lenders, independence is not an abstraction. It is the variable that determines whether rate policy is predictable enough to underwrite against.

A Fed perceived as politically captured forces every lending decision to price in policy reversal risk. That widens spreads and shortens tenors, which constrains the very small businesses the administration argues it wants to help. A Fed perceived as independent — even if it does cut — gives the credit markets a stable curve to work with. That is the environment in which origination scales.

Whether Warsh delivers that perception is the open question. His 2026 testimony is being read against his 2008–2011 tenure as a Fed governor, when he was a vocal hawk skeptical of quantitative easing. The market consensus is that a Warsh Fed cuts, but cuts deliberately, and frames every move as data-driven rather than White House-driven. If that read holds, expect the front end to drift lower through the back half of the year.

## What lending operators should do this quarter

If you run origination, a few concrete moves before the FOMC's June meeting:

- **Audit your warehouse and forward-flow pricing.** Lock in renewals on terms that benefit from front-end easing. Capital partners are negotiating spreads now against a curve that may look very different in 90 days.
- **Stress-test your underwriting at lower factor rates.** A 100 bp shift in cost of funds changes which deals are profitable at scale. Make sure your scoring and routing models reflect the new economics, not last year's.
- **Sharpen your merchant signal.** Cheaper money draws more brokers, more submissions, and more stacking. The lenders that compound through the cycle will be the ones who can tell — in real time — which merchants are quality, which brokers send funded paper, and which submissions are shopped across the street.
- **Activate dormant relationships.** Borrowers who passed in the high-rate environment are about to re-enter the market. Outbound to your declined-but-qualified list and your repeat-merchant base now, while inbound demand is still soft. The funders who own the relationship at the inflection point own the funded deal six months later.

## The bottom line

A Warsh confirmation does not, by itself, lower rates. The FOMC does that, and Powell remains chair through May 15. But the political clearance now in place sharply increases the probability that the next twelve months look very different from the last twelve — slower growth, easier money, more borrower demand, and more competition for every quality merchant.

Origination is a relationship business that runs on signal. The lenders who treat the next sixty days as preparation — tightening their data, mapping their broker channels, refreshing their merchant intelligence — will be the ones who scale into the cycle instead of chasing it.

That is the thesis behind SÜRNMORE: more relationships, more signal, more funded deals — at any cost.

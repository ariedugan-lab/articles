Target beat Wall Street estimates for Q1 2026 and raised its full-year sales outlook, signaling that the retailer’s long sales slump may finally be reversing. CEO Michael Fiddelke has pointed to price cuts and a renewed focus on value as the primary drivers of returning foot traffic. For small business lenders, this turnaround is not a broad signal to relax underwriting. It is a specific red flag for any borrower whose revenue depends on supplying goods to Target or similar big-box discounters.

The mechanism is straightforward but often invisible to standard cash flow models. Target is winning back shoppers by lowering prices. That means the retailers’ suppliers — small manufacturers, private-label producers, packaging firms, logistics providers — are being asked to deliver higher volumes at lower per-unit margins. A supplier that used to sell 10,000 units at a 25% margin now sells 15,000 units at a 18% margin. Gross profit dollars may stay flat or even rise slightly, but the working capital cycle turns violent.

Higher volume requires more raw materials, more labor, more warehouse space, and longer payment terms. Target, like most large retailers, pays its suppliers on net-60 or net-90 cycles. The supplier must finance the gap between paying its own vendors (often net-30) and receiving payment from Target. In a margin-compressed environment, that gap widens relative to cash flow. The supplier’s debt service coverage ratio — the metric most lenders rely on — degrades even as top-line revenue grows.

Standard underwriting that looks only at trailing 12-month revenue and profit will miss this inflection. The trailing data still reflects the old, higher-margin environment. The forward order book shows increasing volume but narrowing spreads. Lenders who approve funding based on historical cash flow are effectively underwriting to a business that no longer exists.

The risk is not limited to direct suppliers. Second-tier businesses — local trucking companies, temp staffing agencies, raw material distributors — also feel the squeeze when their own clients are squeezed by retail margin pressure. The entire chain tightens.

For funders, the concrete implication is a need to segment retail supply chain borrowers and apply stress tests. A simple sensitivity analysis: what happens to the borrower’s cash conversion cycle if unit margins drop by 500 basis points but volume rises by 30%? If the borrower has existing debt, does the additional working capital requirement push the leverage ratio above the funder’s threshold? Most standard scoring models do not run this scenario.

Brokers and ISOs who originate loans or merchant cash advances to businesses in the retail supply chain should watch for specific signals: a sudden increase in purchase orders from Target or Walmart, a change in the borrower’s average invoice size, or a lengthening of days sales outstanding. These are not inherently negative, but they indicate a structural shift that needs a different capital solution. The best product for these borrowers is often a revenue-based financing facility that adjusts repayment to actual weekly revenue, not a fixed-term loan with rigid amortization.

Merchant cash advance providers have an edge here because their repayment adjusts with revenue volume. But they must also adjust their underwriting. A borrower with rising revenue but falling margins may have a stable gross profit dollar amount but thinner net profit after debt service. MCA funders should apply a profit margin floor, not just a revenue multiple. If the borrower’s cost of goods sold is rising faster than revenue, the effective cost of capital becomes unsustainable.

The Target turnaround also creates an opportunity for lenders who can offer working capital lines specifically tied to purchase orders. Purchase order financing, or inventory finance, can bridge the gap for suppliers who have confirmed orders from Target but lack the liquidity to fulfill them. This is a niche that is underserved because traditional banks require lengthy track records and clean balance sheets. Alternative lenders can step in with faster underwriting that focuses on the creditworthiness of the buyer (Target) rather than the supplier. The risk is low as long as the supplier can deliver. The reward is a short-term, high-yield asset.

ISOs should educate their retail supply chain clients about this shift before the clients face a liquidity crisis. A proactive conversation about working capital needs can position the broker as a strategic partner, not just a product pusher. The goal is to help the borrower secure funding before the margin compression forces them to take on expensive emergency debt or miss a payment to Target.

This is not a permanent structural shift. If consumer demand for value continues, Target may eventually raise prices again once traffic stabilizes. But for the next two to four quarters, the squeeze will be real. Lenders who ignore it will see higher default rates in their retail supply chain portfolios. Those who adapt will capture a growing segment of borrowers who need smarter capital than the market currently offers.

The broader lesson: a retailer’s earnings beat does not mean the whole ecosystem is healthy. It often means the strongest link is getting stronger by squeezing the weakest links. Small business lenders must look past the headline and into the invoice-level mechanics of their borrowers’ businesses.

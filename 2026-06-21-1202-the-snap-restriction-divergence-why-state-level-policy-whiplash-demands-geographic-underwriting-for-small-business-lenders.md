The MAHA-aligned push to restrict SNAP purchases of soda, candy, and processed foods is no longer a fringe proposal. In 2026, a growing number of states have adopted or are actively considering bans on using SNAP benefits for what they classify as junk food. The result is a patchwork of rules that creates uneven demand shocks across the same product categories—and a new layer of geographic risk for small business lenders.

For funders, brokers, and ISO networks that serve the 600,000+ small businesses in the food and beverage supply chain—retail grocers, convenience stores, beverage distributors, snack manufacturers, and independent wholesalers—this divergence demands a shift in underwriting. Standard cash-flow models that treat the entire U.S. market as uniform will miss the real signal: a borrower’s risk now depends on where their customers live and how much of their revenue comes from SNAP-eligible products in restrictive states.

## The Policy Patchwork Is Creating Real Demand Discontinuities

As of mid-2026, at least 12 states have implemented or are actively phasing in SNAP restrictions on specific categories. California and New York lead with broad bans on sugary drinks, candy, and highly processed snacks. Texas and Florida have rejected similar measures. The gap matters because low-income consumers—who disproportionately rely on SNAP—adjust their purchasing behavior quickly when benefits are limited. A small beverage distributor that sells 40% of its volume to corner stores in California faces a different demand trajectory than one whose customers are in Texas.

This is not a theoretical risk. Early data from California’s pilot program shows a measurable drop in unit sales of carbonated soft drinks at SNAP-authorized retailers, with corresponding increases in shelf-stable juices and dairy alternatives. For small independent retailers, the shift can compress margins immediately—they either discount to clear existing inventory of banned items or write off stock that can no longer be sold to SNAP customers. Both outcomes hit working capital.

## Why This Creates a New Underwriting Signal

Traditional food-and-beverage underwriting relies on historical cash flow, inventory turnover ratios, and a general sense of category stability. The SNAP restriction divergence introduces a variable that is both sudden and geographically concentrated. A borrower with three stores in a restrictive state may see 15–20% of their sales evaporate overnight if a ban takes effect. That kind of shock is not captured in trailing twelve-month financials.

The signal is especially acute for businesses that depend on high-velocity, low-margin processed goods. These products are often financed through short-term working capital lines. When demand collapses, inventory holding periods lengthen, and lenders face the risk of collateral erosion. Even if the borrower pivots to exempt items, the transition requires upfront capital for new stock, marketing, and possibly new supplier relationships. That puts additional strain on cash flow.

Moreover, the regulatory timeline is unpredictable. A state may announce a ban with a 90-day implementation window—too short for a small business to renegotiate leases or contracts. Lenders that have not evaluated the borrower’s geographic footprint and SNAP reliance are flying blind.

## Concrete Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs

**For funders** underwriting directly: Build a state-level policy risk score into your credit model. Ask borrowers to provide a breakdown of revenue by state, with detail on the share derived from SNAP transactions (if applicable). For businesses in restrictive or pending-restriction states, stress-test cash flows assuming a 15–25% decline in sales of the affected categories. Adjust advance rates downward or require personal guarantees for borrowers with high concentration in those states.

Also, examine inventory composition. Lenders that finance inventory directly—through floor plans or revolving lines—should request a category-level breakdown. If more than 20% of collateral is in soda or candy, and the borrower operates in a restrictive state, the loan-to-value ratio should be recalibrated. Consider requiring a diversification plan as a covenant.

**For brokers** originating deals: This is an opportunity to add value by educating borrowers about the risk before they apply. If a client runs a small grocery chain in a state like Illinois or Michigan, where restrictions are being debated, a broker can advise them to shift product mix early. That reduces the client’s risk profile and makes the loan more attractive to funders. Brokers who ignore this signal may see their deals declined more often as underwriters tighten criteria on SNAP-exposed categories.

**For ISO networks** managing large portfolios: Segment your portfolio by state and by industry sub-vertical (e.g., convenience stores, beverage distributors, snack manufacturers). Identify which segments have the highest overlap with the restricted product categories. Then proactively communicate with those ISO partners about the policy developments in their states. Use the data to negotiate better terms with funders: a portfolio with heavy exposure to restrictive states may warrant a risk-adjusted pricing tier. Conversely, businesses in states without restrictions might be undervalued—a potential growth area for originations.

## The Upstream Supply Chain Risk

Independent small manufacturers of processed foods and beverages are an often-overlooked borrower class. A regional snack company that supplies 30% of its output to distributors serving California could see orders drop significantly. Unlike big food giants like PepsiCo or Coca-Cola, these smaller players lack the scale to reformulate products or shift production to exempt categories quickly. For lenders, the working capital risk is amplified because the demand shock travels through the entire distributor network. A distributor’s inventory problems become the manufacturer’s accounts receivable problems.

When a small manufacturer’s largest distributor can’t move goods, payment terms get stretched. Lenders that have not accounted for this upstream contagion will see late payments and defaults rise in sectors that seemed stable just a year prior.

## How to Operationalize the New Underwriting Framework

Update your loan application to include two new fields: (1) percentage of revenue from SNAP transactions, and (2) geographic breakdown by state of customer locations. For existing borrowers, run a portfolio scan using this data. Flag any account where SNAP-eligible product sales in restrictive or pending-restriction states exceed 10% of total revenue.

Next, incorporate a forward-looking policy indicator. Track state-level legislation via a service like GovTrack or LexisNexis, or use a vendor that monitors regulatory risk. Score each state on a 1–5 scale based on likelihood and speed of SNAP restrictions. Multiply that score by the borrower’s revenue exposure to the state. A high score signals a need for increased reserves or a reduced credit limit.

Finally, require borrowers in high-risk states to demonstrate a contingency plan: a strategy for shifting product mix, agreements with alternative suppliers for exempt categories, or a plan to adjust store formats. These steps do not eliminate the risk, but they show that management is aware and capable of adapting. That is a positive underwriting signal in itself.

## The Broader Market Context

Large food and beverage companies are already reformulating products and lobbying for national standards to avoid this patchwork. But small businesses cannot afford those efforts. Lenders who treat this as a one-off compliance story will miss the structural shift. The SNAP restriction divergence is a permanent feature of the regulatory landscape because the underlying political pressure—from both MAHA advocates and fiscal conservatives—shows no signs of fading.

For the lending ecosystem, the winners will be those who embed geographic policy risk into their underwriting today. The losers will be those who wait until state-by-state bans start showing up in their portfolio as unexpected delinquencies and charge-offs.

This is not a call to stop lending to food and beverage small businesses. It is a call to lend smarter—with a map, a policy radar, and a clear-eyed view of where the demand is actually headed.

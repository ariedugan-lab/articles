# The Airfare Floor: Financing the Working Capital Gap Inside Corporate Travel Management

Commercial airfare is not responding to the usual demand cycle. Airlines are paying billions more in fuel costs this year and they have pushed those costs into ticket prices. The result is not a temporary spike. The airline fuel bill has created a price floor. For small business lenders, the question is not whether travelers will keep paying. It is which businesses in the travel supply chain now need more working capital to operate at that higher price level.

The overlooked borrower in this environment is the corporate travel management company (TMC). A TMC books flights, hotels, and rail for business clients. It often extends net 30 or net 60 payment terms to those clients. But it must settle with the airline through ARC or BSP immediately. When airfare rises, the amount the TMC must front before the client pays rises by the same amount. The TMC's revenue is based on a booking fee or markup, so its margin does not grow with airfare. Its working capital requirement does. That gap is a lending opportunity.

## The real borrowing trigger is the settlement cycle, not ticket demand

Most travel-industry underwriting starts with a demand forecast. That approach is backwards in this environment. Demand for business travel is not the binding constraint. The binding constraint is the cash conversion cycle inside the TMC.

A typical transaction works in four steps. The TMC issues a ticket and triggers an ARC settlement obligation. The airline is paid by the TMC within a short settlement window. The client is invoiced and pays on net terms. If the average ticket price is 20 percent higher because of fuel, the TMC's outstanding receivable balance is 20 percent higher for the same booking volume. The TMC's revenue, if it charges a fixed booking fee, is unchanged. Its need for funding has increased.

This is not a one-time shock. Airlines have signaled that fuel costs are a persistent input, not a cyclical blip. That means TMCs will carry a higher structural receivable balance. Lenders that have not adjusted their facilities are underfunding the borrower precisely when the transaction volume is most valuable.

## Underwriting for the fuel-floor era: use ARC data, not profit and loss statements

A TMC is a better lending candidate than its income statement suggests. The full ticket price is pass-through, not revenue. A lender looking at revenue will see a thin margin and conclude the business is weak. The correct lens is the receivable ledger and the ARC settlement record.

ARC data gives a direct view into the borrowing base. It shows gross ticket sales, the number of transactions, average ticket price, and the settlement cycle. A lender can verify that each receivable corresponds to an issued ticket and an airline obligation. That is high-quality collateral. It is not an invoice for services performed; it is a transportation ticket with a confirmed carrier and a known refund policy. That traceability makes the receivable suitable for asset-based lending.

The underwriting model should include the following metrics.

- Gross ticket volume by month, not revenue. This is the actual cash flow that flows through the TMC.
- Net 30 and net 60 mix by client. The weighted collection period determines the size of the funding gap.
- Top client concentration. If one corporate client is 40 percent of outstanding receivables, the line must be sized with that concentration risk in mind.
- Refund and cancellation rate. Higher airfares have not lowered refund activity. Large corporate clients often have flexible fares. That creates a receivable reversal risk that must be measured.

When airfares rise, average ticket price rises. It is tempting to assume that revenue quality improves. In fact, the same number of tickets produces a larger dollar receivable. The collateral pool grows, but the DSO in dollar terms grows too. A lender that cannot fund the larger pool will force the TMC to either reduce volume or take on expensive merchant cash advances. That is the wrong outcome for both parties.

## Product design: a borrowing base facility tied to ARC receivables

The right product is a revolving line of credit tied to eligible ARC receivables. The lender advances a percentage of approved outstanding tickets. The percentage should depend on the collection history of the client base. For a diversified TMC with net 30 terms, an advance rate of 75 to 80 percent is defensible. For a concentrated TMC with net 60 terms, the advance rate should be lower and the monitoring more direct.

The facility should be structured around ARC weekly settlement cycles. This allows the lender to verify the gross ticket volume in near real time. It also creates a predictable draw-and-repay rhythm. A TMC that issues tickets weekly, invoices clients, and collects on a 30-day cycle will use the line continuously. That is a sticky lending relationship with high transaction frequency.

For funders, this is an operational fit. ARC data can be integrated into the lending platform through the TMC's back-office reporting. The lender can automate borrowing base certificates and monitor the aged receivable report on a daily basis. The collateral is not a vague stream of cash flow; it is a verifiable list of tickets and invoices.

## Risk controls for the fuel-floor era

Persistent high fuel costs do not eliminate risk. They change where risk concentrates. The following controls should be part of the credit approval.

- Require the TMC to maintain ARC accreditation. This is the baseline for the receivable to be valid.
- Watch refund rates as a percentage of gross ticket volume. Flexible business fares can produce high refund activity. A sudden increase in refunds will shrink the borrowing base.
- Monitor changes in corporate client payment behavior. If a TMC's top clients extend payment terms in response to their own fuel-related cost pressure, the lender should see it in the DSO and the aging report.

Do not underwrite to fuel price forecasts. The value of this vertical is that the TMC's cash flow is driven by ticket volume and client payment terms, not by the price of jet fuel. If fuel prices fall, average ticket prices will fall, and the outstanding receivable balance will shrink. That is not a credit loss; it is a reduction in line utilization. The lender should structure the facility so the commitment can flex with the borrowing base.

## What funders, brokers, and ISO networks should do now

Funders should treat corporate travel management as a separate vertical, not as part of the broader travel and entertainment lending bucket. The borrower profile is different. The collateral is different. The product is closer to invoice factoring than to unsecured working capital. A dedicated credit policy and a dedicated operations team are justified because the ARC settlement cycle is predictable and data-rich.

Brokers and ISO networks should target TMCs with 5 to 50 employees and between 2 million and 20 million in annual airfare volume. These firms are too large for a consumer credit card and too small for a corporate credit facility. Many are using merchant cash advances because their bank cannot underwrite the pass-through model. The broker that explains the difference between a cash-flow borrowing base and an ARC-based line will win the deal.

The conversation should not start with a mention of fuel prices. It should start with the TMC's own settlement cycle. Ask how many days pass between paying ARC and collecting from the client. That number is the borrowing need. The airfare floor is the reason the need is bigger today than it was twelve months ago. It is not the underwriting signal. The settlement cycle is the signal.

## The opportunity is the float, not the fuel price

Airlines are paying billions more for fuel. Airfares are likely to stay elevated. That puts pressure on every business in the travel supply chain. The business lender that responds with a general warning about travel exposure will miss the real trade. The TMC sector is not a distressed borrower. It is a borrower with a structurally larger working capital requirement.

The lender that recognizes this can build a durable, data-backed lending product that is only becoming more necessary as airfares remain high. The broker or ISO network that brings an ARC-aware lender to a TMC will create a relationship that renews every week. The price of fuel created the condition. The cash conversion cycle is the credit. Underwrite the cycle, not the headline.

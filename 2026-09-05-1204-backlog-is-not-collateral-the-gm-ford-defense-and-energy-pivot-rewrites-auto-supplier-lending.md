GM and Ford are running a second race. Both automakers are positioning for U.S. defense contracts and energy storage revenue. Public commentary treats this as an expansion story. For small business lenders, it is an underwriting signal. The suppliers that serve these OEMs are about to enter a different cash cycle, and financial statements will not show the full shift until it is too late to restructure.

**End-market diversification is not customer diversification**

The quick read from a broker desk will be positive: GM and Ford are diversifying into defense and grid storage. Suppliers that feed those automakers will benefit from more end-market demand. True. Defense budgets and utility storage programs can smooth out vehicle cycles. But for a small business lender, the binding constraint is not end-market demand. It is the supplier's ability to fund performance, convert work into cash, and liquidate inventory if the program stalls.

When a parts supplier moves from passenger-vehicle production volume to defense program work, the contract mechanics change. A vehicle build is a recurring schedule. The OEM issues releases, the supplier ships, and an invoice becomes due on a defined date. Defense work is milestone-based. Acceptance, government funding, and flow-down clauses can place the automaker between the supplier and the paying customer. The supplier now has a receivable that depends on the prime contract, not just on the OEM's willingness to pay.

**Program revenue changes the collateral story**

The strongest auto-supplier credits have inventory that is tied to known vehicle platforms. If a platform loses share, liquidation is still possible through the parts market. In defense and energy storage work, specificity is higher. Cables, enclosures, thermal controls, and structural components built to a military specification or a utility project design do not have an active spot market. WIP that is 60 percent complete on a canceled program is not inventory. It is a loss.

Lenders that use a standard borrowing base need to classify program-specific WIP and raw material separately. Program inventory should get a lower advance rate. Even when the order comes from a major OEM, the project is the collateral context. Advance rates on unsold generic auto parts are not the same as advance rates on parts manufactured for a fixed defense contract with unique source approval.

The same logic applies to receivables. A defense receivable is not ordinary trade receivables because acceptance has not happened. If a supplier bills a prime for delivered hardware but the prime can reject for documentation, condition, or government specification compliance, the receivable is conditional. Lenders should age it based on acceptance, not invoice date. Until the prime accepts the deliverable, there is no reliable borrower asset. Treating the full invoice as eligible collateral creates a false liquidity cushion.

**The cash conversion trap**

GM and Ford entering energy storage will add another layer: project-based revenue. Energy storage systems are deployed in phases. A supplier may have to build modules ahead of a project schedule and receive payment only after installation milestones. In automotive production, the cash conversion cycle is roughly aligned with shipments and monthly schedule stability. In project work, the gap between spending and payment can stretch ninety to one hundred twenty days.

Suppliers may try to bridge that gap with merchant cash advances or daily ACH loans. That is the wrong product for milestone revenue. A supplier with monthly invoice flow can fund an MCA. A supplier with large draws at project milestones cannot support a fixed percentage-of-receivables daily debit from cash already spent on materials. Lenders that push daily-payment products into this environment are setting up defaults. Brokers need to direct auto suppliers with defense and energy backlogs toward contract financing, factoring, or asset-based lines built around project cash flows.

**What the lender must verify**

Do not approve based on the headline. A borrower that tells a broker GM or Ford won a defense award still needs direct verification. Lenders must confirm who is the buying entity, whether the supplier is a direct subcontractor or second-tier, what clearance and compliance obligations apply, what acceptance criteria govern invoices, whether the prime can withhold payment based on language in the prime contract, and how much of the supplier's projected revenue is recurring production versus one-off program work.

No letter of intent or press release qualifies. The borrower should provide signed work orders, purchase orders, or subcontracts with payment terms. If the supplier is not direct, map the chain back to the prime and identify who is obligated. A receivable from a joint venture is different from a receivable from Ford Motor Company. A GM defense contract might sit inside GM Defense or another subsidiary. Commercial parent guarantees may not cover defense-specific subsidiaries or flow-down disputes.

The actual lending opportunity

This pivot is not here to discourage originations. It creates a clear vertical for lenders with product discipline. Auto suppliers that win defense or energy work need working capital during the transition. The volume is high, but contract terminology matters. Program revenue is financeable when the advance rate is calibrated, the collateral is controlled, and the accepting entity is verified.

For funders, the opportunity is in contract-specific lines and government receivable finance. A small business lender can offer a facility sized to the contract, with a cut-over to standard receivables after acceptance. That avoids contaminating the entire borrowing base with one program. It also gives the lender visibility into project drawdowns and invoicing, which is where early warning signs appear.

For brokers and ISO networks, the instruction is simple. Do not take a defense or energy narrative at face value and settle for any small business loan product. Ask about contract type, payment waterfall, and revenue segmentation. The broker that forces clean documentation into the file is the one that protects the funder and the borrower.

In the GM-Ford defense race, the OEMs can hide liquidity issues behind balance sheets. Their suppliers cannot. Lenders need to underwrite to the supplier's cash cycle, not to the automaker's brand.

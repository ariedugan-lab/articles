## Rank is not risk

Burger King has overtaken Wendy's as the second-largest burger chain in the U.S. by system-wide sales. For lenders, that ranking is not a credit event. It is a signal to re-examine how brand-level aggregates mask unit-level stress.

System-wide sales count every dollar generated by corporate and franchised locations. They are not the franchisor's revenue, and they are not the franchisee's cash flow. A brand can climb the ranking by adding units, by raising prices, by winning traffic, or by holding stores open longer. Each driver has a different credit implication for the operators borrowing money.

Do not underwrite the press release. Decompose the ranking change into unit growth, average unit volume, and same-store sales. Same-store sales growth is the cleanest signal of consumer demand. Average unit volume tells you the scale of a typical operation. Unit growth tells you whether the brand is expanding through franchisee investment or through cannibalization. A brand can move up with strength in only one of those components while the franchise base gets weaker.

## Unit economics, not brand rankings

Franchisees borrow against cash flow, not against chain-level sales. A Burger King franchisee's debt service capacity depends on the P&L of a specific location or portfolio. That P&L starts with revenue, but then deducts royalties, marketing fund contributions, food and paper costs, labor, occupancy, and mandatory capital reserves.

Royalty and marketing fund obligations together often run between 7 and 10 percent of gross sales in QSR franchise systems. Those cash outflows sit ahead of debt service. They are not optional. They do not disappear in a slow month. For a franchisee that has taken on debt for a remodel, those charges can push fixed charge coverage below 1.0 even when EBITDA looks acceptable.

Many franchise loans are underwritten from a tax return or a generic operating statement. That approach misses the fixed, unavoidable charges that determine whether a franchisee can actually repay debt. Lenders should calculate fixed charge coverage ratio using royalties, marketing fund contributions, lease payments, and required capital expenditures. If the ratio is thin at the brand's average revenue level, then a small negative same-store sales move creates a working capital gap, not just a profit warning.

## The refranchising risk

Burger King's path back to No. 2 has been driven by franchisor strategy. The brand has shifted corporate locations into franchisee hands, accelerated remodels, and pushed franchisees to invest in digital and delivery infrastructure. For the franchisor, refranchising improves reported margins and lowers capital exposure. For the franchisee, it adds debt and operating complexity.

When a brand ranks higher because the franchisor transferred risk to the franchise base, lenders inherit that risk. The same system-wide sales figure can accompany a more leveraged franchisee population. Funders with existing Burger King exposure should ask whether those loans were made after a remodel, whether the franchisee used deferred royalties, and whether the equipment finance payments are consuming cash flow that could otherwise service senior debt.

Before approving a Burger King or Wendy's loan, get the Franchise Disclosure Document. Read Item 19 and compare the financial performance representations to the applicant's actual operating statements. If the brand's Item 19 shows a range of average unit volumes, locate the applicant within that range. A franchisee in the lower quartile with the same brand name and a weaker trade area is not the same credit as a top-quartile operator.

## Value menus and margin compression

Burger King's rise also comes during an intense value-menu war. Discounted sandwiches drive traffic and system sales, but they also degrade average ticket and gross margin. A $5 meal can fund royalties and labor, but it may not fund debt service. If a franchisee's revenue mix shifts toward promotional items, revenue quality falls.

Lenders need to ask for transaction-level data. What is the average check? What percentage of sales comes from discount offers? Is same-store traffic growing while average check shrinks? Both can rise, but margin is the variable that matters. A franchisee can report higher revenue and still have less cash available for debt service because every incremental dollar is being sold at a lower gross margin.

For restaurant cash flow analysis, use unit-level P&Ls that separate promotional revenue from regular menu revenue. Stress-test the P&L with a blended margin reduction. If the franchisee's debt service coverage is acceptable only at full-menu margins, the loan is overleveraged in today's competitive environment.

## What brokers and ISO networks should do

Brokers and ISO networks tend to segment by brand popularity. They should segment by cash flow stability instead. Build a portfolio flag for QSR franchisees with high mandatory fund contributions, remodel obligations, and value-menu dependence.

The Burger King vs. Wendy's rank change is a good reason to review files, not because rank itself is a credit attribute, but because the underlying strategies that produced it changed cash flow dynamics. If you have concentration in either brand, re-underwrite those files now.

Submit loan packages with unit-level P&Ls, debt schedules, lease abstracts, and a capital expenditure plan. Highlight fixed charge coverage ratio and debt yield. Funders that see those metrics will respond faster. Funders that receive only a brand name and three years of tax returns will slow down or decline.

For new originations, identify franchisees who have recently completed or started a remodel. That capital expenditure is a signal of deferred maintenance or brand commitment, but it is also a claim on future cash flow. Season the loan request with the actual cost, the amount funded by the franchisee, and the expected sales lift. If the sales lift is not documented, underwrite the loan without it.

## Stress scenarios for QSR portfolios

Run a stress scenario that models a 10 to 15 percent revenue decline, a 200 basis point labor cost increase, and a 5 percent food cost increase. Then recalculate fixed charge coverage. If the franchisee's coverage falls below 1.0 in the scenario, the loan is not term debt; it is a working capital gap.

For high-leverage QSR operators, the correct financing structure may be a revenue-based product or a shorter-term facility, not a ten-year amortizing loan. The product design must match the volatility of the cash flow. A franchisee with a remodel obligation and a value-menu-heavy revenue stream needs a structure that can absorb a bad quarter without triggering default.

Funders should also monitor local competitive density. A Burger King and a Wendy's in the same trade area can split traffic. When one brand starts outperforming by system-wide sales, the underperforming brand's franchisees may lose market share faster than their P&Ls reveal. Look at store-level distance and overlapping delivery zones.

## The bottom line

The No. 2 rank belongs to the brand. The risk belongs to the lender. System-wide sales are an aggregate. They hide unit-level losses, cannibalization, refranchising risk, and margin compression. The next loan request from a Burger King franchisee should not be approved because Burger King overtook Wendy's. It should be approved because the unit's cash flow, after royalties, marketing funds, leases, and capex, can cover debt service with room for disruption.

For small business lenders, the discipline is identical in every vertical: underwrite the operator, not the operator's brand. The Burger King ranking change is a useful reminder that brand headlines and borrower creditworthiness are two different data sets. Lenders who understand that gap will avoid the number-two trap. Lenders who ignore it will fund the next default.

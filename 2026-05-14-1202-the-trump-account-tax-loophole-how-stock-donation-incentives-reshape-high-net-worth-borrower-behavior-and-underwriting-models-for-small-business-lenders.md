A proposed policy change allowing wealthy donors to contribute appreciated stock to “Trump Accounts” without triggering capital gains tax is not just a tax story. For small business lenders, funders, brokers, and ISO networks, it is a signal that the financial behavior of high-net-worth borrowers is about to shift in ways that standard underwriting models do not capture.

The policy, as reported, permits stock contributions to these accounts, effectively letting donors offload years of share appreciation with zero tax liability. The double tax benefit — no capital gains and a charitable deduction — creates a strong incentive for wealthy individuals to move publicly traded equities into these vehicles. That movement changes the composition of a borrower’s liquid net worth, their available collateral, and their ability to service debt on schedule.

For funders who originate loans to business owners with concentrated stock positions, the immediate implication is a reduction in pledgeable assets. A borrower who holds $5 million in low-basis Apple shares today might, under this policy, donate a large portion to a Trump Account. The shares disappear from the balance sheet. The borrower’s net worth on paper stays roughly the same (they receive a tax deduction), but the liquidity and marketability of that wealth drops. The lender loses a high-quality collateral option that could have been pledged via a securities-based line of credit or used as a personal guarantee backstop.

Brokers and ISOs should expect a growing subset of wealthy borrowers who appear asset-rich on tax returns but whose actual accessible collateral is thinner. A borrower’s adjusted gross income may show large charitable deductions, masking the fact that those deductions came from stock donations that reduced the borrower’s liquid holdings. Standard verification of liquid assets via brokerage statements will need to include a check for recent stock transfers to Trump Accounts. If that transfer is not disclosed, the lender’s asset coverage ratio is overstated.

The policy also affects debt service capacity. Wealthy business owners often use periodic stock sales to generate cash for loan payments or business expenses. With a tax-free donation option, the incentive to sell shares is reduced. A borrower who would have sold $200,000 in stock to cover a quarterly payment may instead choose to donate that stock for the tax benefit and then draw on other, potentially more expensive, sources of liquidity — or simply defer the payment. Lenders who rely on historical patterns of realized capital gains as a liquidity proxy will miss the shift.

There is also a timing risk. The policy could be enacted retroactively or with a short window before implementation. Borrowers who anticipate the change may accelerate stock donations, creating a spike in tax-deductible contributions and a sudden drop in personal balance sheet liquidity. Lenders with existing exposure to borrowers who are heavy stock donors need to stress-test for a scenario where $1 million in pledged brokerage assets is reduced to $200,000 within a quarter.

For ISO networks that aggregate borrower data, this is a new data point to track. Tags like “Trump Account donor” or “high charitable deduction” should become part of the risk profile. Brokers should ask borrowers directly: do you plan to use a Trump Account? Have you transferred stock to one in the last twelve months? The answer affects the reliability of the net worth figures on the application.

Funders who lend to businesses that rely on high-net-worth individual investors — for example, real estate syndications, venture debt funds, or small business investment companies — face a parallel risk. If wealthy limited partners divert capital into Trump Accounts instead of committing to new funds, the flow of equity into small businesses slows. Lenders who depend on sponsor equity contributions will see deal pipelines thin. The policy could inadvertently tighten capital availability for startups and growth-stage firms that are not bankable on their own.

The underwriting adjustment is straightforward but requires discipline. First, request the latest two years of brokerage statements and specifically look for large stock outflows not matched by purchases. Second, compare the borrower’s realized capital gains history against their charitable deduction pattern. A borrower who claims $500,000 in charitable deductions but shows only $50,000 in cash donations is likely donating stock. Third, adjust liquid net worth calculations to exclude stock that has been donated or is likely to be donated under the new regime. Use a conservative haircut of 30% to 50% on publicly traded equities held by borrowers in high tax brackets, even if no donation has occurred yet, until the policy’s details are final.

This is not a hypothetical. The policy has been publicly proposed and is being debated. Lenders who wait for enactment will find themselves scrambling to re-underwrite a book of loans that suddenly looks riskier. The borrowers most affected are precisely the kind of high-net-worth entrepreneurs that small business lenders actively court: founders with large equity stakes, serial entrepreneurs with diversified portfolios, and business owners who have held stock for years with low cost basis.

Brokers and ISOs who proactively educate their clients about the underwriting implications will gain trust and referral flow. A borrower who understands why a lender is asking about stock donations is less likely to shop for a less rigorous funder who misses the risk. Transparency in the application process reduces adverse selection.

For the lending operator, the Trump Account policy is not a political statement. It is a balance sheet event. The question is whether your underwriting models are ready to capture the shift in where and how wealthy borrowers hold their wealth. If not, the loan that looks safe today may be undercollateralized tomorrow.

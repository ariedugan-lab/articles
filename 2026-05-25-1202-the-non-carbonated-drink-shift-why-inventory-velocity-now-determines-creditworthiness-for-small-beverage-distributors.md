The hard seltzer party is over. Non-carbonated alcoholic drinks such as Surfside, BeatBox, and similar ready-to-drink cocktails are capturing share of throat at a pace that has caught many beverage distributors and their lenders flat-footed. For small business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks, this consumer shift is not a headline to scroll past. It is a concrete signal to re-examine how you underwrite working capital and inventory risk in the beverage supply chain.

Hard seltzer went from a category that pulled in billions in revenue to one that now faces double-digit volume declines in many markets. Meanwhile, non-carbonated options — often sweeter, lower-alcohol, and packaged in pouches or cans that feel more like a treat than a drink — are growing at 30% to 50% year-over-year in certain channels. The CNBC report on this trend confirmed that Gen Z is the primary driver, choosing flavor and convenience over the carbonated fizz that defined the 2020–2023 boom.

For small businesses that manufacture, distribute, or retail these products, the problem is simple: the inventory they bought six months ago is now slower-moving. The cash tied up in those pallets of seltzer cannot be recycled into orders for the new hot categories. That inventory drag is the equivalent of a hidden working capital drain. Lenders that rely on backward-looking cash flow statements will see a borrower whose revenue held steady but whose inventory-to-sales ratio silently deteriorated.

**The Working Capital Stress Test**

The shift from seltzer to non-carbonated drinks changes the critical working capital cycle for beverage businesses. Distributors and retailers that had high turnover on seltzer now face a glut of product that must be sold at discount, dumped, or written off. The replacement inventory — Surfside, BeatBox, and similar SKUs — carries its own demand risk. The result is a double squeeze: capital consumed by unwanted stock and capital required to chase the new wave.

Consider a small distributor in the Midwest with 60% of its stock in hard seltzer and 20% in non-carbonated lines. If seltzer inventory turnover drops from 8x to 4x, the company must hold twice as much seltzer in dollar terms to maintain the same sales level. That extra capital is not free. It either depletes cash reserves or requires new debt. If the distributor cannot secure additional working capital quickly, it may have to forgo purchasing the trending non-carbonated brands, losing market share to competitors that moved faster.

This is not a hypothetical scenario. Lender portfolios already contain beverage businesses that experienced the craft beer boom and bust, the hard seltzer surge, and now the non-carbonated correction. Each cycle leaves behind borrowers with aged inventory and thinner margins. The businesses that survive are those with flexible supply chains, strong relationships with multiple suppliers, and the ability to pivot inventory mix on a quarter’s notice.

**What Lenders Should Evaluate Today**

Standard cash flow underwriting does not capture inventory composition risk. A business may show stable revenue and positive EBITDA while its inventory aging schedule reveals a ticking time bomb. For funders, the key metrics are:

- Inventory turnover by product category, not just overall. A distributor that turns total inventory 6x per year may look healthy, but if the top three SKUs are hard seltzer lines that are losing velocity, the overall turnover is masking a decline.
- Inventory aging distribution. Products that have been on the shelf for more than 90 days are at risk of being sold at a loss. Lenders should request an aged inventory report alongside financial statements.
- Seasonality of the shift. The non-carbonated trend accelerated in late 2025 and early 2026. Lenders need to compare same-period inventory turnover year-over-year to see if the trend is already embedded in the borrower’s operations.
- Supplier concentration. Beverage businesses that rely heavily on a single brand or category (e.g., a distributor that was primarily a hard seltzer house) are more exposed. Diversification across alcoholic beverage subcategories is a credit-positive signal.

**Broker and ISO Implications**

For brokers and ISO networks, this shift opens a window for both risk and opportunity. Businesses that recognize the trend early will need working capital to fund the inventory pivot. They may require short-term loans to clear out old stock and purchase new product. Brokers who can identify these needs and match them with funders that understand fast-moving consumer goods will close more deals.

On the risk side, brokers must guide funders away from blanket approvals for beverage businesses based on historical performance alone. A company that looked strong in 2024 may now be sitting on a warehouse full of slow-moving seltzer. Brokers should request supplemental data on inventory mix and sales velocity by brand when submitting deals.

ISO networks should consider adding a beverage-specific risk overlay to their underwriting guidelines. This includes requiring inventory lists, supplier contracts, and a seasonal revenue analysis that accounts for the shift in consumer preference. Networks that fail to update their criteria risk funding deals that will default when the borrower cannot sell through its seltzer backlog.

**The Real Cost of Delayed Pivoting**

A beverage business that does not pivot quickly faces a cascade of consequences. Slow inventory reduces cash flow, which forces the business to delay payments to suppliers. Late supplier payments can trigger credit holds, stopping future shipments of the trending products. The business then loses shelf space in retail stores and distribution channels, creating a negative cycle that is hard to break.

Lenders that have funded such businesses without monitoring inventory composition may find themselves in a position where the borrower’s only exit is a distressed sale or a deep discount liquidation. Recovery rates in such scenarios are low. The collateral — beverage inventory — depreciates rapidly both in value and in marketability.

**Action Steps for Funders**

1. Review existing beverage portfolio exposure and identify borrowers with high hard seltzer or carbonated mix. Flag them for a reassessment of inventory turnover and aging.
2. Adjust underwriting criteria for new beverage deals. Require a breakdown of sales by product category for the last 12 months. Request forward-looking orders or purchase commitments for non-carbonated lines.
3. Shorten the covenant period for working capital loans in this vertical. Monthly or quarterly reporting on inventory velocity will give an early warning if the shift accelerates.
4. Build a relationship with beverage industry analysts or use third-party data sources that track shelf movement and consumer preferences. The same data that retailers use to manage inventory is available and can be integrated into credit decisions.

**The Bottom Line**

The non-carbonated drink shift is not a blip. It reflects a lasting change in Gen Z consumption habits that will reshape the beverage distribution landscape for several years. Small business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks that treat this as a temporary fad will miss the risk embedded in their current portfolios and the opportunity in the businesses that are agile enough to adapt.

Inventory velocity is the new credit metric. The businesses that can turn their stock into cash quickly — regardless of what consumers are drinking — will prove resilient. Lenders that understand and underwrite for inventory composition and turnover will outperform those that rely on trailing cash flow alone.

For brokers, the message is clear: the deals that look like safe repeat loans today may become tomorrow’s non-performing assets if the inventory stack is wrong. Verify what is on the shelves, not just what is on the balance sheet.

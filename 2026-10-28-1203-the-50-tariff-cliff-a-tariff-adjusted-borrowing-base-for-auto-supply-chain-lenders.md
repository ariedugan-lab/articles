## The 50% tariff is a collateral event, not a headline

Honda’s warning that 50% Canada auto tariffs would be catastrophic for the industry is a direct signal for small business lenders. Most funders treat tariff risk as a macro variable: a line in the news, a reason to tighten broadly, or a pricing adjustment at renewal. That approach is too coarse for auto parts suppliers, dealer floorplan borrowers, cross-border service vendors, and the brokers and ISO networks that originate them.

A move from 25% to 50% is not a linear increase. It is a threshold event. At 25%, many small operators can still reprice, shift sourcing, absorb some cost, or renegotiate with customers. At 50%, thin-margin parts distributors, machine shops, logistics vendors, and dealers run out of room. OEMs delay orders, push price concessions, and stretch payables. Dealers face floorplan stress when landed vehicle costs jump. Service vendors face parts price spikes and availability gaps. For funders, collateral value and repayment capacity can deteriorate before the next tax return shows it.

The underwriting response should be equally direct: build a tariff-adjusted borrowing base and monitor contract-level pass-through capacity. Do not treat all auto-adjacent small businesses as one risk bucket.

## Three exposures the standard file misses

**1. In-transit inventory and duty timing.** A borrowing base that includes inventory in transit may overstate collateral when the customs entry date determines duty. If a 50% tariff hits while goods are in transit, the landed cost can exceed the advance rate assumptions. Funders should exclude tariff-contingent in-transit inventory or apply a haircut tied to HS code, country of origin, and expected entry date. Brokers and ISO networks should collect customs broker reports and bills of lading as part of the file, not after closing.

**2. Cross-border receivables.** Canadian OEM and Tier 1 customers may stretch payables if tariffs compress margins. Invoice factoring and ABL advance rates need country and customer concentration caps. A supplier with strong receivables from a Canadian buyer can still face collection delay if the buyer slows payments across the board. Funders should stress test receivables at longer days sales outstanding and apply eligibility limits for tariff-exposed obligors. Brokers should document customer payment terms, currency, and any pass-through clause before submission.

**3. Pass-through capacity.** The key question is not whether a borrower is exposed to tariffs. It is whether the borrower can pass the cost through without losing volume. Auto parts suppliers with annual contracts and no price-adjustment language have weak pass-through capacity. Dealers with used-vehicle inventory and service revenue may have more flexibility than new-vehicle floorplan-heavy operators. Equipment financing borrowers in repair and maintenance may benefit from an aging fleet, but they still face parts price volatility. Funders need a simple pass-through score: contract terms, customer concentration, margin buffer, and pricing power.

## The underwriting fix: a tariff-adjusted borrowing base

Lenders do not need a new credit bureaucracy. They need a repeatable adjustment to the borrowing base and cash-flow model.

For inventory, identify tariff-exposed SKUs by HS code and country of origin. Apply a higher haircut to Canadian-origin parts and vehicles when a rate change is pending. Exclude inventory with unresolved customs valuation. For receivables, cap exposure to Canadian auto customers and to any single OEM or Tier 1 buyer. For equipment, review resale values and cross-border transferability. For working capital and MCA-style products, do not rely on top-line deposits without a tariff-adjusted cash-flow view.

Brokers and ISO networks can improve approval rates by pre-packaging the file with a one-page tariff exposure schedule: revenue tied to Canada, top customers, HS codes, inventory location, in-transit goods, contract pass-through terms, and FX exposure. Funders can then price the risk instead of declining the category outright. This is also a origination opportunity. Suppliers shifting toward domestic content need purchase order finance, equipment financing, and invoice factoring. Dealers and service operators need working capital to manage inventory and parts price swings. The lenders that can underwrite the transition will capture better-quality volume than those that issue blanket auto-tariff moratoriums.

## Contract-level monitoring beats broad covenants

A tariff event moves faster than annual financial statements. Funders should set monitoring triggers: a formal tariff rate change, OEM production cuts, customer payment extension requests, FX moves above a defined threshold, customs delays, and sudden inventory buildup. Covenants should focus on pass-through coverage, maximum customer concentration, maximum in-transit inventory, and minimum margin buffer.

ISO networks should standardize tariff fields in the application and train originators to ask for customs data, customer contracts, and supplier diversification. Brokers should not wait for a decline to gather that information. Funders should segment the portfolio by HS code and Canada exposure, then monitor delinquency and line utilization by segment. Alternative data helps: customs broker reports, bills of lading, FX rates, and customer payment behavior. Use them to update the borrowing base between renewals.

## The operator takeaway

The Honda warning is a leading indicator, not a final outcome. The useful signal is the threshold: 25% to 50% changes borrower behavior, collateral value, and collection risk. Small business lenders that underwrite pass-through capacity and use a tariff-adjusted borrowing base will avoid the worst losses and pick up volume from competitors that treat tariffs as a macro story. Funders should reprice and monitor. Brokers should document. ISO networks should standardize the data. The tariff cliff is a credit event, and the file should show it.

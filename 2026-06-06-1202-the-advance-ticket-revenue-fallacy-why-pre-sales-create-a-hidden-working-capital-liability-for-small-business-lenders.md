Broadway just closed a $1.9 billion season, the highest in its history. Ticket sales surged, Tony nominations drove further demand, and venues operated at near-full capacity. For a small business lender, that looks like a green light — strong revenue, growing demand, a proven business model. But treat that headline as a signal to look deeper.

The experience economy is booming, and small businesses that depend on live events, performances, festivals, and ticketed attractions are riding that wave. The problem is that traditional underwriting models, built for predictable, recurring revenue, fail to capture the real cash flow dynamics of businesses that collect money before delivering the service. Advance ticket sales are not the same as earned revenue. They are a liability. And when underwriters treat them as cash, they miss the risk that accumulates on the balance sheet.

**What the Broadway Numbers Actually Mean for Cash Flow**

A record season means a record number of tickets sold in advance. For a theater owner, ticket proceeds sit in a bank account long before the performance happens. Under a standard cash flow analysis, that looks like liquidity. But those dollars are not free. They are unearned revenue — money that must be refunded if the show cancels, the venue closes, or the performance is rescheduled. In many cases, ticket platforms hold the funds in escrow or require a reserve. Even when the business has access to the cash, the obligation to perform remains.

Consider a mid-sized venue that books touring acts. It sells $2 million in advance tickets for a six-month season. The cash appears on the balance sheet as a current asset. But the corresponding liability — the obligation to deliver the experience — sits on the other side. Underwriters who only look at the cash balance without adjusting for deferred revenue will overestimate the business’s available working capital. That overestimate leads to larger advances, thinner margins, and a higher probability of default when revenue timing collides with operational expenses.

**The Refund and Cancellation Risk That Doesn't Show in Bank Statements**

Experience-based businesses face a risk that most retail or service businesses do not: the liability to refund is large, concentrated, and can be triggered by events outside the owner’s control. A single artist cancellation, a weather event, or a public health scare can turn $500,000 in advance ticket revenue into a $500,000 cash outflow in a matter of hours. The business must refund quickly to maintain customer goodwill. If it has already spent that cash on marketing, deposits, or payroll, it is suddenly in a liquidity crisis.

This is not a hypothetical. In 2020, the live events industry saw $30 billion in refunds. Many businesses that had strong pre-pandemic ticket sales went under because they had used the advance cash for operating expenses, assuming the performances would happen. The same structural risk exists today, especially for smaller operators that lack the diversification of a multi-venue company.

For lenders, the underwriting question is not “how much cash do you have?” but “how much of that cash is actually yours?” A business with $200,000 in the bank and $150,000 in deferred ticket revenue has only $50,000 of unencumbered liquidity. That $50,000 must cover unexpected costs, payroll gaps, and working capital needs until the next revenue cycle. If the lender extends credit based on the gross cash number, the borrower may be over-leveraged from day one.

**How Seasonality Compresses the Risk Window**

Record Broadway seasons are seasonal by nature. The bulk of ticket sales happen in a few months. For the rest of the year, venues rely on slower periods, subscription renewals, or ancillary income. Underwriters who use trailing twelve-month revenue averages will smooth out the peaks, but that hides the real stress during the troughs.

A business that generates 60% of its annual revenue in a three-month window has a cash conversion cycle that is far more volatile than a business with even monthly sales. During the slow months, it must still pay rent, insurance, staff retainers, and debt service. If it borrowed against the peak-season cash flow, it may lack the liquidity to meet obligations in the off-season. The borrower’s bank balance in August looks healthy because of tickets sold for December. But in January, after refunds and seasonal slowdowns, that balance may be gone.

Lenders can address this by measuring “minimum available cash” over a 12-month period rather than average cash. Examine the trough months. If the business cannot maintain positive net liquidity in the low season without drawing on the advance ticket pool, it is a riskier credit. Require a debt service coverage ratio based on trailing three-month revenue, not annual averages, to expose seasonal gaps.

**The Underwriter’s New Toolkit for Experience-Based Borrowers**

Standard underwriting relies on bank statements, tax returns, and credit scores. For experience-economy businesses, those documents are insufficient. Lenders need to incorporate three additional data points:

**1. Deferred Revenue Ratio** — Calculate deferred revenue as a percentage of total assets. A ratio above 30% signals a high dependency on advance sales. For those borrowers, adjust the cash balance by subtracting the deferred revenue liability before computing working capital. A true liquidity metric is cash minus deferred revenue.

**2. Refund History and Cancellation Rate** — Request the business’s refund and cancellation data for the last three years. A high refund rate (above 5% of ticket sales) indicates either poor customer experience or weak demand forecasting. Both increase the likelihood that advance cash will need to be returned. Use this data to build a stress test: what happens to cash flow if refunds double?

**3. Seasonality-Adjusted Debt Service Coverage** — Instead of using full-year net income, calculate DSCR for the three lowest-revenue months. If the business cannot cover debt payments during those months without drawing on advance ticket cash, restrict loan size or require a reserve account funded by the borrower during peak months.

**Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs**

For funders: This is a vertical that is growing fast but remains under-served by traditional underwriting. Experience-economy businesses — live music venues, theaters, festival organizers, event production companies, ticketing platforms — are hungry for working capital. But lending to them requires a different risk model. Funders that build the capability to underwrite deferred revenue and seasonality will capture a high-yield portfolio with less competition. Those that stick to generic cash flow metrics will accumulate losses from over-leveraged borrowers.

For brokers and ISOs: This is a pitch opportunity. When you approach a venue owner or event producer, do not talk about rates and terms. Talk about cash flow structure. Ask how much of their bank balance is unearned. Explain that most lenders miss this risk and therefore underprice or overextend. Position yourself as the expert who understands the real liquidity behind the ticket sales. That differentiation closes deals and reduces chargebacks.

For the entire channel: The experience economy is not a fad. Consumer spending on events, travel, and entertainment continues to grow faster than goods. But the financial infrastructure for these businesses is underdeveloped. Lenders that adapt now will set the underwriting standard for the next decade. The ones that rely on backward-looking, average-based models will find themselves holding the bag after the next cancellation wave.

**The Takeaway**

$1.9 billion on Broadway is a headline. For a lender, it should be a red flag to look at how that money flows. Advance ticket sales are a liability, not liquidity. Seasonality compresses risk. Refunds can erase a year of profit in days. The businesses that survive and thrive are the ones that manage cash conservatively — and the lenders that survive are the ones that underwrite that reality.

Build your underwriting to see the liability behind the revenue. That is how you fund the experience economy without funding its hidden risk.

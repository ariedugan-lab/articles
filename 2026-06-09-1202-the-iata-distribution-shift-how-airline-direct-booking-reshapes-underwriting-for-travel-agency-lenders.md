## The IATA Distribution Shift: How Airline Direct Booking Reshapes Underwriting for Travel Agency Lenders

Every June, airline CEOs gather at the International Air Transport Association (IATA) annual meeting. This year’s summit in Rio de Janeiro was no exception: the agenda was dominated by capacity constraints, fuel costs, and—critically—the acceleration of direct distribution. For small business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks that serve travel agencies, the shift from traditional Global Distribution Systems (GDS) to airline-controlled New Distribution Capability (NDC) is not a distant trend. It is a structural change that rewrites working capital dynamics, revenue predictability, and creditworthiness of an entire borrower segment.

### The Distribution Direction: What Airline CEOs Actually Said

Multiple CEO statements at IATA 2026 confirmed that airlines are investing heavily in direct channels. American Airlines, Delta, and United have already set aggressive targets for NDC-based bookings. The goal: capture more ancillary revenue (seat selection, bags, priority boarding) at the point of sale, bypass the GDS transaction fees, and own the customer relationship. For small travel agencies that derive 40-60% of revenue from airline commission and GDS incentive payments, this is existential. The commissions are shrinking, incentive tiers are being restructured, and agencies that cannot connect via NDC risk being cut out of inventory or forced into higher-cost booking paths.

### Why Standard Underwriting Models Miss the Risk

Most small business lenders evaluate travel agencies on trailing 12-month revenue, recurring GDS incentive payments, and cash reserves. These metrics look stable on paper. But the IATA signals suggest a divergence: agencies that have already invested in NDC-certified aggregators or technology platforms will maintain or even improve margins. Those that remain on legacy GDS-only workflows will see their incentive payments decline and commission rates compress. The problem is that current bank statements and accounting reports do not reveal the distribution mix. A lender sees consistent deposits but cannot see that those deposits are slowly shifting from high-margin commissions to lower-margin service fees or last-minute net fares.

A concrete example: A mid-sized travel agency specializing in corporate travel may report $2M in annual revenue with 15% net margin. Underwriting models assume stability. But if 60% of that revenue comes from GDS incentive payments that are being phased out over 18 months, the agency’s true earning power is eroding. The decline is masked by lagging data. By the time bank statements reflect the drop, the agency is already in a working capital crunch.

### The NDC Technology Investment Trap

Small travel agencies face a forced capital expenditure to adopt NDC. They need to upgrade booking software, integrate with NDC-capable aggregators, and train staff. This investment can range from $10,000 to $50,000 depending on scale. For a business with thin margins, that cash outlay strains working capital. Lenders who see a sudden increase in equipment or software expenses may misinterpret it as growth spending when it is actually a defensive cost. The risk is that agencies that cannot fund the upgrade will lose access to competitive fares and become unviable within 24 months.

### Concrete Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISO Networks

**For Funders:** Adjust underwriting questionnaires to include questions about NDC readiness. Ask for the percentage of bookings processed through NDC versus legacy GDS. Request a summary of GDS incentive tiers and their expected expiration dates. Look for diversification into non-airline revenue streams such as hotel booking, tour packages, or corporate travel management fees. If an agency derives more than 50% of revenue from airline commissions, tighten advance rates or require personal guarantees. Also consider the age of the agency: older agencies with legacy systems may face higher switching costs.

**For Brokers:** When packaging travel agency loans, do not rely solely on credit scores and merchant statement volume. Obtain a 12-month trend of commission income from the airline remittance reports. If you see a declining percentage of commission revenue relative to total revenue, flag it as a risk factor. Encourage clients to show their NDC certification or partnership status. Brokers who can articulate this distribution risk will earn trust from underwriters and close deals faster.

**For ISO Networks:** The travel agency vertical is still underserved by alternative lenders. This shift creates an opportunity to originate loans for technology upgrades and working capital to bridge the transition. Build partnerships with NDC aggregators or travel tech vendors to identify agencies that are proactive. Offer short-term bridge loans (6-12 months) specifically for NDC integration costs. Also consider revenue-based financing tied to airline commission income, with covenants that require maintaining NDC connectivity.

### How to Restructure the Underwriting Framework

1. **Segment by Distribution Maturity.** Classify travel agencies into three tiers: 
   - Tier 1: Already NDC-capable, revenue diversified. 
   - Tier 2: In transition, moderate NDC adoption. 
   - Tier 3: Legacy-only, high reliance on GDS incentives. 
   Adjust credit limits and interest rates accordingly. Tier 3 borrowers may need higher risk pricing or collateral.

2. **Monitor Commission Velocity.** Track the frequency and size of airline commission payments. A decline in average commission per booking is a leading indicator. Require quarterly reporting of commission-to-revenue ratio.

3. **Stress Test for Ancillary Revenue Loss.** Airlines are increasingly bundling ancillaries into the base fare, reducing the opportunities for agencies to upsell. Model a scenario where ancillary commission income drops 30% and see if the agency can still service debt.

4. **Evaluate Technology Debt.** If an agency recently took out a loan for software, verify that the software is NDC-certified and not just a legacy GDS wrapper. Software that is not certified will become obsolete.

### The Broader Signal for Small Business Lending

The IATA meeting is a microcosm of a larger trend: industry-level distribution disruptions are becoming faster and more structural. Small business lenders who ignore these signals will face unexpected defaults in verticals that previously seemed stable. The travel agency space is just one example. The same dynamics apply to insurance brokers, ticket resellers, and any intermediary that relies on platform commissions. Underwriting must evolve from backward-looking financials to forward-looking distribution intelligence.

For the travel agency segment, the window to act is narrow. The next 12 to 18 months will separate the agencies that adapt from those that fail. Lenders who build NDC-aware credit models now will capture a defensible loan book. Those who wait for bank statements to show trouble will be left with the losses.

### Action Items for This Week

- Review your current travel agency loan portfolio. Flag any borrower whose merchant account volume comes predominantly from a single airline or GDS.
- Update your broker briefing sheet to include the NDC readiness question.
- Contact a travel technology partner (e.g., Travelport, Sabre, Amadeus) to understand which agencies in your region have certified NDC connectors.
- Set a minimum threshold for commission diversification: no more than 40% of revenue from airline commissions for new originations.

The distribution shift is already priced into airline stocks. It is not yet priced into small business credit. That gap is your opportunity.

## The claims denial float is a working capital signal

UnitedHealth Group topped estimates and raised its 2026 adjusted earnings outlook to at least $20.50 per share from a previous range of $19.50 to $20.00. The headline reads as insurer strength. For small business lenders, it reads as a provider-side cash conversion signal. When a payer manages high medical costs and expands margin, the cash does not disappear. It moves. It sits in claims review, prior authorization queues, denial rework, and slower reimbursement. That is the claims denial float. It is a working capital liability for the small businesses that deliver care.

## What changed for operators

Insurers are using utilization management, prior authorization, claim edits, and payment integrity programs to control costs. Providers feel that discipline as days in AR, denial rates, underpayments, and patient responsibility. A medical practice can show strong revenue on the income statement and still miss payroll. Lenders that underwrite on top-line deposits without adjusting for payer mix and denial velocity will misprice risk.

## Implications for funders

Underwrite revenue cycle, not just revenue. Ask for AR aging by payer, denial rate, clean claim rate, days in AR, and payer concentration. Look at trailing six to twelve months of deposits. Identify seasonality and lag. A practice with 40 percent of revenue from one Medicare Advantage plan is not the same as a diversified practice with 30 percent cash-pay. Funders should build separate scorecards for healthcare services. Use bank statement analysis to detect delayed insurance deposits versus patient payments. MCA and short-term working capital can bridge the float, but only if the borrower has a path to collect. If denials are rising, the advance can become a debt trap. Cap exposure by payer mix and require proof of denial management.

## Implications for brokers

Brokers and ISO networks need to package healthcare deals differently. Do not submit a medical practice as a generic service business. Include payer mix, AR aging, denial reports, and the revenue cycle vendor contract. Show the lender how the borrower converts claims to cash. If the practice uses an RCM company, provide performance metrics. If it has a high deductible patient mix, show collection rate at time of service. Brokers who document revenue cycle quality will place deals faster and at better pricing. Those who submit only tax returns and bank statements will get declines or high-risk offers.

## Implications for ISO networks

Train reps on healthcare revenue cycle vocabulary. They need to know the difference between gross charges, net revenue, allowed amount, contractual adjustment, denial, and write-off. They need to spot red flags: rising AR over 60 days, falling clean claim rate, high denial rate, single-payer concentration, out-of-network dependence, and heavy prior authorization. ISO networks can create vertical playbooks for dental, physical therapy, behavioral health, home health, DME, and labs. Each has different payer mix and denial patterns.

## New lending products

The claims denial float creates demand for revenue cycle financing. Options include medical receivables factoring, AR lines of credit, merchant cash advances, and term loans for RCM technology and staff. Funders can offer a revenue-based advance tied to net collections, not gross billing. They can structure weekly or daily remittance. They can require a lockbox or direct deposit change. For larger practices, a line of credit secured by net AR can work. For smaller practices, a short-term working capital advance can cover payroll during a denial wave. The key is to match repayment to the collection cycle. Do not put a daily payment on a practice that gets paid 45 to 90 days after service.

## Underwriting signals

Watch these metrics:

- Days in AR: above 45 days is a warning for most practices. Above 60 days is a cash flow problem.
- Denial rate: above 5 to 10 percent depending on specialty. Rising denials signal payer tightening.
- Clean claim rate: below 95 percent signals back-office weakness.
- Payer concentration: any payer above 30 to 40 percent is a concentration risk.
- Patient responsibility: high deductibles increase collection risk. Look at point-of-service collection rate.
- Revenue cycle vendor: outsourced RCM can be a strength if metrics are good, a weakness if the contract is month-to-month and performance is poor.
- Deposit trend: compare insurance deposits to patient deposits. A shift toward slower insurance payments is an early warning.
- MCA stacking: healthcare borrowers under cash flow pressure often stack advances. Check for multiple daily debits.

## Opportunity

The same payer cost discipline that drives UnitedHealth's raised outlook creates demand for working capital in healthcare services. Lenders that understand the float can fund good practices that banks reject. They can price for risk and structure for collection. They can build a repeatable vertical. Brokers and ISO networks can add healthcare revenue cycle financing to their product menu without treating it as generic small business lending.

## What to do next

Funders should create a healthcare scorecard with payer mix, denial rate, days in AR, clean claim rate, and deposit lag. Require AR aging and a denial report at application. Use a lockbox for receivables-backed products. Set exposure caps by payer concentration. Brokers should collect revenue cycle data before submission and pre-qualify borrowers on collection velocity, not just credit score. ISO networks should build specialty-specific training and marketing. Target practices with 20 to 50 employees, diversified commercial payer mix, and a documented denial management process. Avoid borrowers that cannot explain how they convert claims to cash. The claims denial float is not a temporary issue. It is an underwriting input.

## Risk management

Do not fund on gross charges. Do not accept accounts receivable aging without verification. Do not ignore payer mix. Do not assume a practice is safe because it is in healthcare. Healthcare is not a monolith. A cosmetic dental practice with cash-pay patients has different risk than a home health agency dependent on Medicare Advantage. A physical therapy clinic with a good commercial mix is different from a DME provider waiting on prior authorization.

## Bottom line

UnitedHealth's earnings beat is not just an insurer story. It is a signal that payers will continue to manage medical costs. That pressure lands on providers and healthcare-adjacent small businesses as slower cash conversion. For funders, brokers, and ISO networks, the play is to underwrite revenue cycle velocity, structure repayment around collection cycles, and treat the claims denial float as a first-class credit risk. The lenders that do will find a durable small business lending vertical. The ones that do not will confuse revenue with cash and misprice the risk.

# The Teardown Premium: Engine Scarcity Is a Working Capital Signal for Aviation Aftermarket Lenders

The story is not that airlines are retiring popular aircraft. It is that engine and component scarcity has changed the economic life of the airframe. When a serviceable engine or a hot-section part is worth more than the aircraft in flying condition, operators part out the plane years early. That creates a teardown premium, and it changes the borrowing base for the small businesses that dismantle airframes, repair engine accessories, trade parts, and move AOG inventory.

For funders, brokers, and ISO networks, the lending signal is not airline credit. It is component-level cash flow. The borrowers are not major carriers. They are FAA Part 145 repair stations, parts distributors, engine accessory shops, teardown operators, and logistics vendors. These businesses are absorbing demand created by engine shortages, but their cash conversion cycle is uneven and their collateral is hard to value with a blanket inventory lien.

## The collateral is the part record

Aircraft parts are not generic inventory. Each part carries a serial number, a traceability chain, and often a life-limited parts record. A serviceable part with an FAA 8130-3 tag and back-to-birth documentation trades at a different price than an untagged part with gaps. A funder that lends against average inventory will misprice the risk. The borrowing base needs serial-level eligibility: part number, serial number, tag status, repair history, remaining life, and market demand by engine family or airframe type.

That is the operator-grade implication. Do not treat aviation aftermarket inventory as one pool. Build a borrowing base by part class. Engine life-limited parts and accessories may carry stronger liquidity. Avionics and flight controls may hold value. Interiors and older airframe-specific parts may not. Apply haircuts by part class, not a single advance rate. Require a periodic inventory schedule and field exam. Reconcile the schedule to purchase invoices and sales history. If the borrower cannot produce traceable records, the collateral is not financeable at standard advance rates.

## The working capital gap is project-based

Teardown economics create lumpy cash flow. A borrower buys an airframe or engine, funds dismantling, pays for repair and recertification, then holds parts until buyers emerge. Revenue arrives in lots. Gross margin can be strong, but days to sell vary by part. That gap is a working capital problem, not just an equipment finance problem.

Funders can structure a revolving line with a borrowing base against eligible parts, plus a separate equipment loan for tooling, test cells, and shop equipment. That is inventory-backed lending with aviation-specific controls. Brokers and ISO networks should package the file with a parts inventory schedule, purchase agreements, repair invoices, customer purchase orders, sales history by part class, and customer concentration. Do not submit a teardown operator as a generic transportation deal. Submit it as an aviation aftermarket inventory and receivables credit.

MRO shops face a different version. Engine accessory repair and component overhaul demand rises when operators keep aircraft in service longer or need replacement parts. But capacity is constrained by test equipment, tooling, and skilled labor. Equipment financing can work here, but underwrite the shop on turnaround time, backlog quality, and customer mix. Revenue-based financing can fit service-heavy MRO shops, but only when receivables and backlog are verified. A backlog is not collateral. A purchase order with a creditworthy counterparty is.

## Revenue quality beats headline demand

The engine shortage is a tailwind, but not all aftermarket revenue is equal. Parts distribution can be recurring, but it is inventory intensive. Teardown revenue is project-based and lumpy. AOG logistics can generate high-margin revenue, but it requires cash to fund expedited freight and receivables. Component repair revenue depends on shop capacity and parts availability.

Underwriters should separate recurring service revenue from one-time part-out gains. Stress test the borrowing base for a decline in teardown premiums. If engine availability improves, the incentive to scrap early falls. Inventory that looked liquid at peak demand can become slow-moving. Use a 20 to 30 percent additional haircut in stress scenarios. Check customer concentration. A borrower selling mostly to one repair station or one lessor carries concentration risk even when the demand signal is strong.

For brokers and ISO networks, the broker origination lesson is documentation discipline. Train reps to ask for FAA 8130-3 tags, back-to-birth records, life-limited parts status, and repair station certificates. Set expectations that aviation aftermarket files need a real borrowing base, not a stated inventory value. That reduces declines and improves funder pull-through.

## What funders should build

Build a small business lending product for aviation aftermarket operators with three eligibility gates. First, verify the business license and FAA Part 145 status where applicable. Second, require serial-level inventory reporting and a third-party appraisal or field exam for initial eligibility. Third, set concentration limits by customer, part class, and engine family.

Use monthly borrowing base certificates. Exclude parts without traceable records, parts with expired tags, and parts that depend on a single obsolete airframe. Monitor engine shop visit demand, MRO turnaround times, OEM part lead times, and feedstock prices. Those signals tell you whether the teardown premium is expanding or compressing. They also tell you when to tighten advance rates.

The early teardown cycle is a working capital event for small businesses in the aviation aftermarket. Funders that underwrite component-level collateral and project cash flow will capture viable borrowers. Funders that use airline credit as a proxy will either decline good files or misprice the collateral. Brokers and ISO networks that document part records and revenue quality will move those files through faster. Good credit risk underwriting here starts with the part record, not the airline brand. That is the operator play: finance the parts, verify the records, and monitor the premium.

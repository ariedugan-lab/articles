Shanghai Disneyland crossed 100 million cumulative visitors in 2025. That number alone is not remarkable for a major theme park. What is remarkable is the context: China's consumer pullback, a tightening regulatory environment, and an overall contraction in discretionary spending. The park's performance directly contradicts the narrative that all tourism-adjacent small businesses face the same headwind.

For lenders, this divergence is not a curiosity. It is an underwriting signal that separates high-quality borrowers from those that will struggle when the next demand shock hits. The question is not whether a small business serves tourists. The question is what kind of tourism it serves.

## The Premium Destination Effect

Shanghai Disneyland is a premium destination. The average ticket price, the on-site hotel stays, the merchandise per guest — all are well above the Chinese domestic theme park average. That premium positioning creates a demand buffer. When household budgets tighten, consumers cut the middle-tier experiences first. They do not cut the aspirational trip they saved for six months. This is a behavioral pattern lenders can monetize.

Small businesses that serve premium destinations — luxury hotels, high-end tour operators, premium food-and-beverage suppliers, private transport services, event planners for corporate retreats — exhibit revenue stability that mass-market tourism businesses lack. Their customer base is less price-sensitive. Their payment cycles are longer, but also more predictable. Their loan-to-value ratios should reflect that.

Concrete implication for funders: When underwriting a small business that supplies linens to a four-star hotel chain, factor in whether that hotel is positioned as a budget or premium offering. A premium hotel near a park like Shanghai Disney, even in a down market, maintains occupancy rates 15 to 20 points higher than a mid-tier hotel in the same city. The supplier's receivable aging will follow that curve.

## The Anchor Tenant Dependency Trap

The flip side of the Shanghai Disney story is the thousands of small businesses that do not operate within the premium bubble but depend on the same foot traffic. Restaurants a mile from the park, shuttle operators, souvenir shops outside the gate — these businesses have a different risk profile. They are exposed to the single anchor tenant's success, but they capture none of its pricing power. Their cash flow correlates with visitor count, but not with the park's revenue per guest.

Lenders have traditionally treated all tourism-adjacent borrowers as a single risk pool. The Shanghai Disney data shows that is a mistake. A vendor that holds a direct contract with the park or a premium affiliated hotel has predictable cash flows. A business that relies on spillover traffic has erratic revenue that drops faster when the park runs promotions that keep guests inside the gates longer.

Brokers and ISO networks should segment their tourism pipeline by whether the borrower's revenue is “contract-based” (suppliers, licensed vendors, approved operators) or “spillover-based” (independent restaurants, peripheral retail, unaffiliated transport). The latter group should face tighter advance rates and more frequent covenant checks, especially after a tourism peak like a summer or holiday season.

## A New Underwriting Variable: Demand Stickiness

Demand stickiness measures how much a business's revenue holds when the broader economy softens. For premium destination businesses, stickiness comes from inelastic demand. For mass-market tourism businesses, stickiness comes from volume. The two behave differently under stress.

Shanghai Disneyland’s revenue per visitor has held steady even as total visitor count growth slowed. That indicates premium pricing power. A small business supplier that provides premium packaging to Disney-themed merchandise can pass on cost increases. A general souvenir shop cannot. The underwriting model should integrate a simple proxy for stickiness: the borrower’s average ticket size relative to local GDP per capita. Businesses whose average ticket is above 5% of local monthly income tend to have stickier revenue than those whose ticket is below 1%.

## How to Build the Signal into Origination

Funders can operationalize this signal in three steps.

First, classify tourism-adjacent loans by destination tier. Use a simple rubric: Tier 1 destinations are those with global brand recognition, pricing power, and non-discretionary demand among affluent consumers (Disney parks, luxury resorts, high-end convention centers). Tier 2 destinations are regional attractions with moderate pricing power. Tier 3 is mass-market tourism with high price sensitivity. Assign different debt-service-coverage ratio minimums to each tier.

Second, require borrowers to disclose their revenue concentration to anchor tenants. Any business that derives more than 40% of revenue from a single tourism anchor — a park, a cruise line, a convention — should face a compensating balance or personal guarantee requirement. The Shanghai Disney vendor ecosystem shows that the concentration risk is real, but it is manageable when the anchor has a strong balance sheet itself.

Third, monitor consumer sentiment data for the specific destination, not just the overall market. The Chinese pullback did not affect all attractions equally. Lenders who used aggregated tourism data missed the premium destination resilience. Use foot traffic indices, online booking data, or even credit card volume from the destination's catchment area to refine risk assessments.

## Why This Matters for Brokers and ISO Networks

The next 18 months will produce a widening gap between tourism-adjacent borrowers. Some will thrive on premium demand that shrugs off macro pressure. Others will face margin compression as mass-market tourists trade down. Brokers who can identify the premium-tier borrowers will win better origination volume and lower default rates. ISO networks that segment their tourism pipeline by destination tier will deliver higher-quality leads to funders.

Actively market working capital products to small businesses that service premium destinations. Their need for capital is real — they must invest in inventory, staffing, and upgrades to maintain the premium experience. Their ability to repay is stronger than the cohort you are currently lending to. The Shanghai Disney data is the evidence you need to adjust your underwriting. Use it.

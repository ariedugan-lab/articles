## The Rite-of-Passage Risk

Shaquille O'Neal called the hard fouls on Caitlin Clark a rite of passage. For lenders, that phrase is a risk indicator, not a commentary. When a league's veterans and media figures normalize physical targeting of its biggest star, the revenue that small businesses have built around that star becomes less predictable.

That matters far beyond the court. The WNBA economy now includes sports bars, restaurants, merchandise shops, print-on-demand sellers, event venues, and travel operators. Many of these businesses borrowed against the league's growth. Their cash flow is tied to viewership, ticket sales, and fan spending. The league's tolerance for physical play determines how often Clark appears, how long she stays healthy, and how quickly the momentum compounds or stalls.

## The New Concentration Risk

Standard underwriting already checks customer concentration. A wholesale supplier that relies on one retailer for 40 percent of revenue gets extra scrutiny. Lenders demand diversification. The WNBA adjacent ecosystem now has a similar problem, but the concentration is invisible in most financial statements.

A sports bar in a mid-sized market might show steady weekend sales. That top-line stability hides the fact that 30 percent of weekend revenue comes from WNBA watch parties. A merchandise seller might show a spike in jersey sales. The spike is directly tied to Clark's national television slots. Neither borrower has a single customer at fault. They have a single athlete at fault.

That is not a reason to reject the loan. It is a reason to underwrite the dependence. If a borrower's revenue depends on a player who is being targeted, the lender needs to know what happens when she misses games. The recent officiating controversy makes that scenario more likely, not less.

Shaquille O'Neal's rite of passage framing normalizes the danger. When the public narrative accepts hard fouls as standard, officials may keep calling games the same way. The star absorbs more contact. The risk of injury rises. The revenue that small businesses count on becomes a variable cost.

## Treat It Like Key-Person Risk

Business lenders already understand key-person risk. A medical clinic with one dominant physician is a harder deal. A marketing agency built around one rainmaker gets a lower advance rate. Sports-adjacent small businesses now have the same exposure at the league level.

The borrower is not the athlete. The borrower is the bar, the store, or the event company. But the borrower's cash flow is still tied to one person's availability and performance. Underwriters need to calculate an athlete dependency ratio: the percentage of recent revenue tied to games, broadcasts, or events featuring that athlete.

If that ratio is high, the deal needs a buffer. Options include lower advance amounts, shorter terms, or a reserve against chargebacks. The buffer is not a rejection. It is a recognition that star-driven revenue is volatile revenue.

## How to Underwrite Sports-Adjacent Borrowers

The next step is practical. Funders can use existing payment data to separate event-driven revenue from baseline revenue.

1. Segment daily card volume by game days and non-game days. Compare same-day averages for WNBA regular season, playoffs, and non-WNBA periods.

2. Identify national broadcast effects. A sports bar may spike only when Clark plays on a major network. That spike is not durable if the league changes its media strategy or if Clark misses games.

3. Stress-test for a 30-day absence. If the marquee player sits for six, ten, or fifteen games, can the borrower still cover rent, payroll, and debt service? Run the same test for a league-level disruption such as a referee strike or a rule change that reduces physicality.

4. Monitor officiating policy. The league's response to the flagrant foul debate will change game flow. A crackdown may reduce injuries and stabilize revenue. A continuation of the current tolerance will keep the risk elevated. Lenders should have a source for these updates and adjust portfolio exposure accordingly.

## The Broker and ISO Playbook

Brokers and ISO networks should use this conversation to position themselves as operators, not order-takers.

When a sports bar or merchandise brand applies for working capital, ask about revenue triggers. What drives your busiest weekends? How much of your recent growth is tied to the WNBA season? What happens during the off-season? What happens if Clark is out?

These questions do more than validate repayment capacity. They signal discipline. Borrowers want funders who understand their business. A broker who can discuss athlete dependency and event-driven revenue will win more deals than one who quotes rates.

ISO networks should distribute this framework to their sales teams. Build a short questionnaire for sports-adjacent borrowers. Have reps classify the revenue mix: baseline retail, event-driven, seasonal, and athlete-driven. That classification becomes a negotiation tool. It also protects the network from submitting bad deals to funders.

## The Opportunity Is Real but Not Uniform

None of this argues against financing the women's sports economy. The WNBA's growth is real. The controversy around Clark has produced more media coverage, more ticket demand, and more merchandise sales. That is exactly the kind of momentum that creates working capital needs.

A merchandise seller needs inventory capital before the next playoff run. A sports bar needs equipment upgrades before watch parties peak. An event space needs deposits for viewing parties. These are fundable needs.

The problem is pricing for volatility. Hype-driven revenue is not the same as recurring revenue. Lenders that conflate the two will build a portfolio that defaults when the hype shifts. The physicality debate at the heart of the rite of passage comment is a reminder that the hype can shift quickly.

## The Flagrant Foul in Lending

Lending on unverified revenue quality is the equivalent of a flagrant foul. It looks aggressive in the moment and creates long-term damage. The remedy is not to leave the sports economy. The remedy is to underwrite the concentration, stress test the star exposure, and require the borrower to operate with a margin of safety.

Shaquille O'Neal says the treatment of Clark is part of the game. For lenders, the game has changed. Small businesses are now part of the league's economics. Funders who recognize that physicality is a risk factor will allocate capital more intelligently. Brokers and ISO networks that ask the right questions will build portfolios that survive contact.

The women's sports economy is still a growth vertical. The businesses in it are still fundable. The difference is that the tolerance for targeting a star is now a documented risk. Underwriting must reflect that risk in advance rates, holdbacks, and repayment terms. Treating it as a rite of passage is not a strategy. Treating it as a credit signal is.

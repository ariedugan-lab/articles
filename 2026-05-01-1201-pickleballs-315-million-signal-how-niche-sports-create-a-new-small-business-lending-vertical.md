Apollo Sports Capital and Tom Dundon just placed a $225 million bet on pickleball. The total investment in Pickleball Inc. now stands at $315 million. That is not a hobbyist’s check. It is an institutional signal that niche sports have matured into a genuine asset class.

For small business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks, this shift creates a new lending vertical. The pickleball economy is not just about professional tournaments. It is a web of equipment manufacturers, court builders, retail stores, club operators, event organizers, and hospitality businesses. Each node in that chain requires capital. And each node presents underwriting challenges and opportunities that traditional cash-flow models struggle to capture.

This article breaks down the concrete implications of that $315 million signal. You will learn where the real lending gravity sits, how to evaluate risk in an emerging sport ecosystem, and why ignoring this vertical means leaving money on the table for competitors who move first.

---

### The Pickleball Economy: More Than a Game

Pickleball is the fastest-growing sport in America for the fifth consecutive year. Participation has jumped 223% over the past four years, according to the Sports & Fitness Industry Association. The demographic is broad: retirees, suburban families, corporate leagues, and young professionals. That breadth spreads revenue risk across multiple consumer segments.

But the direct sport itself is not the lending target. The Professional Pickleball Association (PPA) may be capital-rich from this investment, but the small businesses that support the sport are not. Those are the opportunities.

Consider four distinct sub-verticals:

1. **Equipment manufacturers and distributors** – Paddles, balls, nets, bags, and apparel. Most are small-to-midsize companies with seasonal cash flow. They need working capital for raw material purchases, inventory build, and trade credit.

2. **Court builders and surface suppliers** – Converting tennis courts, building dedicated pickleball complexes, and resurfacing existing venues. These are project-based businesses with lumpy revenue and high upfront material costs.

3. **Venue operators and clubs** – Indoor and outdoor facilities offering court time, lessons, leagues, and social events. Monthly membership models produce recurring revenue but require significant upfront capital for construction, lighting, and amenities.

4. **Hospitality and retail tie-ins** – Restaurants, bars, and pro shops located at pickleball venues or in pickleball-heavy neighborhoods. Revenue is tied to foot traffic from players and spectators.

Each of these sub-verticals needs funding. And each behaves differently from a lending perspective.

---

### Why Traditional Underwriting Misses the Mark

Standard cash-flow models look at historical bank statements, tax returns, and profitability ratios. Emerging sports businesses often lack two years of clean financial history. They may have high initial expenses, low near-term margins, and revenue that depends on seasonal spikes or tournament schedules.

That does not make them bad risks. It makes them mispriced by conventional models.

For example, a court builder may report losses in the first year because of heavy equipment purchases and marketing spend. But that same business may have a backlog of signed contracts and deposits from municipalities and HOAs. A lender that only sees trailing twelve months of cash flow will decline a deal that a smarter underwriter, using alternative data like contract value, deposit percentages, and customer concentration, would approve.

Similarly, a pickleball club with a membership model may have predictable monthly recurring revenue (MRR) that standard models discount because the business is less than two years old. The club may also have untapped collateral in court naming rights, future tournament agreements, or sponsorship commitments. A sharp ISO or broker can surface those assets.

### Concrete Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs

#### For Funders

**Opportunity:** Short-term working capital loans to equipment manufacturers ahead of peak season (spring-summer). Revenue-based financing tied to tournament ticket sales for venue operators. Equipment financing for court builders with purchase orders from verified clients.

**Risk:** The sport’s rapid growth attracts new entrants who may not have operational discipline. A funder must differentiate between businesses with strong unit economics and those riding hype. Demand a minimum number of months of transaction data from payment processors, not just bank deposits. Use social media engagement, local permit records, and league participation data as soft signals.

**Action item:** Build a scorecard that weights contract backlog, deposit-to-revenue ratio, and customer concentration. If one client accounts for more than 40% of a borrower’s revenue, require a personal guarantee or collateral attachment.

#### For Brokers

**Opportunity:** Specialize in pickleball-adjacent businesses. Become the go-to broker for court builders in your region. Package multiple funding requests for a single club that needs construction loans, equipment loans, and working capital lines. Cross-sell merchant cash advances (MCA) to pickleball retail stores that process high volumes of small credit card transactions.

**Risk:** Overconcentration. If you fund five court builders and a shift in regulatory zoning halts new construction, your entire pipeline dries up. Diversify within the vertical: fund one equipment maker, one club operator, one event organizer.

**Action item:** Develop a referral partnership with local pickleball clubs, pro shops, and tournament organizers. Offer them a finder’s fee for referring small business owners who need capital. Educate them on the types of funding available so they can identify creditworthy members.

#### For ISO Networks

**Opportunity:** Create a dedicated “sports and recreation” lending program that aggregates demand across your ISO partners. Negotiate volume-based pricing with funders who understand this niche. Provide underwriters with market data on pickleball participation rates, average player spend, and seasonality benchmarks.

**Risk:** Approval rates may be lower than general small business lending without proper training. ISOs that push unqualified leads will burn relationships with funders.

**Action item:** Host a one-hour underwriting workshop for your ISO network focused on emerging sports verticals. Cover alternative data sources like event attendance, league registrations, and social media reviews. Share case studies of wins and losses.

### Underwriting Guidelines for the Pickleball Vertical

1. **Seasonality adjustment** – The sport peaks in warm months. Annualize revenue by at least twelve months to capture both peak and off-peak. Factor in Q1 and Q4 dips.

2. **Asset-based lending for court builders** – Use purchase orders and raw material invoices as collateral. Lend against net asset value of specialized equipment like coating machines and net systems.

3. **Revenue-based financing for clubs** – Link repayment to monthly credit card sales. If a club has 300 members paying $50 per month, that is $180,000 in steady MRR. Lend up to 1x to 1.5x monthly MRR.

4. **Personal credit and industry experience** – Business owners who have operated in the sports, recreation, or construction industries for at least three years show lower default rates. Require a minimum credit score of 650 for unsecured loans.

5. **Geographic concentration risk** – A single club in a pickleball hotbed like Naples, Florida, or Surprise, Arizona, carries different risk than a club in a low-growth market. Overlay county-level participation data from the Sports & Fitness Industry Association.

### The Broader Trend: Niche Sports as a Lending Asset Class

Pickleball is not an isolated case. Pickleball signals the beginning of a broader trend where niche sports generate enough economic gravity to attract institutional capital. Lacrosse, padel, footgolf, and esports are all following similar trajectories. The underlying pattern is the same: a passionate consumer base, low barriers to entry for participants, and a fragmented supply chain of small businesses.

Lenders who develop the capability to underwrite these businesses today will own the vertical tomorrow. Those who wait for three years of profit-and-loss statements will watch the best deals go to competitors who understand that momentum and contract backlog are reliable lead indicators.

### Conclusion

The $315 million investment in Pickleball Inc. is not a sports story. It is a lending story. It signals that institutional money sees oil in the ground. That oil needs drilling equipment, transport infrastructure, and local service providers. Those are small businesses. And small businesses need capital.

Funders, brokers, and ISOs that step into this vertical now will shape the underwriting standards for an entire asset class. The ones that hesitate will be left chasing deals in a standardized, commoditized market built by others.

The question is not whether pickleball is a fad. The question is whether you are positioned to fund the businesses that make the sport possible.

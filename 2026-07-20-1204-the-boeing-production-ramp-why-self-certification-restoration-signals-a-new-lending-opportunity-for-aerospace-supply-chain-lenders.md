The FAA’s decision to once again allow Boeing to self-certify airworthiness on the 737 Max and 787 is more than a regulatory milestone. It is a concrete signal that the aerospace supply chain is about to shift from a period of constrained output to a measured production ramp. For small business lenders, this transition creates a discrete, time-bound opportunity to deploy working capital into a vertical that has been starved of predictable demand for nearly two years.

This is not a repeat of the self-certification risk angle. That story already covered the underwriting trap created when Boeing lost its delegation authority. This story is about what happens when that authority is restored — and how funders, brokers, and ISO networks can position themselves to capture the resulting wave of financing demand.

**The Signal: From Constraint to Ramp**

When the FAA revoked Boeing’s self-certification privileges in early 2024, every delivery became subject to direct government inspection. That bottleneck slashed production rates for the 737 Max from 38 per month to below 20, and the 787 line slowed to a crawl. Small suppliers — machine shops, composite fabricators, avionics assemblers — saw their order books turn erratic. Many drew down revolving lines of credit just to stay alive.

The reinstatement changes that calculus. Boeing can now certify aircraft internally, which compresses the final inspection cycle by weeks. The company has already signaled plans to ramp the 737 Max back to 38 per month by mid-2027, with the 787 targeting 10 per month. For a supply chain that has been operating at two-thirds capacity, that ramp represents a step-change increase in demand for raw materials, subcomponents, and labor.

Small businesses in the aerospace supply chain do not carry excess inventory. They operate lean, often on net-30 or net-60 terms with Boeing while paying their own suppliers on net-15. The moment production schedules increase, these businesses need to finance raw material purchases, overtime wages, and expedited shipping. Their cash conversion cycles stretch, and their existing credit lines — already drawn down during the downturn — will not cover the gap.

This is where small business lenders can step in.

**Concrete Implications for Funders**

Funders that already underwrite manufacturing businesses should update their industry risk models. The aerospace vertical has been classified as high-risk because of Boeing’s production instability. That classification should now shift to moderate-risk with a growth tailwind. The key underwriting adjustment is to recognize that the ramp is not linear. Suppliers will need lump-sum working capital injections to purchase materials for orders that will be delivered 90 to 120 days later.

Funders should consider offering short-duration, inventory-backed loans with a 6-to-12-month term, structured to align with the production acceleration timeline. The collateral is real: aerospace-grade aluminum, titanium, carbon fiber, and certified components that retain value because they are specific to high-demand platforms. Loan-to-value ratios can be set at 50-60% to maintain a safety buffer.

Another implication is concentration risk. Many small suppliers are single-source for specific parts. The ramp creates a near-term revenue surge, but funders must ensure that the borrower’s customer diversification is adequate. A supplier that derives more than 60% of revenue from Boeing is still exposed to any future quality-related production pause. Underwrite for the ramp, but stress-test for a reversion.

**Concrete Implications for Brokers**

Brokers who specialize in manufacturing finance have a clear lead generation opportunity. Aerospace suppliers are a tight-knit community. Many are located in the Pacific Northwest, Southern California, Wichita, and Texas. They attend trade shows like the Farnborough Airshow and NBAA. Brokers can position themselves as the go-to source for ramp financing by building a targeted outreach list of Tier 2 and Tier 3 aerospace manufacturers with 10 to 200 employees.

The messaging should be direct: “Your Boeing orders are about to increase. Do your current credit lines have room to finance the inventory you need to fulfill them?” Most suppliers will answer no. Brokers can then present a menu of working capital products — inventory finance, purchase order finance, asset-based lines — that are specifically tailored to the aerospace production cycle.

Brokers should also educate their ISO networks on the vertical. Many ISOs avoid manufacturing because it feels complex, but aerospace supply chain financing is relatively straightforward. The borrower’s revenue is tied to a known schedule (Boeing’s published delivery targets), and the collateral is tangible. ISOs can be trained to ask the right qualifying questions: How many parts do you supply per month? What is your lead time for raw materials? How much additional working capital do you need to support a 20% increase in output?

**Concrete Implications for ISO Networks**

ISO networks that aggregate small-ticket loan applications should create a dedicated aerospace ramp product filter. Instead of treating all manufacturing loans the same, flag applicants with NAICS codes 336411 (aircraft manufacturing), 336412 (aircraft engine and engine parts), 336413 (other aircraft parts and auxiliary equipment), and 332710 (machine shops). These are the businesses most likely to benefit from the production ramp.

The network can then offer ISOs a pre-approved funding range based on a simple formula: 10-15% of the supplier’s annual revenue, up to $500,000, with an interest rate premium of 300-400 bps over standard term loans to account for the cyclicality. The underwriting data is already available — tax returns, accounts receivable aging, and a signed purchase order from Boeing or a Tier 1 integrator.

ISO networks should also consider partnering with specialized equipment finance companies. The ramp may also require suppliers to purchase additional CNC machines, inspection equipment, or robotics. Bundling working capital with equipment financing creates a sticky product that covers both inventory and capex needs.

**How to Position This Vertically**

The aerospace supply chain lending vertical is not new, but it has been dormant. The FAA reinstatement reopens it with a cleaner risk profile. Lenders that act early can capture a wave of applications before competitors recalibrate. The window is narrow — Boeing’s ramp will plateau by late 2027, and suppliers will adjust their capital structures thereafter.

Key underwriting considerations for this vertical:

- Verify the supplier’s Boeing purchase order or forecast letter. Boeing typically provides suppliers with rolling 6-month production schedules. Use that as the basis for cash flow projections.
- Assess the supplier’s raw material sourcing. Aerospace materials are specialized and often have long lead times. A supplier that cannot secure titanium or specialty alloys will not benefit from the ramp.
- Evaluate the supplier’s labor availability. Many small aerospace manufacturers struggled to retain skilled machinists during the downturn. A ramp that requires a 20% workforce increase may be constrained by hiring timelines. Lenders should ask about overtime capacity and contingent labor plans.
- Monitor Boeing’s own earnings calls and delivery reports. If Boeing’s ramp falters, the domino effect on suppliers will be immediate. Set up automated alerts for any production guidance changes.

**The Bottom Line for Operators**

The FAA’s vote of confidence in Boeing is not just a headline. It is a concrete operational signal that the aerospace supply chain is entering a new phase. Small business lenders that understand the mechanics of production ramps, inventory financing, and vertical risk can deploy capital profitably while supporting a strategically important industry sector.

Funders should update their credit policies for manufacturing. Brokers should build targeted outreach lists. ISO networks should create dedicated product filters. The opportunity is real, time-bound, and quantifiable. Those who move now will secure a competitive advantage before the rest of the market catches on.

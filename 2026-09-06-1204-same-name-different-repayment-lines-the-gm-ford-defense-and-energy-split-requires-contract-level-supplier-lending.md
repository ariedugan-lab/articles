# Same Name, Different Repayment Lines: The GM-Ford Defense and Energy Split Requires Contract-Level Supplier Lending

General Motors and Ford are taking their century-old rivalry into defense procurement and energy storage. For small business lenders, this is not just a corporate strategy news cycle. It is a shift that reshapes how supplier revenue must be underwritten.

GM and Ford are not only adding revenue lines. They are pulling suppliers into contract structures that differ from traditional auto production. A shop that sells to an automaker's existing vehicle program, its military development work and its energy storage business can show the same top customer names in a credit file while carrying three entirely different repayment streams. Lenders that use the same cash-flow lens across all three will misprice risk. Brokers and ISO networks that sell the same daily-debit product across all three will create avoidable failures.

## One borrower, three repayment types

Conventional auto supply revenue still follows release schedules, part shipments and field acceptance. Invoices run to the OEM and are paid after acceptance. These receivables are commercial obligations under a private supply agreement. The main risk is the program lifecycle, model mix and cancellation terms. Collection can be predicted at a portfolio level.

Defense work is different. Many automakers and their suppliers now operate under prime contracts, DFARS terms and stop-work provisions. Invoices are often milestone progress billings. A supplier may need to work for three months before it can submit a compliant invoice. The payment clock begins after the work statement is approved, not after equipment leaves the loading dock. Security controls, cybersecurity requirements and cost accounting standards are not back-office overhead. They are preconditions to getting paid. A contract award that looks like revenue in the news is not an invoice in the general ledger.

Energy storage adds a third type. Suppliers to battery integrators and automaker energy programs make large raw material purchases before a single installation or enclosure ships. Orders may be tied to grid interconnection dates and utility acceptance. Payment often includes retainage until commissioning. The customer can be a developer, an EPC contractor or a project special-purpose vehicle. The end-market buyer may be a utility, but the contract payer is not.

All three can appear as GM or Ford on a company's bank statement. The correct underwriting response is to separate them by the legal payer, the billable event and the amount that can be monetized before payment is collected.

## Contract-level lender controls

Most small business lenders are built to evaluate a company, not a contract. For GM and Ford suppliers, account-level financials are no longer enough. The credit file must answer five questions:

1. What instrument creates the receivable? A commercial purchase order, a defense subcontract, a utility contract, or a project purchase order?
2. What event triggers the invoice? Delivery, milestone completion, commissioning, or owner acceptance.
3. Who is the legal payer? The OEM, the defense prime, the integrator, or the utility.
4. What percent of the receivable is retained or withheld until acceptance?
5. Do federal procurement rules, security requirements or portal terms restrict who can fund the receivable?

The answer determines the advance rate.

A routine auto receivable can support an advance against clean invoice value. A defense progress billing cannot support the same advance unless the funder verifies the milestone status, government audit exposure and assignment rights. If the receivable is due from a prime contractor, a lender should review whether the prime has the right to set off disputed amounts. A storage invoice with 10% retainage should not be fully advanced. Only the post-commission collectible amount is current collateral.

The same logic applies to pre-invoice inventory. Makers of EV parts, defense components and storage enclosures will carry raw material and work-in-process before billing dates. A standard MCA or term loan does not recognize unbilled contract cost as an eligible asset. Yet that unbilled cost is exactly where the liquidity strain will appear. If a small business has two major energy storage orders in one quarter, its account balance can be negative while its order book is solid. Lenders must either structure a separate unbilled-cost facility or adjust amortization to match contract milestones. Treating the borrower as a daily revenue stream will not hold.

## What brokers and ISO networks should do

In this environment, the origination conversation should not begin with how much money the business needs. It should start with what the order looks like and who controls the payment.

Small manufacturers trying to enter GM and Ford's new lines have the same basic story: a big customer asked me to expand, I bought material and need working capital. The correct product depends on the contract vehicle.

If the order traces to a standard OEM program, a working capital loan or MCA against card or payment volume may be suitable. If the order comes from a defense prime, daily ACH repayment against lumpy milestone revenue is a mismatch. Fixed daily debits can empty the account before labor and material costs are funded for the next phase. The business can perform the contract perfectly and still default on the loan.

If the applicant mentions CMMC, DFARS, ITAR, defense prime, milestone billing or utility interconnection, the ISO should take that file out of auto-MCA mode. Those terms are not objections to funding. They are flags for contract-based underwriting.

Funders should build a triage step in their loan origination system that creates a separate queue for contract-heavy files. A defense contract under $500,000 may cost more to review than it yields. Above that threshold, the margin can support specialist review. An energy storage order with a binding EPC contract and shipped goods can be a strong borrowing-base asset if the lender refuses to advance against retention.

## The lending opportunity inside the rivalry

The GM-Ford defense and energy rivalry will not be limited to two car companies overcoming one supply-chain story. The commercial effect will land in the small companies that follow both companies into federal programs and stationary power markets. Those suppliers will become less auto-dependent, not more diversified in the way lenders usually measure. They will exchange one cash-cycle profile for multiple cash-cycle profiles.

Funders that treat those profiles separately will see a durable, specialized lending vertical with less competition. Funders that continue scoring GM and Ford as one homogeneous customer name will either avoid funding the strongest suppliers or underwrite them into defaults. That distinction is the real bottom-line signal from this rivalry.

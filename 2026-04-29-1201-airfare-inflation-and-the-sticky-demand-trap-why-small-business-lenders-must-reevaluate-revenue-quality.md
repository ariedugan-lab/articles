Airlines are raising fares. Travelers keep booking. Everyone calls it pricing power. For small business lenders, that headline hides a deeper trend: the customers of travel-adjacent businesses are sticky, but their margins are not. When the cost base inflates faster than a business can pass it through, revenue growth becomes a misleading signal. This is the sticky demand trap, and it is now a material underwriting concern for any portfolio exposed to small businesses that depend on air travel.

Think about the businesses connected to airline ticket purchases: corporate travel agencies, airport parking operators, shuttle services, tour operators, event planners who book travel, and even local restaurants near terminals. Their top-line revenue often mirrors air travel demand. But their cost structures are hit first by rising fuel surcharges, higher commissions, and increased supplier pricing. The traveler pays more for the ticket, but the intermediary or service provider may not see a proportional rise in their take.

For lenders, the immediate temptation is to look at a business's growing transaction volume or booking revenue and conclude that cash flow is healthy. That assumption can be dangerous in the current environment. Here is what the numbers reveal.

**The Revenue Quality Problem**

When a corporate travel agency processes a $1,000 ticket that once cost $700, its gross revenue jumps 43 percent. But if the agency earns a fixed commission of 5 percent, its actual dollar income only rose from $35 to $50. The revenue line looks strong, but the cost of sale (in this case, the ticket price) consumed more working capital and the margin percentage actually shrank from 5 percent to 5 percent? No, it stayed flat in percentage terms but the absolute dollar margin only grew $15. Meanwhile, the agency's operating expenses – software fees, staff salaries, rent – are climbing at a slower, but still real, rate. The result is a business that appears to be growing but is generating less free cash flow per dollar of revenue.

Standard cash flow underwriting models that use a simple debt service coverage ratio based on EBITDA or net income often miss this nuance. EBITDA may rise because top line grows, but working capital needs also increase. The business must hold more cash to pay airlines faster than it collects from clients. Net working capital days stretch. The company needs more short-term debt just to operate at the same volume.

For small business lenders, this means a borrower with a 1.5x DSCR based on trailing twelve months could slip to 1.1x when you stress-test the cost pass-through lag. That is not a theoretical risk. It is happening now as airlines announce fare hikes every quarter and travelers absorb them with little resistance.

**The Pass-Through Lag**

Travel-related small businesses rarely have the pricing flexibility of major airlines. A tour operator who books flights and hotels months in advance faces a lag between when they quote a price and when they pay the supplier. If fuel surcharges increase between quote and departure, the operator eats the difference. Repeat bookings from loyal customers make it hard to raise prices mid-stream. The demand is sticky – the customer still wants the trip – but the margin vanishes.

Corporate travel agencies face similar constraints. Many work on net terms with clients and pay airlines upfront via credit cards or cash. Rising ticket prices increase the cash gap. The agency must finance those receivables for longer. Lenders who offer revenue-based financing or merchant cash advances that rely on daily debit from processing volume may see higher repayment rates initially, but the business's net cash position erodes.

**What This Means for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs**

- **Funders:** Adjust advance rates downward for businesses where more than 30 percent of revenue is tied to air travel purchases. Require gross margin data, not just top-line revenue. Underwrite based on trailing three-month working capital cycles, not annual averages. Consider sliding repayment structures that match the seasonality of travel peaks and lags.

- **Brokers:** When presenting a travel-dependent borrower to a funding partner, include gross margin trends and receivables aging. The story is not just "demand is up." Show how the business is managing cost inflation. Are they negotiating longer payment terms with airlines? Have they raised service fees? Brokers who can articulate margin resilience will close more deals. Those who gloss over cost risks will see declinations rise.

- **ISO Networks:** Segment your travel vertical. Separate high-ticket corporate travel agencies from local shuttle services. The former face working capital strain; the latter face fuel cost sensitivity but may pass through via surcharges. Develop tailored program parameters for each sub-vertical. Build marketing materials that speak directly to the cash flow pressure travel businesses face. Your network can position itself as the go-to for travel-sector working capital if you demonstrate understanding of these nuances.

**A Concrete Example**

Take a mid-sized corporate travel agency doing $20 million in annual revenue. Average commission rate: 5 percent. Gross profit: $1 million. Airline ticket prices increase 15 percent over six months. Revenue jumps to $23 million – a 15 percent increase – but commission income only reaches $1.15 million. Meanwhile, the agency's operating expenses rise 5 percent due to higher software, compliance, and staff costs. Net profit before interest goes from $500k to $490k. The debt service coverage ratio drops from 1.6x to 1.3x, even though revenue is up. A lender looking only at revenue growth would miss the deterioration.

Now add working capital: before the price increases, the agency had 20 days of receivables and 10 days of payables. After price increases, they still pay airlines in 10 days but clients take 25 days to pay. The cash conversion cycle lengthens from 10 days to 15 days. The company needs additional working capital just to maintain the same volume. A lender that provided a $300,000 working capital line based on the old cycle may find the borrower drawing fully and struggling to repay.

**The Larger Signal**

The fact that travelers keep booking despite higher fares tells us demand is inelastic in the short term. That is good for top-line stability. But it masks margin compression that accumulates over time. For lenders, the signal is clear: revenue growth is not the same as cash flow health. Underwriting must shift from volume-based metrics to margin efficiency and working capital velocity.

This is not a call to avoid the travel vertical. It is a call to fund with precision. The businesses that survive and thrive will be those that can pass through costs, shorten payment cycles, and maintain margins. Lenders who identify those businesses early will capture a loyal, sticky borrower base. Those who treat all travel-adjacent small businesses as one homogeneous group will accumulate hidden risk.

The sticky demand trap is real. It is unfolding now as airlines raise fares and consumers say yes. For small business lenders, the margin story matters more than ever.

GM plans to build more U.S. battery capacity. The Department of Transportation is attacking Ford over China ties. Most battery cells still depend on Chinese raw materials. For small-business lenders, the story is not about which OEM wins the EV race. It is about a new underwriting input: supply-chain provenance.

Provenance is becoming a credit variable. If a borrower's customer can lose eligibility, tax credits, or procurement access because of where minerals, cells, or components originate, then that borrower's revenue quality is conditional. Funders that only look at bank statements and trailing revenue will underprice this risk. Brokers and ISO networks that ask origin questions early will source cleaner deals.

## The lending signal inside the battery buildout

GM's domestic battery push signals that OEMs will bifurcate supply chains: one chain for programs that need domestic content, another for cost-optimized global supply. Ford's China ties are now a political target. That pressure can turn into supplier qualification rules, audit requests, and contract repricing. Small suppliers do not need to be named in a headline to feel it. They need to prove origin, re-certify parts, dual-source, and hold more inventory while customers sort out compliance. That is a working capital event.

The first-order impact hits Tier 2 and Tier 3 auto suppliers, battery testing labs, industrial maintenance firms, logistics providers, and energy-storage installers. The second-order impact reaches electrical contractors, HVAC distributors, and equipment dealers serving data centers and grid storage. Any small business with revenue tied to battery cells or critical minerals has China exposure, even if its own invoices are domestic.

## Origin concentration is the new customer concentration

Most lenders underwrite customer concentration. Few underwrite origin concentration. That is the gap.

Origin concentration measures how much of a borrower's revenue or collateral depends on a single source country, supplier, or mineral pathway. A supplier can have five customers and still be fragile if all five use the same Chinese cathode material. A contractor can have a clean domestic customer list and still be exposed if the batteries it installs are imported and suddenly disqualified from a program.

Underwriting should map both dimensions:

- Revenue by customer and program.
- Bill-of-materials origin for the products that generate that revenue.
- Contract terms that assign compliance risk.
- Availability of alternative suppliers.
- Working capital needed to qualify a second source.

If the borrower cannot answer those questions, the file is not complete. That is not a political test. It is a documentation test. Undocumented origin is a credit risk because it creates binary outcomes: the customer accepts the part, or it does not. There is little middle ground.

## Underwriting checklist for funders

1. Segment revenue by OEM program and origin status. Flag revenue tied to Ford, GM, Stellantis, Tesla, defense, and utility-scale storage. Ask which programs require domestic content.

2. Request origin documentation. Collect supplier declarations, country-of-origin certificates, bills of materials, and domestic-content calculations. If a borrower cannot produce them, price for audit risk or decline.

3. Separate transition capex from ordinary growth. Dual sourcing requires tooling, testing, certification, and deposits. Fund against milestones. Do not advance a lump sum for an unverified supplier qualification.

4. Test collateral quality. Raw material inventory with mixed origin may be difficult to liquidate. Equipment tied to a single battery program may have limited secondary value. Domestic battery equipment can be stronger collateral if it is standard and transferable.

5. Tie repayment to purchase orders, not forecasts. Use historical shipments to the same customer as a baseline. Then adjust for program delays, compliance holds, and customer concentration.

6. Add covenants for origin reporting. Require monthly borrowing base certificates that show inventory and receivables by origin status. Set triggers for DSO increases, inventory days, and customer payment delays.

## Broker and ISO network playbook

Brokers should not pitch this as green energy financing. Pitch it as supply-chain compliance financing. The borrower's problem is not environmental, social, and governance optics. The problem is keeping an OEM contract, qualifying for a tax credit, or getting paid on a purchase order.

Useful products:

- Equipment financing for domestic battery production, testing, and recycling equipment.
- Invoice factoring for Tier 2 suppliers with assignable OEM receivables.
- Working capital lines for certification, dual sourcing, and bridge inventory.
- Purchase-order finance for suppliers that need to fund a qualified domestic order.

ISO networks should train representatives to ask three origin questions on the first call:

1. Which customers require domestic content?
2. Which components or raw materials come from China?
3. What would it cost to qualify a second source?

Those questions qualify the lead and set up the underwriting file. They also reduce submission waste. A broker who sends a battery supplier with no origin documentation is sending a file that will stall.

## Portfolio monitoring triggers

Origin risk changes with policy. Lenders need early warnings, not annual reviews.

Monitor:

- New DOT, Treasury, CBP, or DOE guidance that names a customer, sector, or mineral.
- OEM announcements of domestic battery joint ventures or supplier qualification deadlines.
- Borrower DSO rising from 45 to 70 days on the same customer.
- Inventory days jumping because components cannot be shipped.
- Customer concentration above 30 percent with a China-linked program.
- Requests for payment deferrals tied to certification or audit delays.

A single trigger may not require action. Two triggers should force a re-underwrite.

## Pricing and structure

Clean provenance deserves better pricing. If a borrower has documented domestic content, diversified suppliers, and assignable contracts, funders can offer tighter spreads and higher advance rates. If origin is opaque, structure with reserves, shorter amortization, stronger guarantees, and reporting covenants. Do not decline every borrower with China exposure. Decline borrowers that cannot quantify or document that exposure.

## The bottom line

GM's U.S. battery development and the DOT's attack on Ford are not just political news. They are an underwriting preview. Supply-chain origin is moving from a procurement detail to a credit file item. Funders that build origin audits into origination will price risk more accurately. Brokers and ISO networks that ask the right questions will find better borrowers. The small-business lenders that win the next cycle will treat provenance as a credit metric, not a headline.

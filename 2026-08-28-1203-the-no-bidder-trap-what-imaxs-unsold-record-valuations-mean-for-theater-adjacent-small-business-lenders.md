An asset can generate record cash flow and still be unsellable. IMAX is proving that point in real time. The company's stock is at an all-time high. Box office is breaking records. Management says it is open to a sale. No credible buyer has emerged. For small business lenders, that split is not a Hollywood curiosity. It is a warning about every loan secured by specialized equipment, leasehold improvements, or franchise assets whose resale value depends on a thin buyer pool.

## The IMAX signal: revenue strength is not exit value

IMAX is not a distressed business. Revenue and box office are at record levels, and the stock has never been higher. But a sale is stuck because the list of realistic acquirers is short. Any buyer would need to absorb a capital-intensive platform, manage studio relationships that are already strained, and pay a valuation that reflects record performance. The result is a no-bid situation despite strong economics.

Down the supply chain, the same condition exists in less visible forms. Independent cinemas, premium large-format installers, theater food and beverage vendors, AV equipment suppliers, and cinema service contractors all carry specialized assets whose value depends on a small number of potential buyers. Their cash flows can be strong. Their collateral can be installed and functioning. But if the owner needs to exit, the liquidation market is limited. That is the credit signal that most small business underwriting models miss.

## The buyer-depth blind spot in small business lending

Standard small business loan underwriting starts with cash flow. Then it adds collateral coverage. Then it applies a personal guarantee. What rarely appears in the worksheet is buyer depth. How many qualified buyers exist for the borrower's equipment, lease, or enterprise? For a restaurant, the equipment is generic, but the lease is location-specific. For a cinema-adjacent business, the equipment is custom, and the location is often a second-generation theater with declining attendance patterns. In both cases, the value of collateral is an estimate, not a quote. IMAX makes that distinction visible on a national scale.

The no-bidder trap occurs when a business has both strong performance and a thin acquisition market. Lenders assume that exit value is a function of cash flow. In reality, exit value is a function of who can buy, who is willing to buy, and at what multiple. When the buyer pool shrinks, the multiple compresses even if revenue grows. That compression is delayed, not absent. It surfaces in refinancing, in forced sales, and in estate plans.

## What IMAX teaches about collateral

IMAX's technology has tangible value only where premium theater content is still distributed. The installed base is a form of trapped equity. Replacement cost is high, but a buyer taking over that equipment also takes on the obligation to keep up with format upgrades, content agreements, and maintenance standards. The same dynamic applies to smaller specialized lenders.

Consider a regional cinema service company with $3 million in revenue, a fleet of installation vehicles, custom rigging tools, and a book of maintenance contracts. The company may have strong EBITDA and consistent receivables. It is still difficult to sell because the qualified buyer pool is limited to larger integrators and private investors who understand the exhibition industry. If attendance declines and studios hold back titles, the value of that contract book falls faster than the equipment depreciates. A lender that underwrote to replacement cost will find that the forced-sale discount is the entire equity cushion.

For asset-based lending, this is the most important takeaway. Advance rates should be set by the liquidity of the collateral, not by the borrower's optimism. In a sector with a thin buyer pool, the orderly-market value is closer to liquidation value than to going-concern value.

## What funders must change

Funders that rely on OEM invoices or replacement cost need a forced-sale liquidity score. For every equipment loan, ask: if the borrower defaults in month 18, which buyers will purchase this asset, and what will they pay? If the answer requires more than one transaction in the last 12 months, the recoverable value is a fraction of the financed amount. Add a buyer-depth check to the credit memo. Treat a thin buyer pool as a pricing event and a term event.

SBA lenders should challenge collateral appraisals that use replacement-oriented methodologies. An 85% liquidation value on a custom cinema installation is only as real as the last check a salvage buyer would write. If no buyer data exists, the collateral reserve should be reduced to the most conservative recovery rate in the region. That may make fewer deals work, but it will make the deals that close more defensible.

For equipment finance, the term should match the expected ownership period, not the physical life of the asset. A projector may last 12 years. The average theater ownership period for that equipment may be four years. The difference is not small. A longer term with a thin exit market is a risk transfer from the borrower to the lender.

## What brokers and ISO networks should do

Brokers have the earliest visibility into borrower stress. When packaging an SBA loan, equipment financing, or revenue-based advance for a theater-adjacent company, ask one question: if the owner stopped today, who buys the business? If the borrower cannot name a buyer, the file carries an unspoken risk. That risk belongs in the term sheet.

For MCA brokers, the same logic applies to merchant stability. A merchant with strong daily card volume and no transferable business model is not an asset-backed credit. It is an owner-dependent cash flow loan. That distinction matters in an environment where equipment resale values are uncertain and exit multiples are under pressure. Underwriters should treat these deals as working capital support for a single-purpose enterprise, not as collateralized loans.

ISO networks should use this as a training signal. Originators can ask about buyer depth during intake without making the underwriting process adversarial. The answer reveals a business plan gap. A borrower who says "someone will buy it" is not a stable refinance candidate. A borrower who can name a competitor, a downstream user, or a strategic acquirer is a more credible risk. That conversation changes the quality of the application before it reaches a funder.

## The underwriting change for theater-adjacent small business lending

For theater-adjacent small business lending, the IMAX signal is a reminder to underwrite the industry's consolidation, not its box office. Strong revenue is necessary but not sufficient. The critical variable is the number of exit routes. If a borrower's business depends on one theater chain, one format standard, or one distributor relationship, the lender is not making a small business loan. It is making a subordinated bet on that chain's capital plans.

Monitor the sale process of every major player in the exhibition and premium format space. Every failed sale, or every sale that requires a steep discount, is a data point for collateral recovery rates. Lenders do not need entertainment industry expertise. They need to know one thing: when the top asset in the sector cannot find a buyer, the assets further down the stack are worth less than their revenue numbers suggest.

The no-bidder signal is an early warning. It applies to IMAX today and to specialized equipment lenders tomorrow. Underwrite the buyer pool before you need it. That is the only way to turn an unsellable asset into a disciplined credit decision.

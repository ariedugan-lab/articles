The hard seltzer boom is over. Non-carbonated alcoholic drinks—Surfside, BeatBox, and similar ready-to-drink (RTD) cocktails—are now capturing what the beverage industry calls "share of throat," especially among Gen Z. The shift, reported by CNBC, is not a passing flavor preference. It is a structural change in consumer behavior with direct consequences for the small businesses that manufacture, distribute, and sell these products.

For small business lenders, this trend represents a clear underwriting signal. The businesses that rode the seltzer wave may now face stranded assets, inventory write-downs, and margin compression. At the same time, new entrants in the non-carbonated space present a lending opportunity—but only if underwritten with the right risk controls.

This article breaks down the concrete implications for funders, brokers, and ISO networks, and provides actionable steps to adjust portfolios and capture the emerging vertical.

### The Seltzer Infrastructure Trap

Hard seltzer required specialized production equipment: carbonation tanks, specific canning lines, and dedicated cold-chain logistics. Many small breweries, cideries, and distilleries pivoted heavily into seltzer production during its 2018–2022 peak. Distribution networks optimized for seltzer’s higher case volume, and retailers (bars, restaurants, convenience stores) allocated shelf space and cooler capacity accordingly.

Now that consumer demand is rotating toward still, non-carbonated RTDs, that infrastructure risks becoming underutilized. A small brewery that invested $500,000 in carbonation lines and packaging equipment cannot easily repurpose those assets for non-carbonated production. The result: depreciation accelerates, debt service coverage ratios erode, and working capital cycles lengthen.

**Concrete implication for funders:** Review your portfolio for any borrower whose revenue is more than 30% concentrated in seltzer or carbonated beverage production. Run a sensitivity analysis assuming a 20% volume decline over the next 12 months. Borrowers with high fixed-cost leverage and limited product diversification will show margin compression that standard cash flow models may miss.

### Inventory Risk on the Retail Side

Retailers—bars, restaurants, and liquor stores—are also exposed. Seltzer inventory that moved quickly two years ago is now sitting on shelves longer. Slower turnover means less cash available for new product buys, which can create a working capital crunch.

A bar that devoted 15% of its beverage SKUs to seltzer may now see those SKUs contribute only 5% of sales. The capital tied up in that inventory is effectively idle. For lenders using daily credit card processing data, the signal shows up as declining average ticket size or increasing chargeback rates on beverage-heavy merchants.

**Concrete implication for brokers:** When advising clients in the hospitality space, flag seltzer inventory levels as a risk factor. Recommend they reallocate shelf space and marketing spend toward non-carbonated alternatives to improve inventory turnover. This creates an opportunity to offer working capital products specifically tied to inventory transition.

### The Gen Z Trend Velocity Problem

Gen Z is the primary driver of this shift. Their preferences are more volatile than previous generations, influenced by social media, brand authenticity, and novelty. That volatility is a direct underwriting challenge: a borrower that thrives on one trend may struggle to adapt to the next.

Standard underwriting models look at historical cash flow. But historical cash flow from a seltzer-heavy business may not predict future performance if the trend has peaked. Lenders need a forward-looking component that tracks consumer trend velocity.

**Concrete implication for ISO networks:** Train your ISOs to ask borrowers about product mix diversification and trend exposure. A borrower who can articulate how they are responding to the seltzer decline—by introducing non-carbonated lines, adjusting pricing, or retooling equipment—is a better credit risk than one who is simply hoping demand returns.

### Underwriting Adjustments for the New Vertical

Non-carbonated RTDs are not risk-free. They face intense competition from large beverage conglomerates, thinner margins due to higher ingredient costs, and the same trend volatility that killed seltzer. However, they also offer lower capital requirements (no carbonation equipment), faster production cycles, and alignment with current consumer demand.

For lenders willing to originate into this space, the key adjustments are:

- **Use alternative data.** Track Nielsen and IRI scan data for the borrower’s specific product categories. Social media sentiment scores (via tools like Brandwatch or Talkwalker) can indicate demand direction weeks before it shows up in financial statements.
- **Shorten advance rates.** For manufacturers pivoting to non-carbonated lines, consider a lower advance rate (e.g., 60–70% of accounts receivable vs. 80–85%) until they demonstrate consistent demand.
- **Require a diversification covenant.** For any beverage-related borrower over $500,000 in debt, require that no single product category exceeds 40% of revenue. This protects against the next trend shift.

### Portfolio Stress-Testing Playbook

Funders with aggregated exposure to beverage-related small businesses should run a targeted stress test. Use the following assumptions:

- Hard seltzer demand declines another 15–25% over the next 18 months.
- Non-carbonated RTD demand grows 20–30% but attracts intense competition from large brands, compressing margins for small producers by 5–10%.
- Retailers and bars that serve these products face a 10–15% drag on same-store sales if they fail to adapt quickly.

For each borrower in the portfolio, calculate the impact on debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). If a borrower’s DSCR drops below 1.2x under the stress scenario, flag for proactive monitoring or restructuring.

**Concrete implication for funders:** Consider building a "trend risk score" for each borrower in consumer-driven sectors. Weight it by how much of revenue comes from categories that have shown a 20%+ swing in consumer preference over the past three years. This is not a one-time adjustment—it should be refreshed quarterly.

### Opportunity for Brokers and ISOs

Every trend shift creates a lending gap. Small businesses that need capital to retool, pivot, or launch new products often cannot wait for traditional bank loans. Brokers and ISOs who understand this specific vertical can originate deals that are both high-velocity and defensibly underwritten.

- **Target non-carbonated producers.** Surfside and BeatBox are scaling fast, but so are dozens of regional brands. These companies need working capital for ingredient procurement, packaging, and distribution. They often have strong revenue growth but thin credit files.
- **Focus on distribution.** Local distributors and wholesalers who are adding non-carbonated RTDs to their catalogs need financing for expanded warehouse capacity and fleet upgrades.
- **Offer merchant cash advances to retailers.** A bar that wants to refresh its drink menu can use a lump-sum cash advance to buy inventory and then repay out of future sales. The repayment structure aligns with the seasonal nature of the beverage business.

### The Bottom Line

The shift from seltzer to non-carbonated drinks is not a niche story. It is a template for how consumer volatility creates both risk and opportunity in small business lending. Funders who ignore product-category concentration will see defaults spike in the next 12–18 months. Brokers and ISOs who educate their clients and align capital with trend-responsive businesses will capture a growing origination vertical.

The key is to move beyond static underwriting. Track the data. Question the trend. And structure capital to match the speed of the market—not the speed of a historical spreadsheet.

Eli Lilly’s top dealmaker recently signaled the company will use its GLP-1 windfall to fund acquisitions that push into new therapeutic areas. For small business lenders, this is not a distant corporate finance story. It is a direct underwriting signal that touches hundreds of small contract manufacturers, lab equipment suppliers, packaging operations, and specialty logistics providers that depend on Big Pharma procurement.

When the most valuable pharmaceutical company in the world shifts its M&A strategy, the entire supplier ecosystem re-ratchets. Small businesses that have built revenue models around Lilly’s existing supply chains face both upside and downside. Lenders, brokers, and ISO networks that fail to update their credit models will misprice risk — either missing high-quality borrowers or funding businesses about to lose their largest customer to a vertical integration play.

**The M&A Multiplier Effect**

Eli Lilly’s GLP-1 revenue has created a war chest of cash and stock. The company has publicly stated it will use that capital to broaden its pipeline through acquisitions rather than rely solely on internal R&D. That means Lilly is actively hunting for smaller biotechs, innovative drug platforms, and even adjacent manufacturing capabilities.

For every acquisition, there is a supply chain ripple. When Lilly buys a biotech startup, that startup’s existing suppliers — contract research organizations, custom synthesis labs, packaging houses — see their contracts renegotiated, consolidated, or terminated. Conversely, if Lilly decides to build rather than buy, it will pour capital into new facilities, which creates demand for construction, equipment, and logistics vendors.

Either way, the small businesses in the pharma supply chain face a binary outcome: a demand surge if Lilly expands its partner network, or a cliff if Lilly internalizes the work.

**Three Concrete Underwriting Signals**

**1. Single-Customer Concentration Risk Escalates**

Many small pharma suppliers derive 40–60% of revenue from one or two large clients. In a stable procurement environment, that concentration may be acceptable if the client is creditworthy and the relationship is long-term. But Lilly’s M&A ambitions change that calculus. A supplier to a Lilly acquisition target might see its contract voided post-close. A direct Lilly supplier might be favored and scaled — or replaced by a newly acquired in-house operation.

Lenders must now assess the probability of M&A disruption for any small business whose top client is a Big Pharma company. That requires tracking M&A announcements, reading earning calls for supply chain language, and stress-testing cash flows under a 50% revenue decline scenario within 12 months.

**2. Lab Equipment and Specialty Logistics Become a New Lending Vertical**

Lilly’s expansion will require new laboratory space, analytical equipment, cold chain logistics, and specialized packaging — especially for injectable GLP-1 drugs and future biologics. Small businesses that provide these services are not traditional manufacturers. They often have lumpy revenue tied to project-based contracts, high capital expenditure needs for equipment, and long R&D cycles.

Standard cash-flow underwriting fails here. Funders need to evaluate the order pipeline, contract duration, and the buyer’s financial health. A small lab equipment supplier that just landed a three-year contract with a Lilly subsidiary may be a better credit risk than a packaging firm with 30 small clients — but only if the contract has non-cancellation clauses and the supplier has diversified its buyer base.

**3. Biotech Acquisition Targets Create a New Borrower Profile**

Eli Lilly is not the only pharma giant hunting. Pfizer, Novartis, and others are also using cash reserves to acquire innovative small biotechs. The founders and early employees of those biotechs often own equity that will cash out. Some will use that liquidity to start new businesses — and they need capital. Others will stay on as executives and require working capital lines for the combined entity.

Lenders should prepare for a wave of high-net-worth entrepreneurs with concentrated equity wealth seeking SBA loans, lines of credit, or term loans for new ventures. Underwriting must account for the liquidity event timing, lock-up periods, and tax implications. A founder who received $10 million in Lilly stock may have a strong personal balance sheet but no current cash flow — a situation that demands a different credit approach than a standard small business loan.

**What Brokers and ISOs Should Watch For**

Brokers and ISO networks are the front line for these shifts. Here are specific actions to take:

- **Segment by NAICS code.** Focus on 325412 (pharmaceutical preparation manufacturing), 334516 (analytical laboratory instrument manufacturing), and 423450 (medical equipment wholesaling). These sub-sectors will see the most M&A volatility.
- **Ask the right questions.** When a pharma supplier applies for funding, ask: “Who are your top three customers? What percentage of revenue does each represent? Have any of your customers been acquired or merged in the last 18 months? Do your contracts include change-of-control clauses?”
- **Monitor Lilly’s 8-Ks.** Eli Lilly files public disclosures about material acquisitions. Every time a new deal closes, brokers should re-evaluate every small business in their portfolio that does business with Lilly or its targets.
- **Look for capital expenditure spikes.** Small suppliers that suddenly purchase new equipment or lease larger facilities are often reacting to a large new contract. That contract could be from a Big Pharma buyer — verify before proceeding.

**How to Adjust Underwriting Models**

Funders that want to capture the opportunity without taking on excessive risk should recalibrate their models along these lines:

- **Add an M&A risk score.** For any small business in the pharma supply chain, assign a score based on: size of largest customer (over 30% of revenue = higher risk), customer M&A activity in prior 12 months (yes = higher risk), and presence of change-of-control clauses in contracts (yes = lower risk).
- **Shift from trailing 12-month cash flow to forward-looking order book analysis.** Request copies of signed contracts and purchase orders. Validate that the buyer has a history of renewing or paying on time.
- **Require personal guarantees from founders with liquidity events.** If the borrower is a former biotech founder who just cashed out, use that liquid asset as collateral but also verify they are not over-leveraged from personal spending.
- **Build a pharma-specific watchlist.** Track the top 20 pharma companies by R&D spending and M&A activity. Cross-reference your portfolio against their supplier lists. Flag any account that shows high correlation.

**The Bottom Line**

Eli Lilly’s M&A push is a leading indicator for a broader trend. Pharma giants are using pandemic revenues and GLP-1 windfalls to reshape their pipelines through acquisition. This creates a two-sided underwriting challenge: surge demand for some small businesses and sudden revenue loss for others. Lenders who treat the pharma supply chain as a monolithic, stable industry will misprice risk. Those who build M&A awareness into their credit models — and work closely with brokers to surface real-time supply chain signals — will capture a high-quality lending vertical that is only beginning to expand.

The window for first-mover advantage is tight. As more capital flows into pharma M&A, the supplier ecosystem will consolidate. Smart underwriting today will separate the funders who thrive from those who absorb the losses.

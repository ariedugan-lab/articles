# The Liquid Rule Rollback Is an Inventory Shock for Airport Concession Lenders

For two decades, airport concession economics have quietly depended on a security rule. The TSA liquids ban forced passengers to dump water, toiletries, and beverages before screening. Post-security retailers captured that demand. Now the U.S. is rolling back some 9/11-era restrictions, including liquids and gate access. That is not a travel story. It is a point-of-sale story for small business lenders.

The rollback changes where passengers spend, what they buy, and how much time they have to buy it. Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that treat airport-adjacent businesses as one travel bucket will misprice risk. The better move is to isolate forced-purchase revenue from dwell-time revenue and underwrite each separately.

## The forced-purchase revenue trap

Airport concessionaires have benefited from a captive audience. A passenger who cannot bring a bottle of water through security buys one at a post-security newsstand. A traveler who forgot toothpaste buys a travel-size tube at a terminal convenience store. Those sales are not loyalty-driven. They are friction-driven. The liquid rollback weakens that friction.

The impact will not be uniform. A terminal newsstand with a beverage cooler near the checkpoint may see a 10 to 20 percent decline in bottled water, soda, and travel-size toiletry sales. A grab-and-go food operator may hold steady if passengers still buy meals. A full-service restaurant or bar may benefit if gate access expansion brings more non-ticketed visitors into the terminal. A duty-free retailer may lose travel-size sales but gain premium cosmetics and liquor if international passengers respond to different rules.

Lenders need SKU-level context. General merchant statements show total card volume. They do not show whether revenue is water, sandwiches, or souvenirs. Brokers should ask borrowers for product mix, vendor invoices, and point-of-sale category reports. Funders should stress-test cash flow against a 15 percent decline in impulse beverages and travel-size health and beauty items. If the borrower cannot service debt after that haircut, the advance rate is too high.

## Gate access changes the traffic mix

The rollback also touches gate access. If airports allow more non-ticketed visitors past security, terminal foot traffic changes. Concessionaires may see more local diners, meet-and-greet groups, and airport employees on break. That can lift average ticket for restaurants, bars, and retail. It can also raise operating costs for restrooms, cleaning, and crowd management. Landlords may reprice leases or adjust minimum annual guarantees.

For small business lenders, this is a lease-level underwriting problem. A concessionaire with a percentage-rent lease shares upside with the airport. A concessionaire with a high minimum annual guarantee carries fixed rent risk even if sales fall. Funders should read the concession agreement before pricing. Brokers should flag clauses that allow the airport to change terminal layouts, move storefronts, or recapture space. ISO networks should segment airport concession leads by lease type, not just by annual revenue.

## Off-airport businesses get a different signal

The liquid rollback does not only affect terminal retailers. It shifts demand to off-airport channels. Travelers who can carry liquids may buy larger toiletries, beverages, and snacks at grocery stores, big-box retailers, and convenience stores before arriving at the airport. That is positive for off-airport convenience and grocery operators near major airports. It is negative for airport newsstands that relied on emergency purchases.

Off-airport parking, shuttle services, and airport hotels may benefit if security friction falls. Faster screening can reduce passenger arrival buffers. Some travelers will arrive later and spend less time in the terminal. Others will use saved time for a meal or retail stop. The net effect depends on departure mix, international versus domestic, and airport policy. Funders should not assume travel volume equals concession revenue. Revenue quality depends on where the friction sits.

## The working capital event

The rollback creates a working capital event for small businesses that need to reconfigure inventory and store formats. Airport concessionaires may need to reduce travel-size SKUs, add local gifts, expand fresh food, or install self-serve beverage stations. Off-airport retailers may need to stock larger liquids, travel accessories, and grab-and-go meals. Both need capital for inventory, fixtures, POS upgrades, and signage.

This is a natural fit for working capital loans, lines of credit, and equipment financing. Funders can structure advances against verified card receipts, but they should tie repayment to the post-rollback revenue mix, not the pre-rollback history. A merchant cash advance based on 2025 volume may overstate 2026 cash flow if the borrower sold mostly bottled water in a post-security kiosk.

Brokers should position the financing as a transition tool. Ask the borrower what percentage of revenue comes from items passengers can now carry through security. Ask how the airport plans to handle gate access. Ask whether the lease allows the operator to change product mix. The answers determine whether the business needs growth capital or a workout plan.

## How to adjust underwriting now

Use a friction-adjusted revenue model. Start with trailing 12-month sales. Separate forced-purchase categories from destination categories. Forced-purchase categories include bottled water, soda, travel-size toiletries, and forgotten essentials. Destination categories include local food, gifts, electronics, and sit-down dining. Apply a higher volatility factor to forced-purchase revenue. Then layer in traffic assumptions from gate access policy.

Check the lease. Minimum annual guarantees turn a sales decline into a fixed obligations problem. Percentage rent leases give more breathing room. Subleases inside a terminal may have different risk than direct airport leases. A small business with a direct lease and high MAG needs more equity or a lower advance.

Monitor vendor concentration. If a concessionaire depends on one beverage distributor or one travel-size supplier, the rollback can disrupt pricing and returns. Lenders should look at inventory turnover and supplier terms. Brokers should collect accounts payable aging and vendor references. ISO networks should build a checklist for airport concession deals that includes lease type, SKU mix, and terminal access policy.

Watch for second-order effects. Reduced security restrictions may lower demand for travel-size packaging, security bins, and some staffing services. It may raise demand for refillable bottles, larger toiletries, and off-airport retail. Funders with exposure to packaging suppliers or airport service contractors should review concentration risk.

## The operator takeaway

The 9/11 security rollback is not a reason to avoid travel-adjacent small business lending. It is a reason to stop underwriting them as a single category. The liquid rule and gate access changes redistribute revenue across the travel chain. Post-security impulse sellers face a revenue-quality test. Off-airport retailers face a demand lift. Restaurants and destination retail may gain from new terminal visitors. Lenders that separate forced-purchase revenue from dwell-time revenue will price risk better than those that rely on gross sales alone.

For funders, the play is selective growth. For brokers, it is a new discovery question. For ISO networks, it is a vertical campaign with clear qualification criteria. The businesses that need capital to adapt are not distressed by default. They are transitioning. The lenders that understand the transition will win the good deals and avoid the bad ones.

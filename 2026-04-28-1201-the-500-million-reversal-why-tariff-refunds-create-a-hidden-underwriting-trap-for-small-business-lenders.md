## The $500 Million Reversal: Why Tariff Refunds Create a Hidden Underwriting Trap for Small Business Lenders

General Motors just dropped a surprise on Wall Street. First-quarter 2026 earnings beat expectations, and the automaker raised its full-year guidance. The headline number that caught every lender's eye: a $500 million tariff refund. That cash is real. It flows down the supply chain. But for every funder, broker, and ISO underwriting small auto suppliers, this kind of lumpy, non-recurring income is not a blessing. It is a distortion waiting to blow up a portfolio.

### The Mechanics of a Tariff Refund

Tariff refunds happen when a company pays duties on imported goods and later proves they were re-exported or used in a way that qualifies for a drawback. GM's $500 million is likely tied to parts or finished vehicles that crossed borders, paid a tariff, and then left again. The refund is a one-time cash injection. It does not reflect underlying profit margin, operational efficiency, or repeatable earnings.

When GM receives that refund, it strengthens the balance sheet. That strength can trickle down: suppliers get paid faster, orders may increase, or payment terms might tighten. But the refund itself is a single event. It has no predictive value for the next quarter.

### Why This Is an Underwriting Trap

Most small business lenders rely on cash flow models that smooth revenue over 3 to 6 months of bank statements. When a supplier receives a large, irregular deposit from a customer like GM, that deposit looks like revenue. It inflates average daily balances, debt service coverage ratios, and repayment capacity scores.

Here is the concrete risk:

- **Inflated cash flow metrics**: A $200,000 refund passed through to a small supplier increases that month's deposits by 40% or more. A lender using trailing 3-month averages will see a healthier picture than reality.
- **Masked underlying weakness**: The refund can hide declining sales, rising input costs, or delayed receivables. A supplier that was barely breaking even suddenly looks like a credit-worthy candidate.
- **False seasonal patterns**: Tariff refunds often come in large, irregular lumps. Lenders who rely on historical seasonal trends will misclassify the influx as a normal uptick and adjust advance rates upward.
- **No repeatability**: Once the refund is consumed, the cash flow snaps back. If the lender funded against that inflated base, the borrower may struggle to make payments six months later.

### The Broader Trade Volatility Effect

GM's refund is not an isolated incident. It is a signal of a broader environment where trade policy creates unpredictable cash inflows. Tariffs, exemptions, refunds, and retaliatory measures generate a cycle of lumpy payments. Small businesses in the auto supply chain are uniquely exposed because they operate on thin margins and tight just-in-time inventory cycles.

Lenders who treat these events as noise rather than risk are making a mistake. Every tariff refund, duty drawback, or trade credit insurance payout introduces a non-recurring element that distorts the true operating cash flow.

### Concrete Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs

**For funders underwriting direct to suppliers:**

- Flag any single deposit line item exceeding 25% of monthly revenue. Do not count it as operating income. Require the borrower to provide a letter from their customer or customs broker explaining the source.
- Adjust underwriting models to exclude non-recurring deposits. Create a separate line item for "extraordinary cash inflows" and apply a 0% weight to available cash flow.
- Re-underwrite existing clients who recently received large supplier payments from GM or other major OEMs. The refund may have inflated their recent three-month statements.

**For brokers originating auto supply chain deals:**

- Educate your borrower before they apply. If a supplier received a tariff refund, tell them to exclude it from their cash flow summary. Present clean numbers directly to the funder. This builds trust and reduces the chance of a decline or clawback.
- Look for opportunities to offer bridge financing against the refund itself. Some suppliers may need working capital while waiting for GM to pass through the refund. This is a short-term, secured product that carries lower risk than general working capital.

**For ISOs monitoring their agent network:**

- Distribute a one-pager on tariff refunds and non-recurring income. Many originators do not understand why a big deposit can be a red flag. Give them the tools to coach borrowers.
- Flag any deal where the business has a significant customer in the automotive supply chain. Ask the agent to confirm whether recent deposits include any tariff-related payments.

### How to Underwrite Around the Trap

The solution is not to avoid the auto supply chain. It is to build a underwriting protocol that treats tariff refunds as a known variable. Here is a practical framework:

1. **Three months of bank statements plus a signed affidavit** from the borrower listing any deposits that are not from sales of goods or services. Require explanation for deposits over 20% of monthly turnover.
2. **Segment by customer concentration**. If GM or another OEM represents more than 40% of a supplier's revenue, the risk of lumpy cash flows increases. Demand a deeper review of the supplier's credit terms and payment history with that customer.
3. **Cash flow normalization formula**: Subtract from total deposits any amounts identified as tariff refunds, tax rebates, insurance settlements, or one-time customer bonuses. Then recalculate debt service coverage ratio and advance rate.
4. **Contractual covenants** that require the borrower to notify the lender within 10 days of receiving any non-recurring payment over $50,000. Yes, this is invasive. Yes, it protects both sides.

### The Strategic Opportunity

This is not just a warning. It is a market gap. Most lenders will overlook the distortion. They will approve deals based on inflated cash flow, suffer defaults when the refund runs out, and then pull back from the sector. The lender that builds the discipline to identify, exclude, and price for non-recurring income can offer faster approvals and better terms to suppliers who have clean, repeatable cash flow.

That is the real edge. In a market where tariff policy shifts unpredictably, the ability to separate permanent cash flow from temporary liquidity is a durable competitive advantage.

### Bottom Line for Operators

GM's $500 million refund is not a story about GM. It is a story about every small parts supplier that receives a piece of that money and then shows up on your desk looking for a loan. The numbers will look better than the business is. The loan will close faster. And the repayment will break unless you built the underwriting to handle the reversal.

Do not chase the inflated number. Underwrite the normalized cash flow. That is how you avoid the trap and capture the real opportunity in the auto supply chain.

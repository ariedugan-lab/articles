Roger Goodell’s international long game is not a football story for lenders. It is a cash-flow story. The NFL’s first regular-season game in Australia creates a concentrated demand event for hotels, bars, restaurants, transportation operators, event staffers, security firms, food distributors, merchandise sellers, and production vendors. Those businesses need working capital before the event and often wait weeks after the final whistle to get paid.

For funders, brokers, and ISO networks, the signal is not “lend to sports bars.” The signal is that international sports expansion creates a repeatable pipeline of short-duration working capital demand. The underwriting mistake is treating event-driven revenue as if it were recurring. The opportunity is building a credit process that separates baseline cash flow from event cash flow.

## The event is one weekend. The cash cycle is 90 days.

A regular-season NFL game in Australia pulls fans, media, sponsors, and team operations into a host city. The economic activity clusters around a few days. But the small businesses serving that activity do not get paid on game day.

A caterer buys inventory and schedules staff before the game. A temp staffing agency pays workers weekly while waiting on a corporate client. A merchandise distributor funds inventory and freight. A bus operator adds vehicles and drivers. A security contractor fronts payroll and licensing. A restaurant adds walk-in coolers, extra staff, and extended hours. Each has a working capital gap.

If a funder underwrites those businesses using trailing 90-day deposits that include the event spike, it will overstate sustainable revenue. A merchant cash advance with a fixed daily holdback can choke the business after the event when deposits normalize. A term loan sized on peak month revenue can create a payment mismatch that shows up as delinquency two quarters later.

The fix is not to avoid event-driven businesses. It is to underwrite the event separately from the baseline.

## Underwrite the event, not the zip code

Event-driven underwriting starts with four questions.

First, what is the contract? Funders should look for signed catering agreements, staffing contracts, security scopes, logistics purchase orders, hotel block confirmations, sponsorship commitments, or venue vendor agreements. Speculative inventory is not the same as a contracted order. A merchandise seller with a league-licensed product line and a venue allocation is stronger than a pop-up stand with no contract.

Second, what is baseline revenue? Strip out the event month. Calculate debt service coverage against the prior six to twelve months of normalized deposits. If the business cannot service a base payment without the event, the repayment schedule is wrong.

Third, when does the money settle? Corporate and agency payables often net 30 to 90 days. International events add cross-border payment delays, FX conversion, and banking cut-off times. A vendor invoicing an Australian entity in AUD and getting paid in USD needs a cash-flow schedule that reflects the float. Funders should verify who pays, in what currency, and under what terms. Brokers should collect the settlement timeline before submitting the file.

Fourth, is the event repeatable? A single game is a one-time pulse. A vendor that serves multiple events, leagues, concerts, and conferences has a portfolio. A staffing agency with contracts across the NFL, rugby, cricket, and music festivals is a better risk than a single-event merch operator. The credit decision should reflect concentration.

## Product design for event-driven SMBs

Standard daily ACH is a poor fit for event cash flow. Funders can use event-triggered repayment structures.

A seasonal term loan can carry lower payments during pre-event months and larger payments after settlement. A revenue-based financing facility can set a holdback percentage that steps down after the event and reconciles against actual receipts. A merchant cash advance can use a lower initial holdback with an event-date true-up. A line of credit can fund inventory and payroll pre-event, then convert to a term loan after receivables clear.

Invoice factoring can work when the receivable is creditworthy. A contract with a league, a major sponsor, a municipality, or a venue management company is not the same as a receivable from a local promoter. Funders should price the obligor, not just the event.

For cross-border deals, add operational checks. Confirm the legal entity, tax registration, and bank account. Run sanctions and AML screening. Understand withholding tax and currency controls. Do not promise same-day funding on international event receivables.

## What brokers and ISO networks should do

Brokers and ISO networks sit closest to the demand. They can package event-driven files in a way that funders can underwrite.

Collect the event calendar. Identify the event date, contract value, payment terms, and baseline revenue. Submit contracts alongside bank statements. Flag whether the event is annual, one-off, or part of a series. Explain how the business handled prior events. If the owner has serviced a similar event in another city, get the settlement records.

Do not submit an event spike as a standard MCA file and hope the funder misses the timing risk. That creates declines, renewals that fail, and chargebacks. Instead, position the deal as event-triggered working capital. Ask for a repayment schedule that matches settlement.

ISO networks can build vertical knowledge. Create a list of event vendors by category: temporary staffing, security, AV, catering, transportation, cleaning, merchandise, logistics. Share underwriting notes across the network. Track which funders actually close event-driven deals and which ones reprice or stall at closing.

## What funders should change in credit policy

Funders need an event-revenue schedule in the credit memo. It should show baseline revenue, event incremental revenue, contract evidence, settlement timing, and concentration. The schedule should drive the repayment structure.

Set concentration limits. Cap exposure to a single event, venue, or promoter. Stress test for cancellation, delay, lower attendance, weather, labor shortages, and currency moves. Underwrite to a conservative percentage of projected incremental revenue, not the full estimate.

Use data where available. POS integrations, ticketing platforms, hotel booking systems, and payroll providers can verify event-driven activity. For international deals, monitor payment rails and FX. Build reserves for chargebacks, refunds, and disputed invoices.

## The long game for lenders

The NFL is not stopping at Australia. Leagues and rights holders are building international schedules because media rights, sponsorship, and fan data compound over years. That means more host cities, more event vendors, and more short-duration working capital cycles. The same pattern applies to rugby, cricket, Formula 1, concerts, the World Cup, and the Olympics.

The lenders that win this vertical will not be the ones with the lowest rate. They will be the ones that understand event timing. They will separate baseline cash flow from event cash flow. They will align repayment with settlement. They will treat contracts as underwriting evidence. They will train brokers and ISO networks to ask four questions: What is the event? What is the contract? When is the money paid? What is the baseline?

Goodell is playing the international long game. Small business lenders need to play the cash-flow game. The next international fixture will create another cluster of SMB working capital demand. Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that build event-triggered underwriting now will be ready to fund it without repeating the same revenue-quality mistakes.

Broadway’s 2025-26 season grossed $1.9 billion, a record. Ticket sales surged 12% year-over-year, and Sunday’s Tony Awards will likely amplify demand for winning shows. This is not an isolated spike. It is the latest data point in a multi-year shift: consumers are prioritizing experiences over goods.

For small business lenders, this shift rewrites the underwriting playbook. The businesses that serve the experience economy—venues, event production, hospitality, transportation, and specialty retail—do not generate stable monthly cash flows. Their revenue is lumpy, seasonal, and tied to specific events, tours, and cultural moments. Standard underwriting models that average monthly bank deposits will systematically misprice risk in this vertical.

This article lays out the concrete implications for funders, brokers, and ISO networks. It explains why the experience economy demands a separate underwriting framework and how to build one without adding complexity.

**The Signal in Broadway’s Record**

Broadway is a concentrated proxy for the broader live entertainment sector. The industry supports over 97,000 jobs in New York alone and generates $1.7 billion in direct spending on hotels, restaurants, and parking per season. When a show like *The Outsiders* wins Best Musical, ticket sales for that production can double within 48 hours. For small businesses that supply costumes, lighting, catering, or marketing services, a single Tony win can compress 20% of annual revenue into two weeks.

That is not a risk you can capture with a trailing 3-month cash flow statement. A business that looked stable in January may have had zero revenue from a hit show until April. Then April alone covers half the year’s overhead. Standard models flag that as a cash flow gap and decline the loan. Yet the business is viable—its revenue is just misaligned with the underwriting calendar.

**Why This Matters for Funders**

Funders who rely on bank statement analysis or daily revenue data see volatility and assume risk. They apply higher reserve requirements or lower advance rates, pushing experiential businesses to alternative lenders with higher costs. That is a missed margin opportunity. The actual risk is lower than the volatility suggests, provided the lender can assess the revenue pipeline.

Three underwriting adjustments are required:

1. **Revenue averaging over a rolling 12-month window.** A single peak month can mask seasonal lows. The annual picture shows the true capacity to service debt. Advance rates should be calculated on the trailing 12-month average, not the last 3 or 6 months.

2. **Event-specific cash flow mapping.** For a business that supplies 10 Broadway shows, map each show’s run, expected ticket demand, and Tony Award impact. Use historical data from similar productions to build a revenue probability curve. Then set repayment schedules that align with expected cash inflows—not the first of every month.

3. **Covenant flexibility for show cancellations.** A single show closing early can blow a hole in a supplier’s revenue. Underwrite for that scenario by requiring event cancellation insurance or a diversified client roster. A business with 30% of revenue from one show is a concentration risk. Require a plan to mitigate that before funding.

**Concrete Implications for Brokers**

Brokers who serve the experience economy should segment borrowers into three tiers:

- **Venue operators** (theaters, concert halls, event spaces): These businesses have predictable overhead but unpredictable ticket revenue. Brokers should offer revenue-based financing with payment holidays during off-peak months.

- **Vendors and suppliers** (costume shops, lighting rental, catering, security): Their revenue is tied to specific productions. Brokers need to evaluate the strength of the production company, not just the vendor’s financials. A vendor with a contract for a show that wins a Tony is a stronger credit than one tied to a flop.

- **Adjacent hospitality** (restaurants, hotels, parking near venues): These businesses benefit from foot traffic but do not control it. Their risk is lower because they serve multiple shows and tourists. Standard working capital products work here, but advance rates should account for the seasonal surge.

Brokers who can map these tiers and match them to the right funding products will win deal flow. The experience economy is growing faster than the broader economy. Consumer spending on live events is projected to grow 8% annually through 2030. That is a lending vertical waiting to be structured.

**ISO Network Strategy**

ISO networks should treat the experience economy as a separate vertical with its own sales scripts and partner qualification criteria. Train agents to ask: “What is your peak revenue month? What percentage of annual revenue comes from that month? Do you have contracts or advance tickets that guarantee revenue three months out?”

Pivot to funders who offer seasonal repayment schedules. If a funder only does daily ACH, that product is a poor fit for a business that gets paid in lump sums. ISO networks that specialize in this vertical can negotiate better rates with funders who understand the cash flow profile.

**The Secondary Market Signal**

Broadway’s record also sends a signal about consumer discretionary spending resilience. As inflation moderates and wages rise, households are allocating more budget to experiences. That trend benefits businesses that are not tied to inventory or supply chains. Their margins are higher, and their risk is more about consumer taste than economic cycles.

But that resilience is not uniform. A show that loses a star actor or gets bad reviews can see ticket sales drop 50% overnight. Lenders must underwrite for that downside. The question is not “will the business survive?” but “can it survive a bad season?” If the answer requires a second loan to cover a cash shortfall, the first loan was mispriced.

**Recommendations for Underwriters**

- Request 12 months of bank statements and 6 months of forward bookings or contracts.
- Calculate a Debt Service Coverage Ratio using annualized cash flow, not monthly averages.
- Apply a concentration penalty if a single client or event accounts for more than 25% of revenue.
- Require event cancellation insurance for businesses with more than 40% revenue from a single annual event.
- Offer lines of credit with seasonal draw limits rather than term loans with fixed payments.

The experience economy is not a fad. It is a structural shift in consumer behavior. Small business lenders who adapt their underwriting to this vertical will capture a growing, high-margin portfolio. Those who treat it like any other seasonal business will either decline creditworthy borrowers or lend into hidden risk.

Broadway’s $1.9 billion season is a warning and an opportunity. The warning: standard cash flow models are blind to event-driven revenue. The opportunity: a new underwriting framework that matches repayment to real cash inflows. Build it now, before your competitors do.

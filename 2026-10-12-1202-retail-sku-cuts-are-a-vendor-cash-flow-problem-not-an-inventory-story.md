# Retail SKU Cuts Are a Vendor Cash-Flow Problem, Not an Inventory Story

BJ's and Lululemon are trimming assortments. The retailer narrative is simple: fewer SKUs, better curation, healthier margins. For small business lenders, the signal is not inventory turns. It is vendor cash flow. When a retailer cuts SKUs, it does not absorb the risk. It pushes markdowns, returns, chargebacks, and slower conversion back through the supply chain. That hits small suppliers, distributors, 3PLs, packaging vendors, and marketing agencies. If your underwriting still treats retail inventory as the primary collateral, you are looking at the wrong side of the balance sheet.

## What an assortment cut does to a small supplier

A retailer dropping 15% of SKUs is not a neutral event. It is a reallocation of shelf space and working capital. The winners get deeper buys and more replenishment. The losers get return authorizations, canceled POs, and packaging that cannot be reused.

The middle group is the real underwriting problem. These suppliers keep the account but lose the tail. Revenue may look stable in aggregate while the mix deteriorates. The retailer demands sharper fill rates, tighter OTIF compliance, new labels, and promotional allowances. Then it deducts those costs from invoices. The supplier's gross invoice may be $250,000. The net settlement may be $215,000 after chargebacks, co-op, and markdown support.

That gap is not a collection problem. It is an underwriting input. Small business lenders that advance against gross receivables without a deduction reserve are lending into a known leak.

## The collateral is more fragile than it looks

Inventory-backed lending assumes a lender can take possession and liquidate. Assortment cuts break that assumption at the SKU level. Finished goods tied to a discontinued SKU may have little secondary market. Private-label packaging has negative value. Raw materials and components bought for a retailer-specific program may be stranded.

Advance rates should reflect that. A generic 50% advance on inventory is too blunt for retail-exposed borrowers. Funders need SKU-level detail: which products are on the retailer's current assortment plan, which are on markdown, which are return-to-vendor, and which are packaging or component stock. If the borrower cannot produce that data, the advance rate should drop, not rise.

Receivables need the same treatment. A retailer's vendor portal is the source of truth. The AR aging may show current invoices. The settlement report may show unapplied deductions, debit memos, and post-audit claims. Lenders should underwrite to the net settlement, not the invoice face. A deduction-adjusted borrowing base is not a premium feature. It is the baseline for retail supplier financing.

## Why this matters for funders, brokers, and ISO networks

Brokers and ISO networks often package retail suppliers as low-risk because the end customer is a large retailer. That logic is incomplete. A large retailer is a strong credit, but it is also a strong counterparty. It can dictate terms, deduct fees, and return goods. The small supplier carries the volatility.

Funders should re-underwrite retail-exposed files with three concentration tests:

1. Retailer concentration. If one retailer is more than 30% of revenue, the file needs retailer-specific monitoring. Above 50%, treat it as a special asset.
2. SKU concentration. If one SKU is more than 25% of revenue, the borrower has binary risk. A single assortment decision can reset cash flow.
3. Deduction concentration. If chargebacks and markdowns exceed 5% of gross sales, the borrower is financing the retailer's margin, not its own growth.

These tests change pricing, advance rates, and structure. They also change the sales conversation. A broker who says 'you have a big retailer receivable' is not solving the problem. A broker who says 'we will fund against net settlement after deductions, and we need the vendor portal read-only access' is setting a realistic expectation.

## The working capital use case is not growth. It is the gap.

Retail SKU cuts create a specific working capital gap. The supplier ships a large order, waits 60 to 90 days for payment, then absorbs deductions. Meanwhile, it must fund the next production run, packaging changes, and compliance costs. The business may be profitable on paper but cash-negative in the operating cycle.

That gap is fundable. It is not a reason to decline automatically. It is a reason to structure the advance around the retailer's payment behavior. Purchase order financing can work when the PO is tied to a confirmed assortment plan and open-to-buy. Invoice factoring can work when the factor controls the retailer remittance and holds a deduction reserve. Asset-based lending can work when the borrowing base excludes discontinued inventory and ages deductions separately. Merchant cash advance and short-term working capital can work when daily remittances are sized to net deposits, not gross sales.

The common requirement is data. Funders need retailer vendor portals, deduction reports, PO confirmations, and SKU-level sales. Without that, the lender is underwriting a story.

## Red flags in retail-exposed files

Watch for borrowers that show strong revenue but rising deductions. A retailer may be taking markdown support after the sale, which turns a clean invoice into a partial payment. Watch for inventory build in packaging and components. That is often a sign of a discontinued SKU that has not been written down. Watch for a single retailer driving most of the growth. That is not diversification. It is dependency.

Also watch for the 'big PO' narrative. A large purchase order from a major retailer can be real, but it can also be cancelable, subject to compliance, or tied to a SKU that is still in test. Funders should verify the retailer's commitment and the borrower's capacity to execute. A PO is not collateral until it converts to a shipped, accepted, deduction-adjusted receivable.

## What operators should do now

Retail assortment cuts are not a temporary cycle. They are a permanent discipline. Retailers will keep culling SKUs, consolidating vendors, and pushing risk backward. Small business lenders should adjust underwriting accordingly:

- Add SKU-level and deduction-level data to retail-exposed files.
- Set advance rates by collateral quality, not by blanket asset class.
- Reserve for chargebacks, markdowns, and returns at the borrowing base level.
- Shorten duration for borrowers with concentrated retailer exposure.
- Train brokers and ISO networks to qualify on net settlement, not gross invoice.
- Monitor retailer assortment announcements as leading credit signals.

The headline is about BJ's and Lululemon. The lending implication is about every small supplier behind them. The retailer gets the margin benefit. The supplier gets the cash-flow risk. Funders that underwrite the deduction, the SKU concentration, and the retailer's replenishment model will price that risk correctly. Funders that do not will find out at settlement.

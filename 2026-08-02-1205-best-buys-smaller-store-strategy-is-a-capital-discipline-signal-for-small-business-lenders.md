## The Smaller-Store Discipline

Best Buy's incoming CEO Jason Bonfig wants to keep growing by going smaller. That sounds like a retail strategy, but it is also a capital allocation philosophy. For business lenders, the signal is direct: the path to greater yield and lower loss severity runs through smaller facility sizes, faster repayment cycles, and diversified exposure.

### Why smaller is a lending signal

Best Buy's smaller stores are not just reduced versions of big boxes. They are lower-capex nodes with tighter inventory assortments, faster inventory turns, and a value proposition built around convenience and service. The physical footprint is smaller, the working capital requirements are lower, and the payback period is shorter. That is disciplined unit economics.

Too many small business lenders run the opposite model. They chase larger deal sizes to justify origination cost, extend longer terms to make payments affordable, and hold concentrated positions in a handful of safe borrowers. The result is a portfolio that looks stable before a shock and illiquid after one. Best Buy's strategy is the counterweight: instead of doubling down on a few expensive boxes, expand reach with many low-cost, high-velocity locations.

Apply that to lending. A $100,000 loan to a single-location retail business is a concentrated bet on one local economy. A portfolio of ten $10,000 facilities across different trade types, geographies, and payment processors is more diversified, more responsive to early stress, and easier to reprice. The loss rate is managed by structure rather than by hope.

### What funders should change

Funders should treat Best Buy's smaller-store pivot as a capital efficiency benchmark. That means four underwriting shifts.

First, cap facility size by verified cash flow, not by collateral or confidence. If a borrower cannot repay a facility in nine to fourteen months from actual operating cash flow, the facility is too large. A smaller store works because its fixed costs fit its revenue capacity. A small business loan works the same way.

Second, favor products with automatic repayment mechanics. Revenue-based financing, merchant cash advances, and ACH-payment term loans convert a loan into a working capital flow. When repayment is tied to daily or weekly cash flow, the risk is spread across sales rather than concentrated in a single monthly payment. This is the lender equivalent of daily inventory replenishment.

Third, demand more frequent data. Best Buy's smaller stores are monitored with real-time sales, inventory, and traffic data. Lenders should not underwrite a small-balance facility with only a credit score and a tax return. Use bank account data, payment processor statements, and point-of-sale history. If the data cannot be verified in near real time, the facility should be smaller or declined.

Fourth, build early intervention triggers. Small stores fail when local cash flow dips and fixed costs stay flat. Small businesses are the same. Set performance triggers tied to deposit velocity and payment performance. When a borrower starts stretching payments or clearing daily ACH late, act at day 10, not day 60. Small-ticket lending is a volume game, and the only way to play it safely is to detect stress early and stop losses at small sizes.

### What brokers and ISO networks should do

For brokers and ISO networks, Best Buy's message is a portfolio construction lesson. Stop recruiting merchants by maximum funding amount. Start originating by appropriate facility size and repayment capacity. A broker who builds a book of $50,000 to $150,000 revenue-based facilities across restaurants, trades, and retail is selling a diversified asset to a funder. A broker who chases a $1 million one-off merchant is selling concentration.

This also creates a sales advantage. Business owners are often over-offered. A merchant with $60,000 monthly revenue does not need a $500,000 loan. A calibrated proposal of $75,000 with a structured daily repayment plan is more credible and more likely to close. That is the smaller-to-grow pitch: use the right amount of capital to fund the next node, not to overbuild the current one.

Brokers should also segment by unit economics. Target merchants with strong revenue per square foot, fast inventory turns, and low fixed-cost ratios. Those are the smaller-store candidates of the small business economy. Funders will reward brokers who deliver a high volume of clean, data-verified small facilities over a small number of large, complicated ones.

ISOs and brokerages should invest in automation that makes smaller originations profitable. If manual underwriting makes a $40,000 facility unprofitable, the model is broken. Use APIs to pull bank and card data, apply decision rules, and push offers to funding partners. Small-ticket lending is about turn speed, not relationship dinners.

### The counterpoint

Going smaller is not the right answer for every borrower. Businesses with real capex needs, such as construction firms purchasing equipment or clinics adding a location, require larger facilities. The point is not to ban large loans. The point is to match the facility size to the operating model's capital intensity and repayment cycle.

Best Buy is not abandoning large stores entirely. It is shifting growth into smaller stores because those have a better return on invested capital. Lenders should use the same test. For each loan, ask whether the borrower's business model can absorb the fixed repayment burden. If the model is a high-velocity, low-capex operation, structure a high-velocity, low-capex loan. If the model is project-based and lumpy, use milestone draws and percentage-of-completion monitoring. The facility size is not a vanity metric. It is a risk parameter.

### The operator-grade takeaway

Best Buy's smaller-store strategy is not a retail story. It is a capital discipline signal. The right way to grow revenue is not always to increase the size of each transaction. It is to increase the number of healthy transactions and reduce the cost and risk per unit. That applies as much to a lending portfolio as to a consumer electronics chain.

Funders who adopt the shrink-to-grow discipline will see lower concentration risk, faster loss detection, and more repeat borrowers. Brokers and ISO networks that sell right-sized capital instead of maximum funding will build a cleaner pipeline and a better reputation with funders.

The next phase of small business lending will not be won by the biggest loan ticket. It will be won by the most efficient capital unit. Best Buy understood that with smaller stores. Lenders should understand it with smaller facilities.

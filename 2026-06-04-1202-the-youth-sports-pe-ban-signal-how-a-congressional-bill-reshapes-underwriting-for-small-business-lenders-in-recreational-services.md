## The Youth Sports PE Ban Signal: How a Congressional Bill Reshapes Underwriting for Small Business Lenders in Recreational Services

A few weeks ago, Eli Manning’s private equity firm, Brand Velocity Group, announced the acquisition of RCX, a company that owns licensing rights for NFL Flag football and other youth sports properties. The deal was framed as a bet on the exploding youth sports market — a market that generates more than $30 billion annually in direct spending across facilities, equipment, camps, leagues, and tournaments.

At the same time, the U.S. Congress is advancing a bipartisan bill that would effectively ban private equity firms from owning or controlling youth sports organizations. The bill’s stated rationale: preventing financial engineering from undermining the integrity of youth athletics, controlling costs for families, and ensuring that non-profit and community models can compete.

These two events look contradictory. They are not. They signal something more important for small business lenders: a coming liquidity event and valuation reset for the entire youth sports ecosystem. If you have exposure to small businesses that depend on PE-backed leagues, franchises, or event operators, you need to update your underwriting models now.

### The Deal and the Bill: Two Sides of the Same Risk

Brand Velocity Group’s acquisition of RCX values the licensing company at a multiple consistent with PE’s aggressive push into youth sports over the last five years. PE firms have bought or funded travel leagues, tournament organizers, facility chains, and even individual sports academies. The logic: family spending on youth sports is recession-resistant, sticky, and growing at 5-7% annually.

The congressional bill, cosponsored by senators from both parties, would prohibit private equity firms from owning a controlling stake in any youth sports entity that organizes or sponsors competitions for minors. Penalties include forced divestiture within 18 months of enactment. While the bill’s path to passage is uncertain, it has already prompted PE firms to model forced exit scenarios. That modeling is now cascading down to the small businesses that serve these entities.

### Why This Matters for Small Business Lenders

Most underwriters treat youth sports businesses as lifestyle or local service operations. A soccer complex, a gymnastics gym, a baseball academy — these are seen as stable, cash-flow-driven, often with repeat recurring revenue from memberships, class fees, and tournaments.

What changes when PE-backed operators are forced to sell or restructure?

- **Revenue concentration risk.** Many small businesses (equipment retailers, food vendors, uniform printers, coaches-for-hire) generate 30-60% of their revenue through contracts with PE-owned leagues or tournament series. If those leagues are sold to fragmented non-profit operators or dissolved, the revenue disappears or becomes seasonal and unreliable.
- **Working capital dislocation.** PE-backed leagues often prepay for facility bookings, commit to large equipment orders, and provide predictable payment schedules. Non-profit or community-run replacements typically pay net-60 or net-90, if they clear at all. A small supplier that relied on net-15 from a PE-owned league could face a cash crunch within two billing cycles.
- **Collateral devaluation.** A small business’s primary collateral — branded merchandise, specialized equipment, leasehold improvements tied to PE-backed league standards — may become worthless if the league’s IP or operating model is abandoned.

The congressional bill does not need to pass to create damage. The mere risk of passage is already causing PE firms to slow or reverse investments. Some are quietly shopping youth sports assets. That creates a market where valuations compress and buyers have less leverage. Small businesses that borrowed against those inflated valuations or projected revenue streams will face covenant violations or refinancing difficulties.

### Concrete Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISO Networks

**For funders:**

- Review your portfolio for any merchant whose primary business is youth sports — even if classified under “recreation,” “events,” or “education.” Flag accounts where the customer’s revenue depends on a PE-owned league, tournament series, or franchise operator.
- Run a concentration test: if the top three revenue sources for that merchant are PE-backed, flag the account for enhanced monitoring. Consider reducing advance rates or requiring personal guarantees.
- Adjust your cash flow models. A stable 36-month history of membership fees may break in 12 months if the organizing entity changes hands. Stress-test with a 40% revenue drop and a shift to net-60 receivables.

**For brokers and ISOs:**

- When presenting opportunities from youth sports businesses, ask the borrower directly: who is your largest league partner? Is it privately held, PE-owned, or a non-profit? If the answer is “we work with X League” and X League is known to have PE backing, flag the file.
- Do not accept “diversified revenue” claims at face value. Many small youth sports businesses spread revenue across multiple leagues, but those leagues may all be owned by the same PE firm through separate subsidiaries. True concentration can be hidden.
- Use this issue as a differentiator in pitches to funders. Showing awareness of legislative risk signals that you provide sourcing intelligence, not just deal flow.

### How to Underwrite Youth Sports Businesses Going Forward

The traditional approach — look at bank statements, calculate average daily balances, apply a blanket factor — is insufficient. Instead, adopt a three-layer underwriting framework:

**Layer 1: Entity structure of the customer’s counterparty.**

Determine whether the small business’s main revenue sources are PE-owned, LLC-owned by a private group, non-profit, or municipal. PE-owned or large private-group ownership carries higher event risk. Non-profit or municipal ownership carries lower liquidity risk but longer payment cycles.

**Layer 2: Contract duration and termination clauses.**

Review a sample contract between the small business and its league partner. How long is the term? What are the termination triggers? If the league is sold or dissolved, does the contract survive? Many PE-backed leagues include change-of-control clauses that allow immediate renegotiation, giving the small business no protection.

**Layer 3: Break-even sensitivity.**

Run a scenario where the PE-backed league disappears entirely. How many months of cash does the small business have to find a replacement? If that number is below three, consider the account high-risk. If the business relies on league-specific branding (e.g., “Official Equipment Supplier for X Tournament Series”), that brand value is zero upon exit.

### A Real-World Example

Take a small uniform printing shop in suburban Atlanta. 70% of its revenue comes from printing jerseys for a PE-owned regional travel baseball league. The shop used a merchant cash advance 18 months ago, renewed at higher rates due to strong revenue. If the congressional bill passes and the PE firm divests, the league may convert to a non-profit model with local sponsors. The uniform contract may be cancelled or put out for competitive bidding. The shop’s revenue drops 60% overnight, and its MCA payment becomes unsustainable.

A lender that had flagged this as a concentration risk could have refused the renewal, required additional collateral, or structured a lower advance against that volatile revenue stream. Without that analysis, the lender is now holding a non-performing asset.

### The Broader Lesson: Legislative Risk Is Real Risk

Most small business lending models ignore legislative risk. They treat it as a black swan, something that happens to large public companies, not to the local gym owner or equipment distributor. The youth sports PE ban shows that regulatory action can target any vertical, and the impact travels faster and deeper than expected.

For funders, the lesson is to build a legislative risk review into your sector analysis. When a bill with bipartisan support targets a specific business model, consider that a material event even before passage. For brokers and ISOs, the opportunity is to educate borrowers about dependency risk and help them diversify before the bill takes effect.

### Actionable Steps This Week

1. Export your portfolio by NAICS code for youth sports-related categories: 713940 (fitness and recreational sports centers), 339920 (sporting and athletic goods manufacturing), 451110 (sporting goods stores), 611620 (sports instruction).
2. For each account, manually verify the ownership of the customer’s main league or tournament partner. Use public records, industry publications, or direct inquiry.
3. Run a stress scenario: reduce projected revenue by 40% and extend receivable collection by 30 days. If the account’s debt service coverage ratio falls below 1.1x, escalate for review.
4. Update your underwriting checklist to include a “counterparty ownership” field for all recreation-sector deals.

The youth sports market is not going away. But the way capital is structured within it is changing fast. Lenders who adapt to this new risk signal will preserve portfolio quality. Those who ignore it will find themselves holding paper backed by league contracts that no longer exist.

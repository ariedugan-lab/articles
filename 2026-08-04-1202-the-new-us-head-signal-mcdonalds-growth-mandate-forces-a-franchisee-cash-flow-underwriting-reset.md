## The earnings beat is not the franchisee's cash flow

McDonald's beat earnings estimates in Q2 2026. The stock still sits 11% below its year-ago level. The market cap is roughly $191 billion. The company also announced a new U.S. head to accelerate growth. For small-business lenders, that announcement is not a footnote. It is an underwriting signal.

The new U.S. chief will be measured on same-store sales, digital share, and unit throughput. Those metrics are corporate priorities. They are not franchisee profit metrics. McDonald's collects royalties, rents, and marketing fees on systemwide sales. The franchisee pays for labor, food, utilities, maintenance, insurance, and local marketing. When the brand promotes value menus, the operator often sees more customers but a lower average check. The bottom line can compress even when top-line same-store sales improve.

Lenders must stop using brand-level strength as a proxy for unit-level health. Same-store sales tells you what the system is doing. Store-level EBITDA tells you what the borrower can repay. The two diverge more often than the quoted same-store sales number suggests.

## What a new U.S. head means for franchisee capital

A growth mandate arrives with capital requirements. New leadership tends to accelerate store remodels, drive-thru upgrades, digital ordering systems, and new operational formats. Each of those initiatives shifts costs from the corporate P&L to franchisee balance sheets. A franchisee can expect to spend hundreds of thousands of dollars per location when a major QSR brand pushes a next-generation store format.

That spending is not optional. Franchise agreements often require operators to meet brand standards and reinvest in the business. A franchisee who fails to keep pace risks non-renewal or default under the agreement. That dynamic creates a forced capex cycle. Lenders need to know which of their borrowers are facing that pressure.

Underwrite the borrower's balance sheet, not the brand's earnings call. Ask the franchisee directly about the franchisor's latest communications. Review the franchise agreement's reinvestment clause. Check for recent UCC filings that show new equipment loans. A sudden increase in secured debt is not always a red flag, but it must be modeled in the debt service coverage.

## Build a franchisee capex overlay

When a brand like McDonald's installs a new U.S. head, funders with QSR exposure should run a portfolio-level review. Flag every franchisee loan that depends on a quick-service brand. Then apply a capex overlay to each borrower's cash flow.

Start with the unit-level income statement. Adjust EBITDA for owner-operator wages and one-time expenses. Calculate the ratio of capital expenditures to depreciation over the past two years. If that ratio is above one, the operator is investing. If it is below one, the operator may be deferring maintenance and could face a catch-up period. Both scenarios change expected free cash flow.

Then model the next mandated investment. If the franchisor announces a remodel that costs $400,000 per store, stress the borrower's ability to service that new debt. A 10-year term loan at current rates creates a monthly payment near $4,000 per $400,000. Does the store generate enough cash flow to absorb that payment and still cover working capital needs? If not, the funded amount should be sized lower or structured with a delayed repayment period during construction.

Also consider the mix of corporate and franchise stores. McDonald's earnings and growth plans include company-operated locations, but most lenders are serving franchisees. Company-owned store economics are not a reliable guide to franchisee economics. Use operator-specific data.

## Segmentation is the underwriting edge

The quick-service restaurant market is not one risk class. There is a wide gap between a well-capitalized owner-operator with three profitable stores and a leveraged multi-unit operator with one weak asset consuming all cash flow. The same brand can produce both credits. The new growth mandate will widen that gap.

Franchisees with strong liquidity and access to SBA 7(a) financing or conventional bank credit will use the remodel cycle to build more valuable businesses. Franchisees with thin margins will scramble for short-term capital and may turn to merchant cash advances. For funders, the second group carries higher risk, but it can also generate higher yield. The key is pricing for the actual risk and not treating the brand name as a substitute for diligence.

Brokers should position themselves before the pressure hits. A franchisee with a planned remodel may need a term loan, an equipment lease, or a working capital line. A franchisee who waits until a franchisor deadline is closer to the emergency segment. That distinction matters for both pricing and approval odds. ISO networks can help by routing franchisee leads with capex context to funders that understand the vertical.

## What funders, brokers, and ISO networks should do now

Funders should tighten underwriting for any franchisee in a growth-acceleration system. Require the latest store-level P&L and a written statement of planned capital expenditures. If the borrower cannot produce those documents, treat the application as higher risk. For renewal decisions, compare store-level EBITDA across the last three fiscal quarters. Look for a pattern where same-store sales rise but EBITDA per store falls. That is the exact squeeze created by value-menu-led growth.

Brokers should re-engage their restaurant and franchise client books. Ask about franchisor communications and planned store investments. If a client operates a QSR brand that recently changed leadership, pull updated financials before they ask for money. A proactive capital plan is a stronger deal than an emergency funding request. A well-structured working capital line or term loan is also a better fit than stacking a merchant cash advance on top of an already strained store.

ISO networks should update their intelligence. Add fields to the application that capture the franchise brand, the number of units, the operator's store-level EBITDA, and any known franchisor initiatives. Train sales agents to ask about remodels, equipment upgrades, and new technology mandates. When a lead includes those details, send it to a funder with a dedicated QSR underwriting model. Sending a capex-heavy franchisee to a generic cash-flow lender is a recipe for mispriced risk.

## The main underwriting shift

McDonald's leadership change is not an isolated event. It is a pattern. Large franchisors under public-market pressure will use franchisee capital to fuel growth. Lenders who understand that dynamic can separate resilient operators from exposed ones.

The companies that win in the next credit cycle will be the ones that underwrite the operator's actual cash flow, model the franchisor's capital demands, and segment franchisees by their ability to fund growth. The earnings beat is real, but it does not pay the franchisee's next equipment invoice. The store-level P&L does. That is the only underwriting signal that matters when the next mandated capex cycle arrives.

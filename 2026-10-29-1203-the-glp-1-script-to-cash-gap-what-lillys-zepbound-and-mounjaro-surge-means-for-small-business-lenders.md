# The GLP-1 Script-to-Cash Gap

Eli Lilly's Q3 beat and raised sales outlook confirm one thing for small business lenders: demand for Zepbound and Mounjaro is not slowing. That does not automatically make every pharmacy, clinic, or medication-adjacent small business a better credit. It makes the cash conversion cycle more important.

The credit signal is the script-to-cash gap. High-cost GLP-1 prescriptions create a working capital float between drug purchase, dispensing, and reimbursement. Independent pharmacies buy inventory before they get paid. Weight-management clinics verify benefits, chase prior authorization, collect patient pay, and wait on payer claims. A demand surge increases revenue and increases the size of that float. Lenders that underwrite top-line script growth without measuring reimbursement timing will misprice risk.

## Demand is not revenue quality

GLP-1 drugs are high-ticket, recurring, and payer-controlled. A pharmacy can show rising prescription volume and still run tight because each script consumes more inventory and generates a receivable with a payer-specific lag. A clinic can show strong bookings and still miss payroll if prior authorization approvals stall or patient financing chargebacks rise.

For funders, the first adjustment is to stop treating GLP-1 volume as a clean growth signal. Recast revenue by product, payer, and collection channel. Separate cash-pay from insurance claims. Separate dispensing revenue from patient-assistance or manufacturer support. Measure gross-to-net prescription economics after PBM fees, DIR-style clawbacks, dispensing fees, and chargebacks. The question is not how many scripts the business writes. It is how much cash each script releases and when.

## Underwrite the reimbursement float

Small business lenders serving independent pharmacies and weight-management clinics need a script-level cash flow model. Build the cycle from inventory purchase to dispensing to claim submission to remittance. Then stress it.

Key underwriting inputs:
- Payer mix and PBM concentration. A pharmacy with one dominant PBM contract is a single-point-of-failure credit.
- Prior authorization approval rate and backlog. Rising PA backlog is a leading indicator of denied claims and delayed cash.
- Days sales outstanding by payer. Use actual remittance data, not management estimates.
- Inventory days and return policy. GLP-1 inventory is expensive and has expiration and handling constraints.
- Denial and chargeback rate. Patient financing and copay card programs can shift risk back to the provider.
- Gross-to-net erosion. PBM contracts can reprice after the claim is paid.
- Patient cash-pay concentration. Cash-pay clinics can be strong, but they need documented prepayment and refund policies.

For clinics, split membership or cash-pay revenue from insurance claims. A cash-pay weight-loss clinic with prepaid packages has a different risk profile than a clinic billing insurers for GLP-1 prescriptions. Both can work. They need different borrowing bases and different reporting.

## What funders should change

Funders should move from revenue-based sizing to receivables and inventory-based sizing for GLP-1-exposed borrowers. Set the borrowing base on eligible, undisputed receivables. Exclude denied, pending, and aged claims. Cap inventory advances and require evidence of turns. Require payer diversification covenants when concentration is high. Add triggers for DSO, denial rate, prior authorization backlog, and inventory days. A renewal should depend on cash conversion, not script count.

Use shorter review cycles. GLP-1 demand can shift fast, and payer policy can shift faster. Monthly reporting is a minimum for high-ticket exposures. Weekly or daily reporting is reasonable when a borrower has concentrated PBM exposure or a large PA backlog. Lenders that can monitor script-to-cash in near real time will take better risk. Lenders that rely on annual financials will see the problem after the float has already stretched.

Avoid term loans sized on top-line growth. The better product is a working capital line tied to eligible receivables and inventory. A merchant cash advance or fixed daily remittance can mismatch a lumpy reimbursement cycle. If a pharmacy pays daily for an advance while waiting 30 to 45 days for payer remittance, the structure can create a second cash flow problem. Funders should match repayment frequency to the borrower's collection cycle.

## What brokers and ISO networks should package

Brokers and ISO networks can improve close rates by standardizing the GLP-1 file. Do not send a lender a tax return and a script count. Send a payer mix schedule, product mix, DSO by payer, PA approval rate, denial rate, inventory turns, and a 13-week cash flow. Show the borrower's largest PBM contracts and renewal dates. Show patient financing chargebacks. Show how the borrower handles manufacturer copay support and refunds.

Position the loan as a float bridge, not growth capital. The use of proceeds should be specific: inventory build for verified demand, payroll during reimbursement lag, or technology to reduce PA backlog. Lenders price clarity. A file that measures script-to-cash will get better terms than a file that promises more scripts.

For ISO networks, build a healthcare receivables vertical with lenders that understand PBM reimbursement. Match high-ticket GLP-1 borrowers to funders with healthcare experience, not generalist MCA desks. Train referral partners to spot red flags: rising denials, payer concentration, inventory days above 30, aggressive patient financing, and contract renewals inside the loan term.

## Red flags and green flags

Red flags:
- One payer or PBM represents more than 30 percent of collections.
- Prior authorization backlog is growing faster than script volume.
- Denial rate is rising while management reports record revenue.
- Inventory days are above 30 and turning slower.
- Patient financing chargebacks are climbing.
- The borrower cannot produce remittance-level data.
- A major PBM contract renews within 12 months and the borrower has no margin cushion.

Green flags:
- Diversified payer base with documented remittance history.
- Clean claims and a defined PA workflow.
- Inventory turns under 15 days with a return policy.
- Stable DSO and denial rate through a demand surge.
- Cash-pay clinic revenue with prepaid packages and clear refund terms.
- A 13-week cash flow that ties to bank statements.
- Reporting that shows script-to-cash, not just script count.

## The operator takeaway

Eli Lilly's beat confirms demand. It does not confirm credit quality. For small business lenders, the Zepbound and Mounjaro surge is a receivables event. Funders that underwrite the reimbursement float will separate durable borrowers from revenue mirages. Brokers and ISO networks that package payer mix, prior authorization data, and inventory turns will place files with better pricing and fewer surprises. The winning underwriting question is simple: how fast does a GLP-1 script turn into collected cash, and what happens when it does not.

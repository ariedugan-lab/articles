Royal Caribbean is nearing a $3 billion deal to take a 50% equity stake in Sandals. The headline reads as cruise consolidation. The lending signal is different. Royal Caribbean is buying distribution into land-based vacations and turning a resort brand into a channel captain. For small business lenders, that shift changes how to underwrite hotels, vendors, tour operators, and the local businesses that depend on resort traffic.

The core issue is not whether tourism demand is strong. It is who controls the booking, the bundle, and the vendor payment. When a cruise operator owns half of an all-inclusive resort brand, it can package cruise and land stays, steer guests to owned or affiliated properties, and set procurement terms for the small businesses that service those properties. That creates a captive channel. Captive channels produce stable volume for some vendors and severe concentration risk for others. Funders need to tell the difference.

## The captive channel model

All-inclusive resorts bundle room, food, beverage, transfers, and activities into one price. When Royal Caribbean and Sandals control that bundle, they control where guest spend goes. Vendors inside the gate, including food distributors, laundry services, maintenance contractors, landscaping firms, entertainment providers, staffing agencies, and security companies, become dependent on the resort operator's procurement decisions. Vendors outside the gate, including independent excursion operators, restaurants, retail shops, and ground transport companies, face a harder choice: partner with the brand or compete against a bundle that keeps guests on property.

This is not a resort story. It is a channel control story. Small business lenders already underwrite healthcare providers by payer mix and auto suppliers by OEM concentration. Hospitality lending now needs the same discipline. A borrower that says it serves Sandals guests is not a diversified tourism business. It is a contract business until proven otherwise.

## What changes for revenue quality

Captive volume can look like high-quality revenue because it repeats. But repeat volume is not the same as durable revenue. If the contract is terminable on 30 days, if the operator can change vendors without cause, or if payment terms stretch to net 60 or net 90, the borrower carries working capital risk that a credit card sales report will not show.

Lenders should test four things before treating resort-linked revenue as bankable.

First, identify the obligor. The payer may be a joint venture, a management company, or a special purpose entity, not Royal Caribbean or Sandals directly. Verify the legal entity that owes the receivable, the guarantors, and the payment history. Brand strength at the parent level does not automatically flow to the vendor contract.

Second, read the contract. Look for term, renewal options, minimum volume commitments, exclusivity, termination for convenience, chargebacks, and setoff rights. A five-year contract with minimum volume is a different asset than a handshake relationship with a resort manager.

Third, calculate concentration. If one resort, one brand, or one joint venture represents more than 25% of a borrower's revenue, underwrite it as single-obligor risk. If it represents more than 50%, the borrower is effectively a supplier to that channel captain. The credit decision depends on the channel captain's payment behavior and the borrower's replaceability.

Fourth, stress the cash conversion cycle. Captive volume often comes with longer payment terms and higher compliance costs. The borrower may need more working capital even while revenue grows. That is a classic margin compression trap. Funders should model a 20% volume decline, a 60-day payment delay, and a contract loss in the same quarter.

## Inside the gate versus outside the gate

Hospitality lenders should split resort-adjacent borrowers into two categories.

Inside the gate vendors have higher revenue visibility and lower customer acquisition costs. They also have concentrated counterparty risk and limited pricing power. Underwrite these files at the contract level. Use invoice verification, lockbox arrangements, assignment of receivables, and concentration caps. For factoring or asset-based lending, confirm that the contract allows assignment and does not include setoff language that can wipe out advances. For revenue-based financing, tie advances to verified invoices rather than aggregate card sales.

Outside the gate vendors have more diversification but less certainty. A local restaurant, tour operator, or retail shop may benefit from increased visitor volume, but an all-inclusive model can keep a large share of guest spend on property. Underwrite with a diversion stress test. Ask what percentage of resort guests leave the property, what percentage book third-party excursions, and what happens if the brand increases on-property activities. For brokers and ISOs, that means asking borrowers how they source customers and whether their traffic depends on guests who never leave the resort.

## Implications for funders, brokers, and ISO networks

Funders should build a hospitality channel concentration score into the credit box. Add questions to the application: Who is your largest customer? What is the contract term? What are the payment terms? Do you have exclusivity? What happens if the brand changes ownership or procurement policy? Pull bank statements and invoices to verify deposits from the joint venture or management company. Do not rely on merchant statements alone if corporate invoicing dominates the revenue mix.

Brokers and ISOs should position working capital for two specific needs. The first is compliance capex. Brand standards often require upgrades to POS systems, vehicles, kitchen equipment, insurance, and safety systems. Lenders can finance those upgrades, but only if the contract duration covers the amortization. The second is receivable gaps. Vendors with strong corporate receivables can qualify for invoice factoring or ABL. Vendors with seasonal cash flow may need a payment schedule that matches low-season revenue, not a daily MCA debit that breaks in the off months.

ISO networks should train reps to recognize channel captains in hospitality. A borrower that works with one resort brand is not a tourism recovery play. It is a contract concentration file. Package it with the contract, purchase orders, payment history, and a clear explanation of the borrower's replaceability. That file will price better and fund faster than a generic hospitality application.

## Underwriting checklist for resort-linked borrowers

- Verify the legal obligor, guarantors, and payment history.
- Confirm contract term, renewal rights, and termination for convenience.
- Calculate top-customer revenue concentration.
- Review payment terms, chargebacks, and setoff rights.
- Stress test a 20% volume decline and a 60-day payment delay.
- Match capex amortization to remaining contract life.
- Confirm insurance, indemnification, and assignment rights.
- Review seasonality and cash conversion cycle.
- Align payment frequency with cash flow for MCAs.
- Cap exposure to a single resort, brand, or joint venture.

## What to watch next

Royal Caribbean's integration plan, Sandals expansion, booking pace, and package attachment rates will determine how quickly this captive channel matures. If the joint venture pushes more vendors into corporate procurement, the lending opportunity shifts from local bank relationships to contract finance. If the joint venture slows capex or stretches payables, working capital stress will show up first in laundry, food, staffing, and maintenance vendors.

The $3 billion Sandals stake is a signal that vacation demand is consolidating under fewer, larger channel captains. For small business lenders, that means underwriting the channel, not the destination. Borrowers with contracts, verified receivables, and diversified customers will remain bankable. Borrowers with one brand and no contract will look like tourism exposure but behave like single-obligor credit risk. Adjust the credit box now.

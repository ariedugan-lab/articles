The GLP-1 implant from Vivani Medical is not just another delivery device. It represents a structural shift in how patients access semaglutide. Instead of weekly injections with high dropout rates and recurring pharmacy refills, a single subcutaneous implant could deliver the drug for months. For lenders serving weight-loss-adjacent small businesses, this changes the revenue model, cash flow timing, and risk profile of an entire vertical.

Most current GLP-1 underwriting assumes a recurring subscription or prescription refill pattern. Clinics charge monthly visit fees, compounding pharmacies rely on repeated orders, and device suppliers sell consumables. An implant flips that model into a large, front-loaded procedural payment followed by a long tail of low or zero revenue. Lenders who treat these businesses like standard recurring-revenue healthcare operations will misprice risk.

**The implant signal: adherence is now a lump sum**

Vivani Medical’s implant is designed to improve adherence by removing the injection burden. Current data suggests that up to 50% of patients on injectable GLP-1s discontinue within a year. An implant eliminates that friction. That is good for patient outcomes but disruptive for cash flow modeling.

For a weight loss clinic, a patient on injectables generates monthly revenue: $1,000 to $1,500 for the drug plus a consultation fee. Over 12 months, that is $12,000 to $18,000 in gross revenue per patient. With an implant, the clinic bills a one-time procedure fee plus the cost of the implant, likely $8,000 to $12,000. Revenue per patient drops 30% to 40% and becomes a single event. To maintain revenue, the clinic must increase patient volume by roughly 40% to 50% per year. That requires more marketing spend, more procedure room capacity, and more staff training. Lenders must evaluate whether a clinic can scale procedure throughput before the implant revenue arrives.

**Compounding pharmacies face a direct demand hit**

Compounding pharmacies that fill off-brand semaglutide scripts have been a fast-growing lending segment. Their revenue depends on repeat compounding orders, often monthly. If branded implants capture a meaningful share of the market, demand for compounded semaglutide will decline. The implant removes the need for weekly injections, and with a branded product at a known price, patients may prefer the convenience over an unknown compound.

A compounding pharmacy that relies on 60% to 70% of its revenue from GLP-1 compounds will see an erosion of that base. Lenders must model a revenue decay curve, not a stable recurring line. The pharmacy’s asset value depends on equipment and inventory that becomes less productive. Underwriting must include sensitivity analysis for a 20%, 30%, or 50% drop in compounding demand over 18 months.

**Device suppliers and procedure equipment lenders**

An implant requires a minor surgical procedure, typically done in-clinic. That means clinics need sterile trays, implant tools, and possibly ultrasound guidance equipment. Suppliers of these devices will see a spike in one-time capital purchases. Lenders financing those suppliers must assess whether the clinic’s implant volume is sufficient to justify the equipment cost.

For lenders providing equipment loans to clinics, the collateral value of that equipment is tied to the implant’s market adoption. If adoption slows, used implant procedure kits have low resale value. The underwriting must peg the loan term to the clinic’s projected implant volume, not a generic equipment value.

**ISOs and brokers: new opportunity in a reformed vertical**

For ISO networks and brokers, the implant shift creates two distinct lending opportunities. First, existing clinics that want to add implant procedures need working capital for marketing and staff training. These are growth loans, not survival loans. The credit profile improves if the clinic has a track record of patient volume and conversion. Second, start-up clinics focused exclusively on the implant model will emerge. These are higher risk because they have no historical patient base, but they can be underwritten on the basis of demographic demand and implant supply agreements.

Brokers should flag any clinic that has 40% or more of its revenue from GLP-1 injectables in the past year. That concentration is now a liability. Recommend that the clinic diversify into implant procedures or other services before the implant becomes mainstream. Lenders who adjust their questionnaires now will avoid a wave of distressed refinancing later.

**Underwriting playbook for funders**

1. **Segment by revenue model.** Distinguish between clinics that charge per-procedure (implant-friendly) and clinics that charge per-visit (injection model). The former have more predictable but lumpy cash flow; the latter face a revenue cliff.
2. **Recalculate customer lifetime value.** With an implant, patient lifetime revenue shrinks from multi-year recurring to a one-year lump. Use a patient retention rate based on implant duration, not historic injection adherence.
3. **Assess referral network dependence.** Implant clinics will need a steady flow of new patients to replace one-and-done implant patients. Is the clinic’s referral pipeline strong enough to support 50% annual volume growth?
4. **Watch for inventory obsolescence.** Compounding pharmacies with large semaglutide raw material stockpiles risk write-downs if branded implants capture share. Request inventory aging reports and a plan for alternative compounding revenue.
5. **Stress-test for implant supply risk.** The implant is a single-source product from Vivani or a future competitor. If the supply chain breaks, clinics with heavy implant revenue stop generating income. Include a 90-day supply disruption scenario in your underwriting.

**The broader implication for lending to healthcare small businesses**

The GLP-1 implant is a leading indicator. Drug delivery is moving toward long-acting formats: patches, implants, oral formulations. Each shift changes revenue timing and risk. Lenders who build models that adapt to delivery form factor, not just drug class, will have a competitive advantage. The old assumption that a recurring prescription is stable is breaking.

For small business lenders, the message is direct: stop underwriting GLP-1 businesses as if the injection model will persist. The implant is coming. The revenue model will invert. The risk profile will shift from steady state to lumpy growth or decline. Adjust your credit criteria now, or face a portfolio of mispriced loans that look fine on paper but break when the adoption curve hits.

**Action items for your portfolio**

- Review all healthcare small business loans with GLP-1 exposure. Label them by delivery model: injectable, compound, or prep-to-be-implant.
- Require quarterly revenue segmentation reports from clinics with over $500,000 in GLP-1 revenue.
- For new loan applications from compounding pharmacies, add a covenant requiring that no more than 50% of revenue comes from a single drug class or delivery method.
- Offer bridge financing for clinics that want to invest in implant procedure capability. Terms should include a royalty or success fee tied to implant volume.
- Train your credit analysts on the difference between subscription revenue and procedural revenue. Cash flow coverage ratios mean different things in each model.

The implant is not hype. It is a structural change. Treat it accordingly.

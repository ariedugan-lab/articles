The direct-to-consumer (DTC) playbook has evolved. What began as a wave of digitally native brands selling razors, diapers, and deodorant online has morphed into a consolidation engine. Mammoth Brands, the parent company of Harry’s and Coterie, now signals that the next phase of CPG growth will be driven not by single-product innovation but by acquisition-led aggregation. For small business lenders, this shift rewrites the risk profile of an entire supply chain.

Mammoth Brands has disclosed ambitions to become the next CPG giant. The company has already assembled a portfolio that includes shaving products, baby care, and personal care lines. Its strategy mirrors the playbook of larger aggregators but with a critical twist: these brands retain strong direct relationships with consumers while simultaneously expanding into retail channels like Target and Walmart. That dual distribution model creates a new set of working capital dynamics for the small businesses that manufacture, package, and distribute the goods.

Lenders who fund contract manufacturers, packaging suppliers, logistics providers, or raw material vendors serving the DTC ecosystem must now account for the aggregator effect. When a single parent company controls multiple brands and channels, the leverage shifts. Payment terms tighten. Order volumes become less predictable. And the concentration risk for any one supplier skyrockets.

**The Aggregator Model and Its Supply Chain Fallout**

Mammoth Brands is not alone. The DTC aggregator model has gained traction across CPG categories. The logic is straightforward: acquire proven digital brands, combine back-office operations, negotiate better terms with manufacturers, and push into retail to capture scale. But the result for small businesses in the supply chain is often a loss of bargaining power.

Consider a small contract manufacturer that produces razors for Harry’s. When Harry’s was an independent DTC brand, the relationship was symbiotic. The manufacturer had leverage because the brand depended on its capacity. Once Mammoth Brands acquires multiple razor brands or brings production in-house, that manufacturer becomes a replaceable component. The parent company can demand lower prices, longer payment cycles, or exclusive arrangements. The manufacturer’s cash flow suffers, and its ability to secure working capital from lenders depends on the health of that single customer relationship.

This pattern repeats across diapers, deodorants, and other categories where Mammoth Brands has a footprint. The aggregator’s procurement team can shift orders among suppliers, trim lead times, and impose just-in-time inventory discipline that pushes working capital risk downstream.

**The Dual-Channel Strain**

Mammoth Brands’ move into retail adds another layer of complexity. A DTC brand that sells directly to consumers typically carries less inventory and gets paid at point of sale. When the same brand enters retail, it must finance orders placed months in advance, pay slotting fees, and absorb the cost of returns. The parent company may pass these costs to suppliers in the form of delayed payments or chargebacks.

Small suppliers caught between the aggregator’s retail demands and their own operational costs face a cash flow gap. Standard underwriting models that rely on historical revenue and simple cash flow projections miss this dynamic. A supplier may show steady top-line growth as the aggregator expands, but underlying working capital needs are rising faster than margins. The lender who does not adjust for this hidden stress will see defaults when the aggregator’s retail push hits a speed bump.

**Concrete Underwriting Signals for Funders**

Funders who originate loans to small businesses in the CPG supply chain must look beyond standard financials. The aggregator’s own credit profile becomes a direct input into the risk assessment of every supplier that depends on it.

First, track the aggregator’s debt load. Mammoth Brands is reportedly considering an IPO, but if it chooses to remain private or takes on additional leverage to fund more acquisitions, that signals potential payment compression for suppliers. A highly leveraged parent has incentive to stretch trade payables. Lenders should require their small business borrowers to disclose the weighted average payment terms from their top customers and monitor changes over time.

Second, evaluate the supplier’s customer concentration. A contract manufacturer that derives more than 40 percent of revenue from Mammoth Brands or any single aggregator is at elevated risk. Funders should push for diversification clauses in loan covenants or at minimum price the risk appropriately. The aggregator’s decision to insource production or switch suppliers is a binary event that can devastate a supplier’s revenue overnight.

Third, assess the velocity of inventory turns. Suppliers that produce for DTC aggregators often hold raw materials specific to those brands. If the aggregator shifts product specifications or discontinues a brand, the supplier is left with obsolete stock. Underwriting models should include a scenario analysis for a sudden 30 percent drop in orders from the top customer.

**Brokers and ISOs: New Origination Opportunities and Warnings**

Brokers and ISO networks should view the DTC aggregator supply chain as both an origination channel and a risk trap. The opportunity lies in the growing demand for working capital among suppliers who must finance larger retail orders. These small businesses need short-term facilities to cover raw material purchases, labor, and logistics before they receive payment from the aggregator.

But brokers must vet the aggregator’s track record on payment timeliness. A supplier that consistently gets paid in 45 days versus 60 days is a stronger candidate. Brokers should ask: does the aggregator use dynamic discounting or supply chain finance programs? If the parent company offers early payment discounts, that signals a healthy relationship. If it pushes net-90 terms, the supplier’s cash flow will be strained regardless of top-line growth.

ISOs can target sub-verticals such as contract packagers, label printers, and transportation firms that serve the DTC aggregator segment. These businesses often fly under the radar of traditional bank lenders because their revenue is lumpy and tied to brand launch cycles. A well-structured revenue-based financing product that aligns with the aggregator’s purchase orders can serve this niche effectively.

**A New Underwriting Framework**

Lenders serving the CPG supply chain must move beyond simple cash flow analysis. The aggregator multiplier demands a layered approach:

- Evaluate the parent company’s credit rating, debt structure, and expansion plans. Public signals like IPO filings, earnings calls, and retail partnership announcements matter.

- Require supplier borrowers to provide aging reports from their top three customers. Track days sales outstanding (DSO) trends specific to those accounts.

- Stress-test the supplier’s liquidity for a scenario where the aggregator cuts orders by 25 percent for two quarters.

- Monitor industry news on brand consolidation. Each acquisition by Mammoth Brands reduces the pool of potential customers for its suppliers.

- Build a network of information sharing among lenders. A supplier’s delayed payment from one aggregator may foreshadow a broader trend. Shared data on payment behavior reduces information asymmetry.

**The Bottom Line for Operators**

Mammoth Brands’ ambition to become a CPG giant is a microcosm of a larger market shift. DTC aggregators are becoming the mid-market version of Procter & Gamble and Unilever, but without the decades of balance sheet strength. Their growth is financed by supplier working capital and private equity leverage. Small business lenders who ignore this dynamic will underwrite risk they cannot see.

Funders who adapt their models to account for aggregator concentration will capture a defensible niche. Brokers who educate their clients on the importance of customer diversification will build loyalty. ISOs who develop products that align with the aggregator’s payment cycles will own a growing origination channel.

The signal is clear: treat the aggregator as a shadow obligor for every supplier loan. The era of the independent DTC brand is ending. The era of the CPG holding company has begun. Underwrite accordingly.

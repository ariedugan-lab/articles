Kohl's was once the safe middle ground of American retail—a place where suburban families bought everything from sneakers to kitchen towels. Today, the chain is fighting for survival. Same-store sales have declined for six consecutive quarters. Its stock is down more than 70% from its 2021 peak. The company is closing underperforming locations, cutting inventory, and testing smaller-format stores.

For small business lenders, this distress is not a curiosity. It is a signal. Kohl's operates in hundreds of strip malls and shopping centers across the U.S. In many of those centers, Kohl's is the anchor tenant—the store that generates the foot traffic that keeps smaller retailers, nail salons, dry cleaners, and fast-casual restaurants alive. When an anchor tenant weakens, the entire ecosystem of dependent small businesses feels the pressure.

This is not a niche problem. Over 40% of small retail and service businesses in the U.S. are located in shopping centers anchored by a department store or big-box retailer. When those anchors stumble, lease renegotiations trigger co-tenancy clauses, foot traffic drops, and working capital cycles get squeezed. Lenders that ignore this signal are underwriting blind.

**The Co-Tenancy Trigger**

Most commercial leases in multi-tenant retail properties include co-tenancy clauses. These clauses allow smaller tenants to reduce rent, terminate leases early, or demand compensation if the anchor tenant closes or reduces its footprint. Kohl's has already closed over 50 stores in the past two years and has announced additional closures in 2026. Every closure activates co-tenancy provisions for the remaining tenants in that center.

For the small business lender, this means cash flow risk that standard financial statements do not capture. A nail salon in a strip mall anchored by a shuttered Kohl's may see its monthly rent drop by 30% due to co-tenancy relief. That sounds positive—lower expenses. But the same salon will see foot traffic decline by 40% or more. Revenue drops faster than rent. The result is a net working capital squeeze that shows up three to six months after the anchor closure.

Funders and brokers who originate loans to businesses in anchored retail centers must start tracking the health of the anchor as a data point. Pull the property owner's tenant roster. Check the anchor's corporate earnings calls. Monitor store closure announcements. Include a co-tenancy risk score in your underwriting checklist.

**The Foot Traffic Dependency Multiplier**

Kohl's average store generates about 1.5 million visits per year. Strip mall tenants in the same center typically capture 5% to 15% of that traffic. When Kohl's weakens, it does not just close stores—it reduces hours, cuts inventory, and runs fewer promotions. Traffic declines even before a closure is announced. Small businesses that rely on that spillover traffic see revenue drops of 10% to 25% long before the anchor actually leaves.

This creates a lagging indicator problem for lenders. Your borrower's bank statements might show solid revenue today because the anchor is still open. But if Kohl's is cutting marketing spend and reducing operating hours, the foot traffic decline is already in motion. By the time it hits the borrower's top line, the loan may already be impaired.

To address this, incorporate foot traffic data from location analytics providers (Placer.ai or similar) into your credit review. If the anchor tenant's visit count drops by 15% year-over-year, flag that borrower for a deeper cash flow stress test. Consider requiring a reserve or adjusting the advance rate.

**Vendor Financing Risk in the Supply Chain**

Kohl's turnaround strategy includes shifting more shelf space to national brands and pulling back on private label goods. That decision ripples through the supply chain. Small manufacturers and distributors that supplied Kohl's private label apparel, home goods, and accessories face sudden order reductions. Those businesses often rely on factoring or inventory financing to operate. When orders from a 500-store chain get cut by 20%, the working capital gap can be existential.

For brokers and ISOs serving the wholesale and manufacturing sectors, this is a direct underwriting signal. Ask any borrower who lists Kohl's as a top customer: What percentage of revenue comes from that account? Is the account under a long-term contract or at-will? Have they received any notice of reduced purchase orders? If the answer is vague or absent, assume risk is elevated.

**The Real Estate Roll-Up Opportunity**

Not every signal from Kohl's distress is negative. Some small businesses will benefit. When a weak anchor closes, the property owner often re-leases the space to a stronger tenant—a gym, a grocery store, a discount retailer. The transition period, however, creates six to eighteen months of disruption. Lenders with capital ready to fund working capital lines or equipment leases for small businesses during that rebuild phase can capture higher yields. The key is timing. Fund early before the new tenant opens, but only after verifying the replacement lease is signed.

Brokers can position themselves as advisors by helping borrowers anticipate the transition. If you have a client in a center where Kohl's is rumored to close, start the conversation now. Do not wait until the borrower is in distress. Proactive restructuring—converting a term loan to a flexible line of credit, extending amortization—can prevent defaults and deepen the client relationship.

**What This Means for Underwriting Models**

Most small business lenders use cash flow models that assume revenue is a function of the borrower's own operations. That assumption breaks down in tenant-dependent retail ecosystems. The health of the anchor tenant is an external variable that directly drives borrower performance. Ignoring it is like underwriting a restaurant without considering the weather on its patio season.

Concrete adjustments to make:

1. Add a geographic overlay to your portfolio. Map your exposure by shopping center. Identify which centers have department-store anchors. Rank those anchors by their corporate credit rating (or at least by recent earnings trends).
2. For any borrower in a center with a distressed anchor, require covenant reporting on monthly foot traffic or year-over-year revenue comps. Set a trigger at a 15% decline that activates a review.
3. For new originations in such centers, reduce the maximum advance rate by 10% or require personal guarantees if the anchor's EBITDA is negative.
4. For factoring and invoice finance, flag any receivable where the debtor is Kohl's or a related entity. Implement a concentration limit—no more than 15% of the factored book from any single retail chain.
5. Train your underwriting team to read retail earnings reports. You do not need to be an analyst, but you need to know when a major tenant is bleeding.

**The Bottom Line for Operators**

Kohl's is not alone. JCPenney, Macy's, Sears—the list of struggling department store anchors is long. Each one creates a cascade of risk for tens of thousands of small businesses. Lenders who treat retail distress as a single-company story are missing the systemic exposure. The anchor tenant trap is quiet. It does not show up on a balance sheet. It shows up in the borrower's back account six months later.

Funders: Audit your portfolio for anchor tenant dependency today. Do not wait for the next closure announcement.

Brokers: Use this as a value-add conversation. Show your borrowers that you understand the risks they cannot see. That builds trust and repeat business.

ISOs: Source leads in centers where anchors are stable or improving. Your conversion rates will be higher, and your default rates lower.

The signals are already on the tape. Read them before the loss arrives.

The merchant cash advance industry has been operating in a regulatory gray zone for the better part of a decade. As of February 17, 2026, in New York, that gray zone is gone.

Governor Hochul's signature on the **Fostering Affordability and Integrity through Reasonable Business Practices Act** — the FAIR Act — represents the first material rewrite of New York General Business Law § 349 in over forty years. Two changes matter for anyone funding, brokering, or originating against small businesses:

1. The Attorney General can now pursue enforcement actions against **"unfair"** and **"abusive"** practices — not only the narrower **"deceptive"** standard that has governed § 349 since the 1980s.
2. The statute's protections now **extend to businesses and nonprofits**, not just individual consumers. The "consumer-oriented conduct" carve-out that funders have leaned on for years is dead.

That second point is the structural one. For years, the standard funder defense to a § 349 claim was simple: this is a B2B transaction, the statute doesn't reach us. The legislature explicitly closed that door. Small business obligors are now consumers for purposes of unfair and abusive practice enforcement.

The MCA market hit roughly **$19.65 billion in 2025** and is on a path to **$26.87 billion by 2030**, per industry estimates. None of that growth pauses for the FAIR Act — but the unit economics, underwriting posture, and operational discipline required to participate in it just changed.

## What the AG can now look at

Read the statute through the lens of an enforcement lawyer, not a compliance officer, and the practical attack surface looks like this:

- **Confessions of judgment** filed without meaningful disclosure or filed as a matter of course. Not as a remedy of last resort.
- **Stacking** — funding a merchant who is already paying out to two, three, four other funders, with daily ACH withdrawals that demonstrably exceed deposit volume. The pattern, not the individual deal, becomes the abusive practice.
- **Reconciliation provisions that don't reconcile.** Boilerplate language promising true-ups that the funder, in practice, never honors. Courts have already been moving here. The AG now has a parallel lane.
- **Account freezes** triggered by missed daily debits without a cure period or merchant communication.
- **Broker conduct** the funder enabled or failed to police — misrepresentation of factor rates as APRs, undisclosed origination fees, opaque assignment chains.

None of this requires new case law. It requires the AG to look at portfolios already on the ground.

## The judicial backdrop is converging too

The FAIR Act doesn't exist in isolation. New York courts have been steadily building a body of case law around when an MCA is, in substance, a loan — and therefore subject to the state's **criminal usury cap of 25%**. The triangulation matters:

- **Fixed daily payments** with no genuine reconciliation,
- **Full recourse** against the merchant including personal guarantees,
- **No real revenue-share economics** — the funder is paid the same whether the merchant has a record month or a closure.

When all three are present, the recharacterization argument is no longer fringe. It's the default analysis. Layer the FAIR Act on top of that and the funder defending one of these advances in 2026 is fighting on two fronts: a usury argument in front of a judge and an unfair-practice argument from the AG's office.

## The operator read: this is an underwriting problem

The reflex move for the industry will be marketing posture — disclosures, disclaimers, attorney-reviewed contract language. That misses the point. The FAIR Act doesn't punish bad copy. It punishes patterns of behavior. Three operational shifts separate the funders who will compound through this cycle from the ones who will spend it in litigation:

**1. Re-underwrite the stack, not just the merchant.**
Bank statement parsing has been good enough to detect existing MCA debits for years. Stop relying on broker disclosures. If a merchant is already supporting two daily withdrawals that consume more than 12-15% of average deposit volume, the question isn't pricing — it's whether to fund at all. Stacking risk is now a **regulatory** input, not just a credit input.

**2. Make reconciliation real or take it out of the contract.**
Every MCA agreement on the market has a reconciliation clause. A vanishingly small number of funders actually run them. The FAIR Act makes that gap actionable. Pick a path: build the operational muscle to honor true-ups against actual receipts on a defined cadence, or restructure the product so reconciliation isn't promised. The middle ground — boilerplate without operations — is now the most expensive position you can hold.

**3. Tier your broker network.**
The funder is liable for the conduct of the channel. Brokers who misrepresent factor rates, hide stacking, or pressure merchants into confessions of judgment are now creating direct AG exposure for the funders they sell to. The economics of cheap broker volume just inverted. The compliant broker network is suddenly a moat, not a cost center.

## What this means outside New York

New York moves first, but it does not move alone. California's commercial financing disclosure regime is already live. Connecticut has been actively scrutinizing the space — NPR's reporting on cash-advance account freezes earlier this year drew direct legislative attention. Florida and Virginia have working groups studying the issue.

Funders who treat the FAIR Act as a New York-only problem will rebuild their compliance stack three more times before 2028. Funders who treat New York as the template — and standardize underwriting, reconciliation, and broker oversight to the strictest applicable standard — will be the ones still originating at scale when the patchwork resolves.

## The bottom line

The MCA category isn't going away. Small businesses still need fast capital that banks won't underwrite. The product solves a real problem. But the operating cost of running the product has gone up, permanently, in the largest commercial credit market in the country.

The funders, ISOs, and broker networks who treat the FAIR Act as a forcing function — to clean up underwriting, instrument reconciliation, and own their channel — will end this cycle with better books, lower charge-offs, and pricing power. The ones who treat it as a marketing problem will be reading their next set of headlines from a deposition transcript.

Compliance is no longer the brake on origination. It's the moat around it.

On July 21, 2026, Novo Nordisk filed a lawsuit against Eli Lilly, asking a federal court to permanently stop Lilly from running what Novo calls deceptive advertisements for its GLP-1 drugs. The suit demands corrective advertising. For small business lenders, this is not a pharma squabble. It is a concrete risk signal for a fast-growing lending vertical: weight-loss clinics, compounding pharmacies, and telehealth providers that depend on GLP-1 prescriptions.

The advertising war between the two dominant GLP-1 manufacturers is escalating. Clinics and providers, many of them small businesses, have ridden the wave of patient demand fueled by direct-to-consumer ads. But the lawsuit reveals that this demand is not purely organic. It is manufactured, contested, and now legally fragile. When the advertising engine stutters, the cash flow from those patients may stall.

## The patient acquisition dependency

Weight-loss clinics and telehealth platforms spend heavily on marketing to attract GLP-1 patients. A typical clinic in this space allocates 20-35% of revenue to digital ads, search engine optimization, and influencer partnerships. The cost to acquire a patient can exceed $500. That expenditure is a bet on a steady stream of new prescriptions. But if regulatory action forces Lilly to pull or alter its ads, the entire demand pipeline shifts. Competitors like Novo Nordisk may gain ground, but they also face tightened scrutiny. The net effect is uncertainty in patient volume.

For a lender, the key underwriting question shifts from "Is the clinic growing?" to "How much of that growth came from contested advertising?". A clinic that relies on Lilly's marketing to fill its pipeline may see new patient acquisition drop by 40% or more if Lilly is forced to run corrective ads or reduce spend. That drop directly impacts working capital cycles. The clinic has already spent money on staff, rent, and inventory. Without the ad-driven influx, cash burn accelerates.

## Regulatory exposure as a credit risk

The lawsuit is not an isolated event. It signals that the FDA and the Federal Trade Commission are watching the GLP-1 advertising space closely. Corrective advertising orders, fines, or even clinical trial data disputes can ripple through the entire referral ecosystem. Small providers lack the legal budgets of Novo or Lilly. They cannot afford to defend a deceptive marketing claim. Their exposure is indirect but real: if a clinic uses marketing materials that reference Lilly's claims, and those claims are deemed deceptive, the clinic faces potential liability.

Lenders must now ask: Does the borrower have a compliance review process for marketing materials? Are they using third-party ad copy that could be retroactively challenged? Any legal action that ties a small provider to deceptive advertising can result in loan default, asset seizure, or reputational damage that makes future funding impossible.

## The concentration trap

Many small business lenders have been enthusiastic about the GLP-1 vertical because of high margins and strong demand. But the lawsuit reveals a concentration trap: too many clinics depend on the same advertising channels and same branded drugs. If Lilly's marketing is constrained, patients may shift to alternative providers or opt for non-pharmaceutical weight loss programs. The clinic's revenue concentration in GLP-1 scripts becomes a liability.

For funders, this means the underwriting model must include a "marketing concentration" metric. A clinic that gets 70% of its new patients from a single ad channel or a single drug brand's marketing wave is a riskier proposition than one with diversified patient acquisition. The weight-loss clinic that relies on TikTok ads referencing Lilly's drugs may be overexposed.

## Implications for brokers and ISOs

Loan brokers and ISO networks that originate deals for weight-loss clinics need to adjust their deal packaging. The standard credit memo that highlights rapid revenue growth and high margins must now also address marketing dependency and legal exposure. Brokers who ignore these factors will see higher default rates in their referral portfolios. Funders will start to price for this risk, demanding higher rates or reduced advance amounts for clinics that cannot demonstrate a diversified patient acquisition strategy.

Brokers can differentiate themselves by asking clinics about their ad spend, legal review processes, and alternative patient sources. A clinic that has a stable referral base from primary care physicians, for example, is less vulnerable to the ad war than one that buys all its leads from Facebook. Sharing that insight with funders builds trust and reduces future chargebacks.

## The corrective advertising risk

Novo Nordisk's demand for corrective advertising is the most concrete threat. If the court orders Lilly to run corrected ads, the narrative of the drug class shifts. Patients who were motivated by Lilly's claims may lose confidence. Some may stop treatment altogether. For clinics, this means a potential drop in prescription renewals, not just new starts. Recurring revenue from ongoing patients is the backbone of clinic cash flow. A widespread loss of confidence could cause a 15-20% decline in monthly recurring revenue for clinics heavily linked to Lilly's marketing.

Lenders should model this scenario. Run a stress test: what happens to the clinic's debt service coverage ratio if new patient volume drops by 30% and recurring revenue drops by 15%? If the clinic cannot survive that shock, the loan is overleveraged.

## Practical underwriting adjustments

1. **Review marketing spend as a percentage of revenue.** Anything above 25% should trigger a deeper review. Ask for a breakdown by channel. If more than half of that spend is on ads that directly reference a single drug maker's marketing, flag it.

2. **Request a legal compliance statement.** Have the borrower certify that their marketing materials have been reviewed for accuracy and that they have a process to update them if regulatory action occurs. This is not about legal guarantees but about awareness.

3. **Measure patient source diversity.** Ask for a breakdown of how new patients found the clinic. A healthy clinic gets patients from multiple channels: referrals, organic search, social media, paid ads, insurance networks. If paid ads represent more than 40%, the clinic is vulnerable.

4. **Incorporate a regulatory watch list.** Track lawsuits involving GLP-1 manufacturers. A new lawsuit or FDA warning is a trigger to review the borrower's exposure. This is like monitoring a supplier's credit rating in a supply chain loan.

5. **Adjust cash flow projections for corrective advertising scenarios.** For clinics that market heavily with GLP-1 claims, build in a 20% revenue decline scenario for the first six months after a major advertising restriction. If the loan cannot survive that scenario, require additional collateral or a personal guaranty.

## The opportunity in the noise

Not all clinics are equally exposed. Those that operate via insurance-reimbursed medical weight loss programs, or those that have built a brand independent of any single drug maker, will weather the ad war. For lenders, this is a chance to separate strong operators from those riding a marketing wave. The clinics that survive will be those with deep patient relationships and diversified revenue.

For funders, the lawsuit is a reminder that the hottest lending verticals can turn cold quickly when the marketing machine that feeds them faces a legal challenge. The GLP-1 advertising war is not a reason to exit the vertical. It is a reason to underwrite with precision. Operators who treat this as a signal to tighten terms and ask harder questions will protect their portfolios. Those who ignore the lawsuit may find themselves funding clinics that have no patients left to serve.

The takeaway is direct: patient acquisition cost is not just a marketing metric. It is a credit risk metric. When the ads stop, the cash flow stops. Underwrite accordingly.

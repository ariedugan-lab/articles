**The Footwear Markdown Cascade**

Dick's Sporting Goods reported a 15% stock drop after missing Q2 expectations. Management blamed a 'challenging' footwear market. For small business lenders, that phrase is a warning signal, not a footnote.

Dick's is the largest U.S. sporting goods retailer. When it is forced to discount footwear, pricing power evaporates downstream. Independent sneaker shops, regional sports retailers, footwear repair businesses, and category-specific online sellers all operate in the same demand pool. If a big-box anchor is marking down inventory to move units, small merchants must do the same or lose traffic. Revenue may stay flat. Gross profit will not. That is the exact environment where revenue-based lending products become dangerous.

## The Markdown Cascade

Retail footwear is not a stable necessity. It is a fashion-adjacent, seasonal, trend-driven category. When Dick's says the market is challenging, it means demand softened at a specific price point or the product mix missed. The immediate response is promotional activity, which ripples through every channel.

For a small footwear merchant, the cascade works like this:

- The merchant placed a seasonal buy at wholesale prices set months earlier.
- A large chain puts comparable product on sale to protect market share.
- The merchant discounts to match, reducing the contribution margin.
- The merchant's sales volume may stay healthy, but cash generated per transaction drops.
- When inventory has to be cleared, the merchant sells at or below cost.
- If the original purchase was financed by supplier terms or a cash advance, the repayment obligation stays fixed.

That sequence creates a hidden liability. A lender looking at bank deposits sees a business that is still active. The P&L may not yet show a loss. But the cash flow buffer that absorbs debt service is gone.

## Revenue Quality vs Revenue Quantity

For lenders and funders, this is a revenue quality problem. Standard underwriting using revenue, bank balance, and credit score cannot see margin compression. It only sees the top line. The Dick's announcement is a reason to add footwear-specific revenue quality checks, especially for merchants with a large percentage of revenue tied to athletic footwear.

Start by measuring footwear exposure. If a merchant derives more than 30% to 40% of revenue from footwear, run a markdown stress test. Model what happens to cash flow if average gross margin falls from 45% to 30% for two quarters. That is not a far-fetched scenario in a big-box footwear promotion cycle.

Then ask for margin-relevant data:

- Cost of goods sold, not just sales totals.
- Inventory turnover in days, not just current inventory level.
- Average transaction size by month. Falling average tickets with stable transaction count is a discounting indicator.
- Refund and chargeback rates. Footwear returns spike when customers are buying price first, fit second.
- Accounts payable aging. Merchants under margin pressure often stretch supplier payments before they draw down cash.

These data points belong in every renewal and approval file for footwear-exposed borrowers.

## What 'Challenging' Means in Underwriting Terms

Dick's did not say footwear is dead. It said the current market is difficult. That can mean:

- Demand shifted to other categories, so existing inventory is stale.
- Promotional pricing is heavier for larger competitors.
- Inventory builds in the wholesale channel, forcing closeouts.
- Brand strength is dividing: strong products sell through, weak products need discounts.

For small business lenders, the result is bifurcation. A boutique that sells limited-run or specialty footwear can still thrive. A retailer competing on price with big-box inventory is now a higher-risk borrower. Technology and business analytics can differentiate the two.

Advisors and ISO networks should not treat all footwear merchants as a single vertical. They should segment by product mix, brand, price point, and reliance on markdowns. The same NAICS code category may contain two merchants with completely different risk profiles.

## The Broker and ISO Playbook

Brokers and ISO networks need to act before file submission. If you have footwear merchants in your pipeline, collect the following before sending to a funder:

- Percentage of revenue from footwear.
- Top three shoe brands and their wholesale relationships.
- Gross margin estimate based on cost of goods and retail pricing.
- Inventory days, or a simple count of aged stock.
- Whether the merchant has already changed pricing in response to big-box promotions.

This is not busywork. It protects funders from unprofitable advances and protects brokers from submitting deals that will early default. In a market where a major player loses 15% of market value in a day, the downstream risk is immediate. The next few quarters are when footwear merchants will look for capital. Some will need capital for the right reason: to diversify into profitable categories. Others will need capital because their margin compression has left them short. A broker's job is to identify which case is real.

## Opportunity for Diversification Lending

There is a real opportunity in the markdown cascade. Footwear merchants with strong operations may need working capital to shift into sports apparel, outdoor gear, team sports equipment, or accessories. That type of pivot is not a blanket merchant cash advance. It requires a structured product:

- A term loan or asset-based line tied to ecommerce, equipment, or fixtures, not inventory.
- A covenant requiring a store-level margin floor and a plan for disposing of aged footwear.
- A repayment model based on profit contribution, not gross sales.

Lenders that can build this structure will win relationships from distressed footwear merchants. Lenders that only offer percentage-of-sales advances will underwrite the top line and eat the margin risk.

## Geographic and Market Niche Signals

Footwear demand is local. Before funding a footwear merchant, check the local competitive landscape. If a Dick's, Academy Sports, or Hibbett location near the merchant is expanding or shifting to clearance, that is a negative signal. If a local running store is tied to a fitness community and sells services, subscriptions, and race events, that is more resilient than a general sneaker retailer.

In addition, watch brand-specific signals. Nike, Adidas, New Balance, and other major suppliers are changing direct-to-consumer strategies and allocation rules. Smaller retailers that lose wholesale access are forced into secondary channels. That is not a temporary issue. It changes the merchant's ability to source quality inventory at a profitable cost.

## Actionable Underwriting Standard

For funders and operators, add a footwear stress test to your standard retail underwriting process. The test should be triggered by any of these conditions:

- The merchant's name, processing descriptor, or business code is a footwear or sporting goods retailer.
- More than 30% of revenue comes from shoe sales.
- The merchant's average ticket is declining while transaction count is flat.
- The merchant's location is within a 5-mile radius of a big-box sporting goods store.
- The merchant's inventory days are above 90 and rising.

If any of those conditions exist, require:

1. A current inventory aging report.
2. A gross margin estimate.
3. A written plan for handling a big-box markdown event.
4. A repayment structure based on profit and operating cash flow, not just card processing volume.

This is not a one-off adjustment. The Dick's announcement shows that a major retailer's product problem becomes a small business financing problem. Lenders who ignore that connection will fund unprofitable merchants and call them performing until the cash buffer is gone.

## The Bottom Line for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs

The footwear markdown cascade is a new reason to re-evaluate every retailer in the athletic goods ecosystem. Dick's 15% stock drop is not a stock market event. It is a pricing signal that travels through inventory channels, into small business margins, and straight into loan performance.

Use it as a trigger to tighten underwriting, update broker submission standards, and build lending products that reward real cash flow rather than top-line revenue. Footwear is a large, consumer-driven category. In the current cycle, it is also a category where revenue can mask serious cash flow damage. The lenders that account for that damage will reduce defaults. The brokers that document it will protect their networks. The funders that ignore it will inherit the margin risk of a challenging footwear market.

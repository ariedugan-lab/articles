Costco reported record-breaking gasoline volumes in its Q3 2026 earnings, driven by rising fuel prices that pushed first-time members to its pumps. The company’s ability to turn a macro headwind into a customer acquisition event is instructive. But for small business lenders, the real signal is what Costco’s model hides: most small businesses cannot replicate the membership-moat buffer that lets big-box retailers absorb fuel cost spikes without crushing margins.

This gap creates a measurable underwriting blind spot. When fuel prices rise, the businesses most likely to default are those with high fuel-cost-to-revenue ratios, low pass-through pricing power, and thin working capital reserves. Lenders that fail to quantify this exposure will see unexpected losses in portfolios that look healthy on standard cash-flow metrics.

**The Costco Earnings Signal in Context**

Costco’s gasoline business is a loss leader with a purpose. The company sells fuel near or below market cost, taking a margin hit that is subsidized by membership fees and high-volume merchandise sales. Rising pump prices actually strengthen the model by attracting price-sensitive drivers who become members. In Q3 2026, CFO Gary Millerchip noted that gas volume “reached record levels” and that higher fuel prices drove “a meaningful number of new members.”

Compare this to a small independent gas station operator or a local delivery fleet. They cannot sell below cost to gain customers. They have no membership fee to offset margin compression. Their fuel cost is a direct variable expense that must be passed through to end customers or absorbed. When prices jump 15% in a quarter, the small operator faces an immediate working capital squeeze. Inventory costs rise before receivables adjust. The result is a cash flow gap that standard underwriting models often miss because they treat fuel cost as a stable, historically averaged line item.

**The Underwriting Blind Spot**

Most small business underwriting relies on trailing twelve-month revenue, net income, and debt service coverage ratios. These backward-looking metrics assume cost structures are stable and linear. Fuel costs are neither stable nor linear. A landscaping company that spent 8% of revenue on fuel in a $3.50/gallon environment may see that ratio jump to 12% at $4.50/gallon. The same business may have zero ability to raise prices mid-contract. The cash flow shortfall shows up first in delayed payments to suppliers and then in loan servicers.

The blind spot is amplified for businesses that operate on thin margins and high fuel intensity: food trucks, last-mile delivery services, logistics subcontractors, non-chain gas stations, agricultural service providers, and HVAC/plumbing fleets. These operators often have strong top-line growth but weak working capital buffers. A fuel price surge can erase 30 to 60 days of operating cash flow.

Standard debt service coverage ratio calculations that use average fuel cost from a prior period artificially inflate the borrower’s capacity. Lenders who do not stress-test fuel sensitivity are pricing risk as if fuel volatility does not exist.

**A New Underwriting Metric: Fuel Cost Penetration Rate**

To address this gap, funders and ISO networks should adopt a simple but powerful metric: Fuel Cost Penetration Rate (FCPR). Calculate it as the percentage of gross revenue consumed by direct fuel expenses over the most recent three-month period. Benchmark against the same period one year ago and against industry averages.

For high-FCPR borrowers, require a fuel price sensitivity table that shows the impact of a 10%, 20%, and 30% fuel price increase on operating cash flow. Use this table to set advance rates, interest spreads, or payment frequencies. A borrower whose cash flow turns negative at a 15% fuel price increase is a different risk than one who can absorb a 30% increase.

Concrete implications by role:

- **Funders:** Update your credit application to include a dedicated fuel expense line item. Do not rely on broad COGS figures. Request fuel purchase invoices or fuel card statements for the past six months. For borrowers with FCPR above 10%, require a fuel price hedge or a working capital reserve equal to one month of fuel costs.

- **Brokers:** When originating deals for transportation, food service, or field-service businesses, proactively ask about fuel cost exposure. A broker who can provide a fuel sensitivity analysis as part of the package will get faster underwriting decisions and better pricing. Use the Costco earnings story as a conversation starter to show you understand the true risk.

- **ISO Networks:** Design training modules that teach partners to spot fuel-vulnerable borrowers. Create a standard fuel-cost-rider clause that can be added to merchant cash advance agreements, allowing payment amounts to float with fuel price indices. This reduces default risk while keeping funding accessible.

**Opportunity: Fuel-Linked Working Capital Products**

The same vulnerability that creates risk also creates product opportunity. Fuel price volatility generates demand for short-term capital to cover the gap between rising input costs and lagging receivables. Lenders who can structure fuel-linked working capital advances—tied to fuel price indices, with repayment schedules that adjust when prices fall—will capture a high-margin niche.

Consider a merchant cash advance product for independent gas station owners whose revenue is positively correlated with fuel prices. When pump prices rise, their top-line revenue increases, but their margin per gallon stays flat or shrinks. A standard MCA based on credit card receipts may over-lend during a price spike, then leave the borrower underwater when prices drop. A fuel-indexed advance that ties advance size to long-term average margin rather than peak revenue avoids that trap.

Similarly, for delivery fleets, offer invoice factoring with a fuel-cost adjustment that accelerates payment when fuel prices exceed a trigger threshold. That keeps cash in the operator’s hands during spike periods without adding permanent debt.

**The Costco Benchmark for Small Business Lending**

Costco’s gas business works because membership fees provide a structural buffer against fuel price fluctuation. Small business lenders cannot replicate membership fees, but they can replicate the discipline of pricing for volatility. That means underwriting for the worst-case fuel scenario, not the average.

Use the Costco earnings as a warning signal. When a company with massive scale, low fuel margins, and a captive membership base describes gasoline volumes as “record-breaking” due to rising prices, the implication for unhedged small businesses is clear: their cash flow stress is at least as severe, and their ability to withstand it is far weaker.

Lenders who ignore this signal will see rising delinquencies in portfolios that otherwise look strong. Those who integrate fuel cost sensitivity into their underwriting models will capture lower-risk borrowers, develop differentiated products, and build lending relationships that survive the next fuel price cycle.

**Actionable next steps for funders and brokers:**

1. Review current portfolio exposure to industries with FCPR above 10%. Flag any borrower in logistics, food trucks, landscaping, or independent fuel retail for fuel stress testing.

2. Update credit applications to require trailing three months of fuel expense data. If the borrower cannot provide it, treat that as a red flag.

3. Develop a fuel-cost sensitivity table template that brokers can use in deal submissions. Standardize the assessment across your network.

4. Consider launching a fuel-linked working capital product pilot in Q3 2026, targeting businesses in regions with the highest fuel price volatility (e.g., West Coast, Northeast).

5. Monitor Costco’s gasoline margin disclosures in future earnings calls as a real-time indicator of consumer fuel price sensitivity. When the membership moat gets tested, small business risk gets amplified.

The message is direct: Costco’s record gas volumes are not a retail curiosity. They are a signal that fuel price volatility is reshaping small business cash flow in ways most underwriting models have not yet accounted for. Operators who treat this as an underwriting event, not just a news story, will lead the next cycle of B2B lending performance.

When a prime contractor sells subsidiaries to a startup and accepts an equity stake, the deal is not a headline. It is a credit event for every small business supplier attached to those entities. Boeing's agreement to sell three eVTOL subsidiaries to Archer Aviation in exchange for an undisclosed stake is the newest example of a large company converting physical operations into stock. Lenders serving the aerospace supply chain need to treat this transaction as a receivables risk event, not a market-expansion story.

### The counterparty changed. The invoice did not.

A small machine shop that has invoiced Boeing's eVTOL business for the last two years does not wake up with a new invoice system. The same legal entity name may appear on purchase orders. The bank account might not change. But the parent that stood behind those invoices is no longer Boeing. In a divestiture, ownership of the entity transfers. If Boeing did not guarantee the subsidiary's payables, the supplier's credit exposure now sits with a startup whose balance sheet is built on equity, not cash flow.

The 'undisclosed stake' in Archer matters. Boeing accepted stock in a startup as payment. That stock is not working capital for the eVTOL subsidiaries. It is a payment to Boeing. The subsidiaries do not automatically get new liquidity. They get a new owner with a mandate to integrate them into an Archer platform. For suppliers, payment terms remain the same, but the probability of collection is tied to Archer's cash runway.

### The same-entity trap

Underwriting systems often rely on customer names and account histories. In a divestiture, the name stays but the risk changes. Lenders must update their counterparty mapping. Ask borrowers for the current ownership structure of their largest customers. Require that any contract with an aerospace subsidiary include a change-of-control clause. If the clause is missing, assume the receivable is riskier.

Concentration recalculation is now mandatory. If a borrower has 30% of receivables from a subsidiary formerly owned by Boeing, that concentration should be downgraded. The size of the exposure did not change. The credit quality did. A lender that continues to advance 85% against those invoices is now lending against an unrated startup's ability to pay.

### What to look for in Archer's capital structure

Archer is acquiring the subsidiaries by issuing equity. The absence of cash in the transaction is the most important signal. If Boeing, a company with capital markets access, was willing to take stock instead of cash, the subsidiaries' cash-generating ability likely could not fund a cash sale. Lenders need to know Archer's cash balance, burn rate, and next funding milestone. If the startup's runway is shorter than the supplier's payment terms, the invoice is tenuous.

Do not treat the Boeing stake as collateral. Boeing taking a stake can also be read as Boeing retaining upside without retaining payables risk. The deal separates the supplier base from Boeing's balance sheet while giving Boeing option value. From a lender's perspective, that separation is a downgrade.

### The new vertical: advanced air mobility lending

The divestiture also clarifies the shape of the eVTOL supply chain. Archer will need suppliers. Those suppliers are small businesses. They make motor windings, battery enclosures, composite fuselage sections, flight controllers, wiring harnesses, and test fixtures. They also provide certification support, flight-test instrumentation, and charging infrastructure installation. Each of those business types has working capital needs: long payment cycles, milestone billing, inventory for prototype runs, and specialist labor costs.

This vertical is not bankable by hype. Lenders who want to originate it need a specific underwriting framework. Start with the borrower's customer list, not a press release. Approved invoices must be tied to a clear statement of work and a confirmed purchase order. The purchase order must name the paying entity and include acceptance criteria. If the customer's contract says payment is on certification milestones, a routine accounts-receivable advance is not the right product.

### What funders should do now

Run a portfolio screen for any borrower with NAICS codes in aerospace product and parts manufacturing, navigational instruments, or avionics. Ask for accounts receivable aging and the legal names of their top five customers.

Check whether any top customer is a subsidiary of a company that has been sold. Public transaction announcements happen after the fact; the borrower may not know yet. The lender can be the one to flag it.

Re-underwrite all advances where the payor is an eVTOL startup or a recently acquired subsidiary. The advance rate should reflect the startup's cash runway, not the legacy parent's balance sheet.

If the supplier's contract allows payment in equity or contains warrants, exclude that potential upside from the borrowing base. The value is speculative and illiquid.

### What brokers and ISO networks should do

For brokers, this is a client retention event. Call every aerospace supply chain client. Ask if they have received a notice of acquisition or a new customer portal for any Boeing eVTOL division. If they have, explain the receivables risk and offer to connect them with a funder who understands the change. That proactive call positions the broker as a credit advisor, not just a lead source.

For ISO networks, build a campaign around advanced air mobility suppliers. The vertical is narrow but funded. There is grant and defense money flowing toward eVTOL certification and infrastructure. Independent sales organizations should develop a list of suppliers that have publicly announced contracts with Archer, Joby, or other eVTOL platforms. Focus on companies with existing customers and paid invoices, not startups pitching prototypes.

### The bottom line

Boeing's equity stake in Archer is a financial engineering move. It shifts the risk of early-stage aviation from a large prime contractor to a startup and its supply chain. Small business lenders must not treat the Boeing name as a guarantee after that shift. The invoice remains an invoice. The borrower remains the same. But the true payor is now a startup with a cash runway and a stock price. Underwrite accordingly.

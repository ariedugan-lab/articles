# The Refund Allocation Test: How Retailer Tariff Refund Choices Rewrite Small-Business Cash-Flow Underwriting

The latest retail earnings cycle handed commercial lenders a controlled experiment in how business owners respond to unexpected cash. Tariff refunds hit retailer ledgers this quarter, and the responses diverged. Some retailers chose to lower prices. Others chose to boost margins. For small-business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks, the correct read is not whether a refund was received. The correct read is how the borrower deployed it.

The 'choose your own adventure' earnings moment is really an underwriting prompt. A tariff refund is a non-recurring cash flow. It arrives through a government process, not through customer demand. When a merchant receives one, the allocation decision reveals management quality, pricing power, and cash-flow sustainability. That is exactly the information a lender needs before setting advance rates, loan amounts, or covenant structures.

## Why allocation is a credit signal

Most underwriting models start with historical cash flow. Tariff refunds distort that starting point. If a borrower deposits a large refund and the lender treats it as ordinary operating income, every downstream calculation is corrupted. Average bank balances look stronger. Payback capacity looks higher. Leverage ratios look safer. In practice, the refund is a one-time event. It can support one seasonal peak or one debt repayment. It cannot support a revolving credit facility or a merchant cash advance that renews on expected future cash flow.

The bigger risk is not the check. It is what the check changes. A merchant who uses the refund to lower prices is making a strategic bet that volume will replace margin. A merchant who uses the refund to pad margins is making a bet that pricing power will hold without the refund next year. Both bets can be rational. Both can also be fatal. The lender's job is to identify which bet is being made and to underwrite the resulting cash-flow shape, not the refund-inflated historical shape.

## What price cutters are telling you

When a retailer or small importer passes a tariff refund through to customers, top-line revenue can look stable or even grow. Unit volume rises because prices fall. Gross margin shrinks. For revenue-based lenders, this is the dangerous combination. A merchant cash advance or royalty-linked loan is paid from future revenue. If revenue holds steady because prices dropped, the repayment source is intact in dollars but damaged in quality. Each future sale produces less margin to cover operating expenses and debt service.

Underwrite the price cutter on gross profit and EBITDA, not revenue. Ask whether the price cut is temporary, seasonal, or permanent. If the refund funded a promotional period, the revenue bump disappears when prices return. If the refund funded a permanent price reset, historical revenue is a poor baseline. Re-underwrite the business at the new margin profile before extending additional capital.

For brokers and ISOs, this is a file-level question. When an applicant shows rising revenue but flat or falling gross margin, ask for the refund allocation statement. If the answer is 'we cut prices,' the broker should place that merchant with a lender who uses cash-flow-based underwriting and can price for margin risk. Do not route a price cutter into a high-advance-rate product built on trailing revenue.

## What margin keepers are telling you

Retailers that hold the refund as margin are showing pricing discipline. That can signal brand strength, differentiated inventory, or a rational market. For a small business, it can also mean the owner treated a government payment as profit. Some will use the refund to pay down debt, which is a genuine positive. Others will distribute it to the owner, which is equivalent to a dividend recap funded by a non-recurring event.

A margin keeper with stable gross margin is not automatically a safe borrower. The refund created a one-time add-back to net income. If the lender normalizes EBITDA by adding back the refund as 'other income,' the next period will show a gap. That gap is the refund cliff. Debt service coverage computed on trailing twelve-month EBITDA will overstate capacity. The lender must project forward without the refund line and test whether the merchant can service debt from recurring gross profit alone.

For underwriting committees, the direction is simple. Separate refund income from operating income. Force a separate line item for tariff refunds and other government restitution. Then run the credit file twice: once with the refund included as a liquidity event, and once with it excluded as non-recurring. If the second run fails, the facility is sized incorrectly.

## Adjusting the borrower conversation

Every funder, broker, and ISO should add the refund allocation question to their standard due diligence checklist. The question is not 'did you receive a tariff refund?' The question is 'how did you allocate it, and what is the projected impact on gross margin and operating cash flow?'

A few concrete adjustments:

- Exclude one-time refunds and refund receivables from eligible revenue and EBITDA calculations.
- Ask for a cash-flow statement, not just a profit-and-loss report. The deposit is visible in bank statements, but the allocation decision shows up in gross margin and inventory behavior.
- Build a scenario where the refund is absent. If the merchant's recent performance is refund-dependent, reduce the facility size or require a personal guarantee.
- For MCA renewals, re-pull bank statements and compare revenue velocity after the refund period. A merchant whose deposits normalized below the refund-inflated baseline is a different credit.
- For brokers, classify merchants into three buckets: price reset, margin buffer, and debt reduction. Each bucket has a different lender fit.

## Where this bites hardest

Import-dependent small businesses are the primary exposure. Retailers, distributors, and e-commerce sellers that buy directly from overseas suppliers and receive refunds under Section 301 exclusions or customs protest decisions will show unusual cash-flow entries in 2026. Lenders with concentration in hardware, home improvement, electronics, apparel, and furniture should update underwriting memos now.

Also watch for expected refunds booked as receivables. Some merchants may apply for refunds that have not been paid. If the receivable is counted as an asset, its collectability is a risk. The refund process can take months and can be reversed. Lenders should discount or disallow uncollected refund receivables entirely.

## The operator takeaway

Tariff refunds are not free working capital. They are a one-time allocation event. The way a business owner handles that event is a reliable signal of how they will handle the next disruption. Lenders that underwrite the allocation decision will separate durable merchants from refund-dependent ones. Those that simply annualize the check will build a book of loans that look fine on paper and fail in the next period.

For funders, brokers, and ISO networks, the message is direct. Do not ask whether the refund landed. Ask what changed after it landed. The answer tells you how to set the advance rate, how to size the deal, and whether to approve the renewal.

American Airlines cut its 2026 earnings outlook again, directly citing higher fuel costs. The market reaction was swift, but the ripples extend far beyond airline stocks. For small business lenders, this event is not just a macro headline. It is a concrete signal to revisit a core underwriting assumption: the reliability of fuel cost pass-through clauses.

Most lenders already screen for fuel sensitivity in businesses like trucking, logistics, ground transportation, and even food distributors. Standard practice is to check if the borrower has pass-through agreements with customers that allow fuel surcharges. On paper, these clauses appear to protect margins. But American Airlines' guidance cut reveals a structural flaw in that logic. When fuel costs rise sharply and persistently, large counterparties renegotiate, delay, or simply refuse to pay pass-through amounts. The small business holding the clause becomes the buffer, not the beneficiary.

This is the fuel cost pass-through illusion. The clause exists, but its enforcement depends on market power, contract renewal timing, and the counterparty's own financial stress. American Airlines, facing a $3 billion profit gap, will seek cost savings everywhere — including squeezing suppliers and service providers who thought they were insulated by fuel surcharges. Lenders who underwrite solely on the presence of a pass-through clause are signing off on a phantom protection.

## The Mechanics of the Trap

Fuel surcharges are typically calculated based on a published index — for example, the U.S. Energy Information Administration's weekly diesel price or a specific airport jet fuel price. The small business adds a percentage or fixed amount to each invoice. In a stable or slowly-rising market, this works. The counterparty pays the surcharge because the amounts are small relative to the total invoice.

But when fuel spikes are abrupt and sustained, two things happen. First, the surcharge amount becomes a large fraction of the total invoice, triggering procurement review and pushback. Second, the counterparty — especially if it is a large corporation like an airline — will demand a renegotiation of the pass-through formula, cap the surcharge, or fold it into a new fixed price contract. The small business then must either accept lower margins or risk losing the contract entirely.

American Airlines' latest earnings cut proves that even a legacy carrier with immense purchasing power cannot fully absorb fuel costs. It will shift that pressure downstream. Any small business that counts American — or any major airline — as a material customer now faces a working capital squeeze that is invisible to standard cash flow models. The pass-through clause is only as strong as the counterparty's willingness to honor it.

## Why This Matters for Funders and Brokers

For funders, this introduces a new underwriting variable: pass-through enforceability. You cannot simply check a box on a loan application. You must evaluate the concentration risk, the contract renewal date, and the counterparty's own cost-cutting trajectory. A business that appears to have healthy margins on a trailing 12-month basis may see those margins evaporate within one fuel cycle if its primary customer is American Airlines or a similar carrier under earnings pressure.

Brokers and ISO networks should advise their borrower base to review customer contracts immediately. If a small business relies on fuel surcharges for its working capital buffer, the time to renegotiate is before the next fuel spike, not during. Brokers who surface this risk to their funders gain credibility and reduce the chance of a file being declined later due to sudden cash flow deterioration.

The implication is concrete: lenders should request the last 12 months of fuel surcharge invoices and compare them to the contractual formula. If the surcharge actually collected is consistently below the formula — or if the counterparty has a history of paying late on surcharges — that is a red flag. It signals that the pass-through is not real. The borrower is actually absorbing fuel costs and hiding them in accounts receivable.

## A New Underwriting Signal: The Fuel Surcharge Gap Ratio

We propose a specific metric for lenders to incorporate: the Fuel Surcharge Gap Ratio. Calculate the difference between the contractual pass-through amount (based on the index) and the actual surcharge collected, divided by total revenue over the same period. A gap above 1% of revenue indicates structural under-collection. A gap growing over consecutive quarters suggests the counterparty is squeezing the borrower. This is a leading indicator of working capital stress that standard balance sheet ratios will miss.

Funders who integrate this ratio into their underwriting process for fuel-exposed small businesses gain an edge. They can identify borrowers who are effectively lending their own cash flow to larger customers through unpaid surcharges. These borrowers are higher risk, especially when the counterparty — like an airline — is cutting earnings guidance.

Furthermore, for businesses that do not have explicit pass-through clauses but operate in fuel-intensive sectors, the absence of a clause is now a primary risk factor. Lenders should treat those businesses as having direct commodity exposure and adjust pricing or advance rates accordingly. The days of assuming a small business can "just raise prices" to cover fuel are over. Airlines cannot do it, and neither can Main Street operators.

## The Broader Vertical Implications

This is not limited to aviation-adjacent small businesses. The same dynamic applies to any small business that serves large logistics providers, trucking fleets, or even courier networks. FedEx, UPS, and Amazon are all large counterparties with fuel surcharge programs. Their earnings calls frequently cite fuel as a pressure point. When the biggest players are under duress, the smallest players bear the pain first.

For ISO networks, this is an opportunity to differentiate. Run a portfolio analysis of your existing fuel-exposed borrowers. Flag those with high revenue concentration to a single large counterparty. Offer them a working capital advisory session before the next fuel shock. This positions you as a risk manager, not just a deal generator. Funders will notice lower default rates from your pipeline.

## Action Steps for Operators

1. **Audit pass-through contracts.** Not just the existence, but the enforcement history over the last 24 months. Look for gaps, caps, or payment delays.
2. **Calculate your borrowers' Fuel Surcharge Gap Ratio.** Set a threshold — a gap above 1% of revenue triggers a deeper review.
3. **Stress-test cash flow under a $5 per barrel jet fuel increase.** Model what happens to margins if the counterparty refuses to pay surcharges for two months.
4. **Redline any loan approval that assumes pass-through protection without independent verification.** The clause on paper is not the clause in practice.
5. **Monitor airline earnings calls and guidance changes.** American Airlines' 2026 cut is not an isolated event. Expect other carriers to follow, and with them, a cascade of renegotiations downstream.

The fuel cost pass-through illusion is a hidden trap in small business lending. The head fake is that contracts protect the small operator. The reality is that power asymmetry and counterparty stress turn that protection into a liability. Lenders who see through this illusion will avoid losses. Those who rely on it will find their portfolio funding the next industry write-off.

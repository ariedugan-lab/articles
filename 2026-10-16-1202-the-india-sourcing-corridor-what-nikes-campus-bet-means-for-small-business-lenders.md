# The India Sourcing Corridor: What Nike's Campus Bet Means for Small Business Lenders

Nike's India campus announcement lands during a messy turnaround. China sales are weak. The headline reads like a long-term growth bet. For lenders, that is the wrong frame. The better frame is sourcing corridor migration.

When an anchor buyer like Nike commits to a new geography, it does not just open offices. It starts building a vendor ecosystem. That ecosystem needs working capital before it needs retail traffic. Apparel cut-and-sew shops, footwear component makers, trim and packaging suppliers, testing labs, customs brokers, freight forwarders, and 3PL operators all face a ramp cycle. The money event is not the press release. The money event is the purchase order, the lease, the machinery order, and the first invoice.

Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that treat Nike India as a retail story will miss the origination pipeline. Those that treat it as a supply-chain underwriting signal can build a repeatable vertical.

## What actually changes for small business lenders

A campus commitment signals several things at once. It signals buyer intent to diversify sourcing. It signals multi-year vendor onboarding. It signals compliance and quality requirements that small suppliers must fund before revenue. It also signals a new set of trade flows: raw materials, components, finished goods, packaging, and returns.

For a small supplier, that creates four capital gaps.

First, pre-revenue ramp costs. A new buyer audit, certification, sample development, tooling, and pilot production can take months. The supplier pays wages, rent, and material deposits before the first PO converts to cash.

Second, purchase order fulfillment. PO finance can bridge the gap between accepted orders and buyer payment. But only if the PO is real, the buyer credit is confirmed, and the supplier can execute. Backlog alone is not collateral.

Third, receivables. Once goods ship, invoice factoring or asset-based lines can fund the DSO. Buyer concentration matters. If one buyer represents 70 percent of receivables, advance rates and reserve requirements must reflect that.

Fourth, equipment and facilities. Sourcing shifts often require new machines, molds, cutting tables, inspection equipment, or warehouse space. Equipment financing and commercial real estate debt become part of the same conversation.

## The tier problem is where risk hides

Most lenders want Tier 1 suppliers. Those are the names on the contract. But the working capital stress often sits at Tier 2 and Tier 3.

A Tier 1 supplier wins the Nike program. It then subcontracts components, stitching, packaging, or logistics to smaller vendors. Those smaller vendors carry longer payment terms, thinner margins, and higher rejection risk. They also lack the balance sheet to absorb a delayed ramp.

That is not a reason to avoid the corridor. It is a reason to underwrite at the contract level and the invoice level. Generic cash flow underwriting will overprice good suppliers and underprice bad ones.

Key questions for funders:

- Who is the ultimate buyer and what is the payment history?
- Is there a signed PO, a letter of credit, or a buyer acknowledgment?
- What tier is the borrower, and who pays whom?
- What is the supplier's concentration before and after the new program?
- How much of the ramp is reimbursable, and when?
- What happens if volume starts six months late?

Brokers and ISO networks should package these answers before submission. A file that says 'Nike supplier' is not a credit memo. A file that shows buyer confirmation, PO terms, historical shipments, and a clear use of funds is.

## A sourcing corridor underwriting checklist

Lenders can build a repeatable playbook for apparel, footwear, and consumer goods corridors. The same logic applies to India, Vietnam, Mexico, or any geography absorbing volume from China.

Start with demand verification. Do not lend against a press release. Verify buyer commitments. Look for job postings, supplier audits, factory registrations, import records, and shipping data. If the borrower cannot produce a PO or buyer communication, the request is venture risk, not working capital.

Then map the cash conversion cycle. For a new program, the cycle is longer than the steady state. Add development, tooling, production, transit, customs, and buyer terms. A 120-day cycle can become 180 days during a ramp. Advance rates must match.

Then isolate compliance costs. Buyers impose chemical management, labor, environmental, and traceability standards. These are not optional. They consume cash. A lender that ignores them will see unexpected draws.

Then stress the buyer concentration. Corridor migration often increases concentration before it diversifies. A supplier may go from ten small buyers to one large buyer. That can improve revenue quality and worsen credit risk at the same time.

Then structure milestones. Release capital against verified events: buyer approval, tooling completion, first shipment, accepted invoice. This is not just risk control. It is a sales tool. Suppliers want speed, but they also want a lender that understands the sequence.

## Where brokers and ISO networks should focus

The origination opportunity is not only in India. It is in every small business that touches the corridor.

Target these categories:

- Apparel and footwear contract manufacturers adding capacity.
- Component and trim suppliers with new buyer programs.
- Industrial packaging, labels, and hangtag producers.
- Testing, inspection, and compliance consultancies.
- Freight forwarders, customs brokers, and bonded warehouses.
- 3PL and last-mile operators serving new distribution nodes.
- US importers and wholesalers rebuilding inventory positions around new source countries.

Brokers should train reps to ask about sourcing changes. A simple question can surface demand: Are any of your buyers moving volume to a new country? That question opens working capital, equipment, and receivables conversations.

ISO networks should build vertical landing pages and email sequences around sourcing corridor terms. Use language buyers use: purchase order financing, supplier onboarding capital, trade receivables factoring, import working capital, and apparel supply chain lending. Avoid generic 'business loan' messaging. The borrower in this corridor has a specific problem and a specific timetable.

## What can go wrong

The biggest risk is timing. Nike can announce a campus and still take years to shift meaningful volume. India has infrastructure and tariff advantages, but China remains a massive production base. A supplier may invest ahead of demand and wait.

The second risk is buyer behavior. Anchor buyers often dual-source. They may place small pilot orders without guaranteeing volume. Lenders that fund fixed costs against pilot orders will get burned.

The third risk is documentation. Cross-border purchase orders, letters of credit, and invoices can be messy. Fraud risk is higher when documents are unfamiliar. Funders need verification workflows, not just a broker's summary.

The fourth risk is concentration. A supplier that wins one large program may look transformed. In credit terms, it may be more fragile than before.

## The operator takeaway

Nike's India campus is not a retail lending story. It is an anchor-buyer sourcing signal. That signal creates a multi-year working capital cycle across suppliers, logistics providers, and service vendors.

For funders, the play is a sourcing-corridor underwriting product: PO finance, invoice factoring, asset-based lines, equipment loans, and milestone-based working capital. For brokers and ISO networks, the play is targeted origination before banks update their credit boxes.

Do not wait for the campus to open. Build the checklist now. Verify buyer intent. Underwrite the contract. Fund the cash conversion cycle. The lenders that understand the corridor will capture the best files while competitors are still reading the headline.

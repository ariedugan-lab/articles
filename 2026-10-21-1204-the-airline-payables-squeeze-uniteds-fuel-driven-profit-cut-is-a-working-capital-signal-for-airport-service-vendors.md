## The signal is in payables, not passenger demand

United Airlines cut its 2026 profit outlook as fuel prices climbed, while travelers kept booking despite higher fares. For the small business lending market, the easy read is that travel demand remains resilient. That read misses the credit signal. Airlines facing fuel-driven margin compression protect cash at the vendor level before they cut capacity. They slow invoice approvals, stretch payment terms, renegotiate service contracts, defer discretionary work, and push cost risk onto suppliers. For small businesses that clean cabins, cater flights, handle ground operations, maintain equipment, run airport parking and shuttle services, or provide staffing and laundry, bookings can stay strong while cash conversion weakens.

This is a new underwriting angle for airport service vendors. Do not underwrite the passenger. Underwrite the payer.

## Why airport service vendors feel it first

Airport service vendors sit close to airline procurement. Many have labor-heavy cost structures and pay payroll weekly or biweekly while airline receivables turn on 30, 45, or 60 day terms. When an airline cuts its profit outlook, procurement teams look for cash and margin levers. The first levers are not always visible in public traffic data. They show up in accounts payable.

Common pressure points:

- Ground handling and cabin cleaning: labor is fixed and scheduling is tied to flight activity. If invoice approvals slow, the vendor funds payroll before it collects.
- Catering, laundry, and provisioning: inventory and spoilage risk rise when airline menus or schedules change. A fuel-driven profit cut can trigger SKU rationalization and tighter approval cycles.
- Maintenance, repair, and overhaul suppliers: AOG work remains urgent, but non-critical work orders can be deferred. Payment disputes and approval delays rise.
- Airport parking and shuttle operators: bookings may hold, but higher airfares reduce ancillary spend and fuel costs hit shuttle fleets directly. Airport access fees and labor add fixed cost.
- Security, staffing, and IT vendors: contract renewals face price pressure. Airlines may extend payment terms or demand more service for the same fee.

The result is a working capital gap that looks like a demand problem but is actually a payables problem. A lender that only reviews top-line bookings or airport traffic will misprice the risk.

## The underwriting playbook

Funders should separate demand risk from payment risk. United's profit cut does not prove travelers will stop booking. It does signal that airlines will manage cash tightly. Build the credit decision around four questions.

1. Who pays the borrower, and on what terms?
Map revenue by airline payer, contract type, and payment terms. Blended AR aging hides concentration. A vendor with 60 percent of revenue from one airline and 45 day terms is a different credit from a vendor with five airline customers and 30 day terms. Require a payer schedule and AR aging by customer.

2. Can the borrower pass through fuel and cost increases?
Review contracts for fuel surcharges, CPI escalators, index-linked pricing, and change-order mechanisms. A fixed-price contract with no fuel pass-through is a margin trap when fuel prices climb. A contract with a clear surcharge and approval SLA protects cash flow. Ask when the surcharge triggers, how it is calculated, and whether it is retroactive.

3. How fast does cash convert?
Compare days sales outstanding by payer to days payable outstanding and payroll cycles. If airline receivables stretch while payroll and lease obligations stay fixed, the borrower needs working capital. Size the facility to the cash conversion cycle, not to revenue. Use receivables financing, invoice factoring, or a working capital line sized to eligible airline invoices. Avoid long-duration term debt for a temporary payables stretch unless the contract book supports it.

4. What is the contract concentration and renewal risk?
Concentration limits matter. A single airline payer above 40 to 50 percent of revenue is a red flag unless the contract has strong termination protections, pass-through clauses, and a long renewal runway. Diversified airline customers and non-airline commercial revenue reduce risk. Check whether the borrower has direct contracts or subcontracts. Subcontractors can face slower approvals and weaker payment rights.

## Implications for funders

Funders should add airline payer concentration and contract clause review to the credit memo. Build a scorecard that weights payer-level AR aging, fuel pass-through language, contract renewal dates, and payroll coverage. Set borrowing base concentration limits by airline. Price for payables stretch. Monitor airline quarterly outlooks, fuel hedge disclosures, capacity changes, and vendor payment behavior as leading indicators. If an airline cuts profit guidance, act before the borrower's AR aging deteriorates.

Use monthly AR reporting for airport service vendors. Flag unpaid invoices over 60 days, disputed invoices, unapplied credits, and sudden changes in invoice approval. Watch for payroll advances, merchant cash advance stacking, or tax deferrals that mask a receivable problem. Those are signs the borrower is funding an airline payment delay with expensive capital.

## Implications for brokers and ISO networks

Brokers should package airport service vendor deals around payer quality, not passenger volume. Submit a payer schedule, AR aging by airline, contract excerpts with fuel and payment terms, payroll and lease schedule, and a cash conversion analysis. Do not lead with airport traffic or booking data. Lead with who pays, when they pay, and what happens if fuel prices stay high.

ISO networks should segment airport service vendors as a vertical. Train originators to ask for airline payer concentration, fuel surcharge clauses, and invoice approval SLAs. Create prequalified tiers for vendors with diversified airline customers and clear pass-through protection. Separate consumer-facing airport businesses, such as parking and shuttle operators, from pure B2B service vendors. They carry different fuel and demand exposure.

## Red flags and green flags

Red flags:
- One airline payer above 50 percent of revenue
- AR days rising by more than 15 days in a quarter
- Fixed-price contracts with no fuel surcharge
- Large unbilled work in progress or disputed invoices
- Payroll funded by short-term advances while airline receivables age
- Capex commitments tied to airline volume without contracted revenue

Green flags:
- Multiple airline payers and non-airline commercial revenue
- Fuel surcharge, CPI, and change-order clauses
- Invoice approval SLAs and prompt-pay discounts
- Stable AR aging with low disputes
- Contracted minimums or take-or-pay provisions
- Clean lien position and ability to assign receivables

## The takeaway

United's fuel-driven profit cut is not a demand warning for every travel-adjacent small business. It is a payables-cycle warning for the vendors that keep airlines running. Bookings can remain resilient while airline procurement tightens. Small business lenders that underwrite payer behavior, contract protections, and cash conversion will find opportunity in vendors with strong utilization and weak cash flow. Lenders that underwrite passenger volume will miss the risk.

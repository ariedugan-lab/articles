## The Signal

United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby is not just planning routes. He is building an airline that uses AI to make its network more efficient and its megadeals more productive. The phrase "from JFK to AI" captures a strategic shift: the future of an airline is no longer only about planes and gates. It is about how well an airline can convert operational data into reliable departures.

For small business lenders, this shift matters because airport-adjacent businesses are not actually lending against airports. They are lending against airline operations. A parking lot, a food vendor, a shuttle service, a hotel, or a freight forwarder all have cash flow tied to the same things: flight schedules, load factors, cancellations, and passenger behavior. When an airline improves its operations with AI, it changes the cash flow profile of every small business in that orbit.

## The New Underwriting Variable: Operational Efficiency

Most underwriting models for airport-adjacent small businesses rely on macro proxies. Lenders look at passenger counts, average ticket price, and seasonal revenue. That is not enough. A carrier can have growing passenger counts while its schedule reliability deteriorates. Chronic delays push ground transport demand into unpredictable peaks, reduce dwell time in concession areas, and create receivables stress for service providers that get paid on completion.

United's AI investments are designed to do the opposite. AI-led scheduling, predictive maintenance, and gate optimization compress turnaround times and reduce disruption spillover. That is a credit positive for small businesses that depend on steady flight activity. But it also creates a new underwriting gap: lenders who do not track airline operational quality will be pricing business risk without knowing whether the primary revenue driver is improving or degrading.

The concrete implication: add an airline efficiency overlay to portfolio risk scoring. For any airport-adjacent merchant, identify the dominant carrier at that airport and monitor its on-time performance, completion factor, and schedule changes. A lender that can see a carrier's AI-driven reliability improving can unlock more working capital confidence. A lender that ignores it is flying blind.

## Megadeals Are a Supplier Credit Event

Kirby's megadeals are a separate but connected signal. When an airline commits to hundreds of aircraft deliveries, it creates a long lead-time capex cycle. That cycle does not just benefit the OEMs. It creates working capital pressure and opportunity across the supplier chain: parts distributors, interior outfitters, food service equipment vendors, logistics firms, and maintenance subcontractors.

For funders, the first effect is demand. Suppliers often need bridge capital to fund inventory and labor before the airline's own milestone payments land. Revenue-based financing and asset-based lines can fill that gap. But the second effect is concentration risk. A small business supplier that books a large order with an airline-backed program may look strong on paper while its cash flow is highly dependent on delivery schedules, contract renewals, and payment terms. If the airline delays a megadeal, the supplier's invoices stretch and its borrowing cost spikes.

Underwriters should separate airlines' planned capex from committed capex. A "proposed" megadeal is not a credit event. A signed order with delivery slots is. For brokers, this means asking suppliers whether their revenue is tied to firm orders or optional purchase rights. The answer changes the risk profile.

## AI Also Changes Where Money Is Spent

AI is not only about operations. It is also about demand management. Airlines use AI to adjust pricing, rebook passengers, and steer demand across flights. That affects where and when airport-adjacent small businesses capture revenue.

Consider two businesses side by side in an airport terminal. The first is a grab-and-go vendor near a gate cluster that United uses for high-frequency, short-turnaround flights. The second is a sit-down restaurant in a quieter concourse. If United's AI optimizes connections to keep passengers moving, the grab-and-go vendor may see more transactions and the restaurant may see less dwell time. Both are "airport-adjacent," but their revenue quality is diverging.

Lenders should model not just passenger volume but passenger flow. Revenue quality for airport businesses includes dwell time, transaction frequency, and peak-hour concentration. Dynamic pricing and AI-based rebooking can shift those variables quickly. A merchant cash advance underwritten on average daily revenue is dangerous if the borrower's schedule becomes spikier. Funders need to look at daily and hourly settlement data, not just monthly totals.

## What Funders, Brokers, and ISO Networks Should Do

For funders:

- Add a "carrier quality" field to airport-adjacent deals. Log the dominant airline, its on-time performance, and its fleet expansion plans.
- Stress test borrowers whose revenue is built around a single airline's hub. Ask what happens if schedule frequency drops 15% or if average turnaround time shrinks by ten minutes.
- For suppliers, request a copy of the airline contract or purchase order. Verify whether payment terms are tied to delivery milestones, and whether the airline can cancel or defer without penalty.

For brokers and ISO networks:

- Treat airport-adjacent businesses as a distinct vertical with a specific inventory of questions. Do not sell them generic working capital.
- Ask business owners how their revenue responds to flight delays, cancellations, and schedule changes. Owners who track these variables are better credits.
- Look for businesses that have already adapted to AI-driven airline operations. A shuttle company that uses real-time flight data to dispatch vehicles has a stronger cash flow model than one that runs on printed schedules.

The key phrase is "operational awareness." The best borrowers in this vertical will be the ones who can describe how the airline's operational changes affect their daily cash cycle. The best lenders will be the ones who listen.

## Portfolio Question

At a portfolio level, the question is simple: do you know how your airport-adjacent book would perform if the dominant carrier's AI-driven efficiency gains accelerate? If yes, you can underwrite with more precision. If no, your exposure is larger than your collateral. United's strategy, from JFK to AI, is a reminder that airline strategy is now cash flow strategy for every small business in its orbit. Lenders should adjust before the data forces them to.

Best Buy reported better-than-expected earnings on May 28, 2026. Revenue and profit both beat consensus estimates. The company is still in a turnaround, but the numbers tell a clear story: the consumer electronics recession that began in late 2022 has likely hit its floor.

For small business lenders, this is not a headline to scroll past. It is a leading indicator that a large, previously distressed vertical is becoming creditworthy again. The ecosystem of small businesses that depend on electronics sales—independent computer shops, mobile repair stores, home theater installers, accessory wholesalers, and service providers—has spent two years compressing margins and burning working capital. The Best Buy signal suggests that pressure is easing.

This article outlines why the stabilisation matters, how to adjust underwriting for the survivorship bias in this cohort, and where funders, brokers, and ISOs can act before the next cycle tightens.

---

### The Vertical That Went Cold

Consumer electronics small businesses were a reliable lending vertical from 2015 through 2021. Pandemic-era stimulus and remote work drove demand for home office gear, large screens, and peripherals. Borrowers in this space showed strong revenue growth, high inventory turnover, and manageable default rates.

Then the post-boom correction hit. By mid-2023, same-store sales at Best Buy had declined for seven consecutive quarters. The entire chain—suppliers, independent retailers, service shops—felt the compression. Inventory aged, margins shrank, and lenders pulled back. The cohort became a watchlist category for many funders.

The result: a high-density pool of small businesses that survived in survival mode. Many that made it through have already cleaned up their balance sheets, cut overhead, and shifted revenue mix toward services and memberships—the same playbook Best Buy used.

Best Buy’s Q1 2027 numbers confirm that the stabilization is structural, not a one-time anomaly. Comparable sales improved. Gross margin held. The company raised its full-year guidance. That is the kind of signal operators can bank on.

---

### What the Stabilization Means for Underwriting

**1. Revenue quality matters more than revenue growth**

During the downturn, many electronics retailers leaned into high-commission, low-margin categories like extended warranties and accessories to keep top-line flat. That revenue is fragile. Best Buy’s beat came partly from improved service revenue (Totaltech memberships, installation fees) and from a return of demand in core categories like laptops and home appliances.

When underwriting an electronics small business, calculate the share of revenue from services versus product sales. A borrower where services and recurring memberships account for more than 20% of revenue is structurally less sensitive to inventory turnover shocks. That is a positive signal for a funder.

**2. Inventory aging is the hidden variable**

Electronics inventory depreciates quickly. A 12-month-old laptop model loses 30% of wholesale value. During the downturn, many businesses accumulated aged stock they could not move. That inventory is now a sunk cost on balance sheets.

Request an aging schedule on the top 10 SKUs. If the borrower still holds inventory older than 18 months, that is a red flag even if current cash flow looks healthy. Good borrowers have already written down or liquidated that stock. Bad ones are hoping for a rebound that has already passed them by.

**3. Working capital needs are shifting**

Best Buy’s turnaround involved aggressive cost cutting across supply chain and store operations. Small businesses do not have the same leverage, but they have trimmed fat. The result: working capital needs are lower per unit of revenue than they were two years ago.

A borrower that maintained revenue of $1 million in 2024 but has reduced its inventory-to-revenue ratio from 0.35 to 0.28 is a lower-risk proposition. Lenders can offer smaller lines tied to actual turnover rather than blanket inventory financing. That reduces exposure while still meeting borrower demand.

### Concrete Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISOs

**For funders:**

Review your existing portfolio of electronics-adjacent credits. Many of these borrowers were put in a watchlist or pulled from origination during the downturn. Re-evaluation is now warranted. Use the revenue quality and inventory aging criteria above to segment which ones to re-engage.

New origination in this vertical should focus on borrowers with a service revenue component. Pure hardware-only resellers are still risky because the sector has structural margin pressure from online discounters and direct-to-consumer brands. But service-heavy models—repair shops, installers, IT support for small offices—have pricing power and stickier cash flow.

**For brokers:**

The electronics vertical was underserved during the downturn. Many small business owners in this space were turned down by banks and alternative lenders alike. They are now starved for growth capital. Brokers who can present a clean underwriting narrative around service revenue and inventory turnover will have first access to quality deals.

Target independent computer retailers, mobile repair chains, and home theater installers. These businesses often have personal guarantees and strong owner equity because the owners are tech-savvy operators who kept their businesses afloat through the downturn. That is the profile that performs best in a recovering vertical.

**For ISOs:**

ISOs that service merchant aggregators or franchise networks should look at the supply chain around Best Buy. Independent sellers on Best Buy’s marketplace, third-party service providers (Geek Squad sub-contractors), and local installers who do last-mile delivery all have transactional cash flow that can be monetised through merchant cash advances or revenue-based financing.

The key negotiation point: ensure the funding structure matches the borrower’s revenue cycle. Electronics sellers experience seasonal spikes around back-to-school and holiday. A fixed ACH daily repayment schedule will conflict with that. ISOs can differentiate by offering flexible payment models tied to actual sales volume.

---

### The Risk That Remains

Stabilization is not recovery. Consumer electronics is still a cyclical vertical. The next downturn may be triggered by a trade war escalation (many components come from China and Mexico) or by a shift in consumer spending toward experiential goods (travel, dining).

Additionally, the survivorship bias is real. The companies that survived the 2022–2025 downturn are generally better managed. But a cohort of businesses that barely scraped by may now present themselves as healthy. Their financials might show improved margins because they deferred maintenance, cut marketing, or reduced owner draws. These moves are not sustainable.

Funders should stress-test borrower cash flow under a scenario where revenue dips another 10% from current levels. If the borrower can still service debt with a 10% drop and a 15-day inventory holding increase, they are safe. If not, pass.

---

### The Window

Consumer electronics small business lending is not a new vertical. It is an old one that just became viable again. The window will not stay open indefinitely. As more lenders re-enter, competition will compress margins on the funding side. The first movers who adjust underwriting to the new revenue quality signals—service mix, inventory aging, working capital efficiency—will capture the best borrowers.

Best Buy’s earnings beat is the all-clear siren. Operators who hear it and act quickly will have a tailwind. Those who wait for full recovery will find the best deals already funded.

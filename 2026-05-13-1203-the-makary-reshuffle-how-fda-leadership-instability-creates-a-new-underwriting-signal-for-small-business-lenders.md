On May 12, 2026, FDA Commissioner Marty Makary resigned amid internal dysfunction, industry backlash, and White House pressure. The departure marks a turning point for the agency’s stability. For small business lenders, brokers, and ISO networks, this event is not a distant political story. It is a concrete underwriting signal.

Every business that touches FDA-regulated products — dietary supplements, medical devices, food manufacturing, cosmetics, or pharmaceuticals — now faces a period of regulatory drift. Approvals slow down. Enforcement priorities shift. Compliance costs rise. Revenue streams become less predictable.

Lenders who ignore this signal will price risk incorrectly. Brokers who fail to educate borrowers will see defaults climb. ISOs that don’t adjust originations will lose portfolio quality.

This article breaks down the direct implications for your lending operation and provides actionable steps to protect your book.

## The FDA Vacuum and Its Immediate Effect on Small Businesses

The FDA does not operate on autopilot. A commissioner sets enforcement priorities, approval timelines, and rulemaking agendas. When the chair leaves mid-tenure, especially under acrimonious circumstances, career staff hesitate. Political appointees leave or are reassigned. Policy initiatives stall.

For small businesses, this means:

- **Delayed product approvals.** A medical device startup that expected a 510(k) clearance in eight months may now wait twelve to eighteen. That delays revenue and forces bridge financing.
- **Shifting enforcement focus.** New leadership often targets different compliance areas. A supplement company that passed an FDA inspection last year might face a different set of questions under the next commissioner.
- **Regulatory whiplash.** Temporary guidance documents become permanent. Permanent rules get rescinded. Small businesses cannot plan capital expenditures when the rules change quarterly.

Concrete example: The supplement industry, largely populated by small- to mid-sized manufacturers and marketers, relies on FDA’s Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) framework. A new commissioner could tighten or loosen Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) enforcement. Lenders funding inventory for a supplement brand must assess whether the borrower’s compliance posture is adequate for the next regulatory wave.

## Underwriting Adjustments for Funders

Funders originate deals based on cash flow, collateral, and industry risk. The Makary resignation adds a new layer: regulatory trajectory risk.

Here is how to operationalize this in your underwriting:

### 1. Review Borrower Regulatory Dependencies

Start by segmenting your portfolio by FDA exposure. Identify borrowers in:
- Dietary supplements
- Medical devices
- Food and beverage (especially functional foods with health claims)
- Cosmetics (FDA authority is expanding under MoCRA)
- Animal health products

For each borrower, ask:
- Do they have any pending FDA applications or approvals? If so, what is the timeline? Has it slipped?
- Have they received any FDA warning letters, Form 483s, or import alerts in the past 24 months? If yes, that is a red flag in a shifting enforcement environment.
- Do they have a regulatory affairs team or outside counsel? A borrower with no regulatory support is a higher risk.

### 2. Adjust Revenue Quality Metrics

Revenue from FDA-regulated products is not all equal. A supplement company that derives 80% of revenue from a single product awaiting FDA approval is riskier than one with a diversified portfolio and established compliance systems.

Lenders should create a “regulatory quality” score within their credit box. For example:
- Tier 1: Revenue from products fully approved and with no pending regulatory actions.
- Tier 2: Revenue from products that are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) or have existing FDA clearances but face potential rule changes.
- Tier 3: Revenue dependent on pending approvals, premarket notifications, or exempt categories that could be reclassified.

Apply higher reserve requirements or lower advance rates for Tier 3 revenue.

### 3. Stress-Test for Compliance Cost Increases

In an unstable regulatory environment, compliance costs rise. Borrowers may need to:
- Hire additional quality assurance staff
- Invest in new laboratory testing
- Update labeling
- Recall products

Run a breakeven analysis assuming a 10-20% increase in compliance spending. If the borrower’s debt service coverage ratio drops below 1.2x under that scenario, the deal is too risky until the regulatory picture clears.

### 4. Shorten Repricing Triggers

Consider adding quarterly covenants tied to regulatory milestones. For example, a medical device startup must provide proof of ongoing FDA communications. Failure to produce documentation triggers a rate reprice or increased collateral requirements.

## Broker and ISO Network Implications

Brokers and ISOs are the front line. Borrowers will ask for funding to cover regulatory delays or compliance upgrades. If you do not understand the new risk, you will originate deals that blow up your funding partners and hurt your own reputation.

### Educate Borrowers Before They Apply

Brokers should proactively inform clients in FDA-regulated sectors about the potential for slower approvals and higher costs. Suggest they:
- Obtain a regulatory audit from a third party
- Build a cash reserve equal to three months of compliance spending
- Diversify product lines away from single-approval dependencies

When a borrower comes with a request for a large lump sum to bridge a regulatory gap, ask hard questions. Is this a one-time delay or a sign of deeper systemic problems? If the borrower cannot articulate a clear regulatory timeline, pass on the deal.

### Update Your ISO Compliance Checklists

ISOs that manage a network of brokers should issue a regulatory risk bulletin. Make it mandatory for brokers to flag any borrower in an FDA-regulated industry. Provide a simple questionnaire:
- Does the borrower have any pending FDA actions?
- Has the borrower experienced a significant change in regulatory staffing?
- Is the borrower’s revenue concentrated in a single product category that is under regulatory review?

Set a maximum exposure limit for deals in industries with high regulatory uncertainty. For example, cap originations to supplement companies at 10% of your monthly volume until the new FDA commissioner is confirmed and policy direction is clear.

### Monitor Portfolio Concentration

If your ISO has a high concentration of healthcare or supplement deals, now is the time to diversify. The Makary resignation is a systemic event for that sector. Do not wait for defaults to mount. Proactively source deals in less regulated industries like professional services, retail, or construction.

## The Timing Window for Lenders

Uncertainty creates both risk and opportunity. Some borrowers will become desperate for capital as their regulatory timelines slip. Lenders that move quickly can capture higher spreads on short-term bridge loans — but only if they have the underwriting discipline to separate temporary liquidity needs from permanent structural problems.

The most aggressive lenders will offer “regulatory bridge” products with:
- Higher rates (500-700 bps above standard)
- Shorter durations (6-9 months)
- Personal guarantees + blanket liens on all assets
- Milestone-based draw schedules tied to FDA submission dates

These are not for the faint of heart. They require deep sector expertise and constant monitoring.

The safer play is to sit on the sidelines until the new commissioner’s agenda becomes clear. Use the next 60-90 days to tighten criteria, re-underwrite existing exposure, and prepare for the next wave.

## What to Watch Next

Pay attention to three signals:

1. **The new commissioner nomination.** If the nominee is a hardliner on enforcement, compliance costs will spike. If the nominee is industry-friendly, approvals may accelerate but quality control could loosen. Either way, the risk profile shifts.

2. **Congressional hearings.** Oversight hearings often reveal enforcement priorities. Lobby which committees are targeting supplement or device regulation.

3. **FDA budget appropriations.** A flat or reduced budget means longer approval times and fewer inspections. That increases risk for any borrower that relies on FDA feedback.

Bookmark FDA.gov and set up Google Alerts for “FDA pending approval” and “supplement FDA warning letter.” This is not passive reading. It is active risk management.

## Final Take

The Makary resignation is not a headline to scroll past. It is a re-rating event for any small business with FDA exposure. Lenders who update their credit criteria now will avoid the losses that come with regulatory whiplash. Brokers who counsel borrowers proactively will strengthen their relationships. ISOs that enforce new compliance checklists will protect their portfolio quality.

Do not wait for defaults to tell you the risk was real. Act on this signal today.

The used car market is no longer cycling through a post-pandemic correction. It has landed on a new, higher plateau that shows no signs of descending. Automakers learned during the chip shortage that lean inventory and controlled supply produce stronger margins and pricing power. They have no incentive to revert to the old model of flooding lots with units. For small business lenders who finance independent used car dealers, this structural shift rewrites every assumption about collateral value, inventory velocity, and dealer solvency.

**The Old Depreciation Curve Is Dead**

Traditional floor plan lending relied on predictable depreciation schedules. A 3-year-old sedan loses 15 to 20 percent of its value each year. Lenders set advance rates at 80 to 85 percent of wholesale value, confident that even a forced liquidation would cover the note. That model assumed a stable supply of new cars that would push used prices down over time.

That supply is not coming back. Automakers have publicly committed to maintaining production discipline. The days of building 100 days’ worth of inventory are over. New vehicle supply remains 20 to 30 percent below pre-pandemic levels. As a result, used car prices have not reverted to the 2019 trend line. They stabilized at a level roughly 35 percent higher than the old baseline and are holding there.

For a floor plan lender, this means the collateral backing each loan is worth more than the historical model predicted. That sounds like a safety margin. It is not. The real risk is that the floor has risen without a corresponding adjustment in how lenders measure dealer health. A dealer who bought inventory at the inflated price has no room for error. If demand softens, the same vehicle that held its value yesterday could drop 10 percent overnight when a new model year floods the market or interest rates push buyers out. The depreciation curve is now step-function, not linear.

**Inventory Velocity Becomes the Primary Credit Signal**

In the old regime, lenders focused on days-on-lot as a secondary metric. Primary weight went to personal credit scores and aggregate financials. That approach is insufficient now. A dealer can have a strong balance sheet and a 750 credit score, but if inventory sits for 60 days instead of 30, the margin on that unit evaporates. The cost of capital on a floor plan line is typically 1 to 2 percent per month. When a vehicle carries a 5 percent margin and sits for 60 days, the interest alone eats half the profit. A 90-day hold turns profit into loss.

Lenders must elevate inventory turnover to the top of the underwriting stack. Require dealers to report unit-level days-on-lot. Set maximum allowable aging thresholds in loan covenants. Funders who already use real-time inventory feeds from dealer management systems should integrate velocity triggers that automatically flag accounts where average days-on-lot exceeds 45 days. Brokers and ISOs can add value by advising dealer clients to maintain turnover discipline before they apply for funding. A dealer with sub-30-day turnover across the lot presents a fundamentally different risk profile than one with 45-day turnover, even if the financials look identical.

**Collateral Valuation Must Shift from Static Guides to Dynamic Pricing**

NADA Guides and Black Book provide lagging indicators. They reflect transactions from two to six weeks ago. In a market where prices can move 5 percent on a single auction day, lagged valuations create a blind spot for lenders. The solution is to incorporate real-time wholesale price feeds from platforms like Manheim, KAR, or ADESA. Lenders should set advance rates based on the current auction price trend, not the printed guide value.

A practical approach: use a trailing 7-day moving average of auction prices for the specific make, model, and mileage bracket. Cap advance rates at 75 percent of that figure for inventory aged under 30 days. For inventory aged 30 to 45 days, drop the advance to 65 percent and require a principal paydown. For inventory over 45 days, require a floor plan reduction or a personal guarantee top-up. This dynamic structure protects the lender from the downside of a rapid price shift while still allowing the dealer to operate with adequate working capital.

**Dealer Margin Compression Is the Second-Order Risk**

Higher acquisition costs compress dealer margins. The typical independent used car dealer operates on a gross margin of 10 to 12 percent. When the cost of the vehicle itself is 30 percent higher than three years ago, the same absolute profit dollars now represent a thinner percentage. That leaves less room to absorb reconditioning costs, advertising, and floor plan interest.

Lenders must stress-test dealer cash flow under margin scenarios. Run a projection assuming a 2 percent reduction in gross margin. If the dealer’s net income turns negative under that stress, the loan is too risky at the current advance rate. Funders should also watch for dealers who respond to margin compression by chasing volume. A dealer who needs to sell 30 cars a month to hit the same profit they used to get from 20 is taking on more inventory risk, more floor plan debt, and more exposure to market softening. Broker networks can train their originators to ask dealers point-blank: “How has your per-unit gross margin changed in the last 12 months?” If the answer is down more than 15 percent, proceed with caution.

**The Opportunity for Lenders Who Adapt**

The structural used car price floor is not a temporary disruption. It is the new normal. Lenders who adjust their underwriting frameworks now will capture the best independent dealers while competitors relying on outdated models will either overexpose themselves to risk or miss quality origination because they cannot properly price the risk.

Action items for funders:

- Replace static LTV tables with dynamic advance rates tied to real-time auction data.
- Incorporate inventory turnover as a binding covenant, not a reporting metric.
- Run margin-compression stress tests on every floor plan renewal.
- Require dealers to carry inventory insurance with a deductible no higher than $1,000 per unit.
- Set maximum inventory age covenants at 45 days, with automatic margin calls at 60 days.

Action items for brokers and ISOs:

- Educate dealer clients on the importance of turnover velocity before they seek funding.
- Collect per-unit margin trends as part of the initial application package.
- Flag dealers whose average days-on-lot exceeds 45 days as high-risk referrals.
- Recommend that dealers use real-time inventory valuation tools to maintain transparency with lenders.

This is not a story about rising prices. It is a story about permanently changed economics in the used car supply chain. Small business lenders who treat it as a cyclical blip will find their portfolios repriced by the market. Those who build for the structural reality will gain a durable underwriting advantage.

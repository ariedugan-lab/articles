# The Two-Speed Store Base: Starbucks’ Split Quarter Is a Store-Level Underwriting Signal

Starbucks reported a U.S. comparable sales beat while international sales missed expectations. The easy read is that the U.S. turnaround is working and the global business is not. For small business lenders, that read is too broad. The useful signal is a two-speed store base: U.S. store-level cash flow is improving in selected markets while international store-level cash flow is uneven. Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that lend to coffee retail vendors, equipment suppliers, renovation contractors, distributors, and licensed-store operators need to underwrite to the end-customer store cohort, not the brand headline.

A brand-level comp does not tell you whether a specific borrower gets paid. It does not tell you whether a remodel purchase order converts into an invoice, whether an international distributor will delay payment, or whether an independent coffee shop faces more competition from a stronger Starbucks store nearby. The lending decision sits at the borrower and contract level. The Starbucks quarter is a reminder to segment the portfolio.

## The U.S. beat is a capex and vendor cash-flow signal, not a blanket recovery

If U.S. turnaround momentum continues, the first place it shows up for small businesses is spending. Store remodels, equipment, digital pickup, labor scheduling, and supply chain upgrades create purchase orders for vendors. That can support working capital, equipment financing, and accounts receivable lines. But the revenue is not automatic. Vendors often carry labor and materials before they bill. Renovation contractors face milestone billing, retainage, and change orders. Equipment suppliers face delivery, acceptance, and installation risk. Distributors face inventory build ahead of orders.

For funders, the relevant question is not whether Starbucks U.S. comps beat expectations. It is whether the borrower has a verified purchase order, a delivery schedule, and a clean receivable from a creditworthy end customer. Use purchase-order finance and asset-based lines only when you can trace the order to a specific store program or supplier contract. Do not let a brand-level press release replace documentation.

For brokers and ISO networks, this is a packaging opportunity. Ask applicants for the share of revenue tied to U.S. store remodels, the customer concentration, and the payment terms. A vendor with three large customers and a six-week billing lag needs a different structure than a distributor with diversified small accounts. The first file needs concentration limits and receivable verification. The second file needs inventory monitoring and margin checks.

## The international miss is a separate underwriting pool

Starbucks international sales falling short of estimates is not a U.S. vendor problem. It is a different cash-flow pool. Small businesses that export, distribute imported goods, or serve international store operators can see order delays, cancellations, and slower receivables. A U.S. comparable sales beat does not offset that risk. Funders should split exposure by geography in the borrower's revenue mix. If a borrower gets most of its revenue from U.S. store programs, price it as U.S. exposure. If a meaningful share depends on international store openings or distributor orders, underwrite the payment behavior and order pipeline separately.

This split matters for borrowing-base calculations. A lender may include U.S. receivables that are current and verified. It may exclude or discount international receivables with longer payment histories or disputed invoices. It may require letters of credit or credit insurance for cross-border orders. Brokers should not submit a blended file that hides the geography. ISO networks should train referral partners to ask where the goods ship and who pays the invoice. The answer changes the risk.

## Licensed stores and independent coffee shops are not the same channel

Starbucks operates company stores and licenses stores in airports, grocery, campus, and travel locations. A U.S. comp beat at company stores does not automatically translate to licensed-store operators. Those operators carry different rent, labor, traffic, and contract terms. Lenders serving licensed stores need location-level data: lease terms, royalty or license fees, traffic drivers, and seasonality. A strong brand does not protect a weak location.

Independent coffee shops are another channel. A stronger Starbucks turnaround can signal category demand, but it can also increase competitive pressure. Do not approve an independent cafe loan because a national brand reported better U.S. comps. Underwrite local traffic, lease burden, labor availability, menu margin, and the owner's cash buffer. The national headline is a macro input, not a repayment source.

## Underwriting moves for the next 90 days

1. Map end-customer exposure. Build a simple table for each borrower: U.S. store programs, international store programs, licensed stores, independent shops, and non-coffee customers. Require percentages. Set concentration limits where one program or one geography drives most revenue.

2. Verify the order. For vendors, require purchase orders, master service agreements, delivery schedules, and proof of acceptance. For renovation contractors, require milestone bills and retainage terms. For equipment suppliers, require installation sign-off. Tie advances to completed work, not announced capex.

3. Stress the receivable. Pull accounts receivable aging by customer and geography. Look for slow-paying international distributors, disputed invoices, and credits. Adjust the borrowing base for any receivable that depends on a delayed store opening.

4. Test the working capital gap. Calculate the cash conversion cycle by customer. A vendor with 60-day payables and 90-day receivables has a funding need even with strong orders. Size the line to the gap, not the backlog.

5. Monitor leading indicators. Track purchase-order volume, invoice aging, payroll, rent, and customer concentration monthly. For contractors, track percentage completion and change orders. For distributors, track inventory turns and order cancellations. Red flags include revenue booked on announcements without POs, inventory build without shipping schedules, and receivables aging from international counterparties.

## The operator takeaway

Starbucks' split quarter gives small business lenders a clean segmentation lesson. The U.S. beat is a store-level and vendor-level signal. The international miss is a separate risk pool. Neither should be used as a blanket approval or decline. Funders that underwrite to verified orders, geography, and end-customer cash flow will price risk better. Brokers and ISO networks that package files with that evidence will place more deals with fewer surprises. The brand headline is context. The borrower's contract, receivable, and store cohort are the credit.

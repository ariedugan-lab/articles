# The Settlement-Duration Gap: Underwriting Regulatory Promises in Small Business Lending

When a merger closes, the press release focuses on scale. The settlement agreement focuses on behavior. Small business lenders need to read the second document. Paramount's commitments to Hollywood to land WBD are a live example. The company promised theatrical releases and other concessions for five years. Skeptics are already asking what happens when the clock runs out. For a funder, broker, or ISO network, that question is not cultural commentary. It is a credit trigger.

The issue is settlement-duration revenue. This is revenue that exists because a regulator, court, or merger condition forces a large buyer to behave in a certain way for a defined period. It looks like contracted revenue on bank statements. It rarely has the legal durability of a receivable. The borrower may have invoices, purchase orders, and steady deposits. But the demand generator is a time-boxed promise, not an open-ended commercial relationship.

Funders that underwrite settlement-duration revenue like ordinary B2B revenue will misprice risk. Brokers that package it as stable contract revenue will get declined. ISO networks that ignore it will see renewal and default surprises when the commitment expires. The fix is not to avoid the vertical. It is to underwrite the expiration.

## The Difference Between a Contract and a Settlement

A contractual receivable has four features: an enforceable payment obligation, a defined deliverable, assignability, and remedies for breach. A settlement commitment has different features: behavioral obligations, a time limit, compliance monitoring, no automatic renewal, and political or legal risk. If the commitment ends, the buyer can revert to prior behavior without breaching the contract. That is the gap.

Consider a production equipment rental company in Los Angeles or Atlanta. It buys trucks, cameras, lighting, and stage equipment. Revenue jumped after a studio committed to theatrical slates. The owner wants working capital to expand. The funder sees 18 months of rising deposits. But the commitment has three years left. The loan term is five years. The funder is financing an asset beyond the revenue floor. If the studio pulls back after year three, the borrower still owes two years of payments.

That is not a hypothetical. It is the basic term-mismatch problem in event-driven lending. The same pattern appears in healthcare pilots, energy tax credit step-downs, defense procurement commitments, and local subsidy programs. A policy creates demand. A small business builds capacity. A lender finances the capacity. Then the policy changes.

## Why This Hits Small Business Lenders First

Large suppliers can absorb a commitment expiration. They have diversified buyers, legal teams, and refinancing options. Small businesses do not. A 20-person production services firm may have one studio buyer at 45 percent of revenue. A theater concession operator may have one multiplex contract tied to a release slate. A post-production shop may have invested in editing bays because a merger settlement required more theatrical output.

For a funder, the risk shows up in payment behavior. The borrower may pay on time for 30 months. Then the commitment ends. Revenue falls. The borrower uses reserves to make payments. Then it misses. By the time the funder sees the miss, the leading indicators were visible for two quarters.

For a broker, the risk shows up in declines and clawbacks. If the broker sold the deal as a stable contract-backed loan, the funder will reprice or decline the next file. For an ISO network, the risk shows up in merchant attrition. A merchant that loses settlement-driven revenue will churn or default. That damages renewal revenue and referral relationships.

## The Underwriting Playbook

1. Separate base revenue from settlement revenue. Ask for monthly revenue with and without the merger-commitment work. If the borrower cannot split it, treat all disputed revenue as settlement revenue. Do not average across the commitment start. Use the base case.

2. Get the document. Request the settlement agreement, merger order, side letters, supplier master service agreements, and purchase orders citing committed volumes. If the borrower only has a press release, treat the revenue as uncommitted. Press releases are not collateral.

3. Map the calendar. Identify the commitment start, end, renewal options, performance gates, termination for convenience, force majeure, and change-of-control provisions. Build a maturity wall for every settlement-driven relationship. The expiration date is as important as the payment history.

4. Match term to duration. If the commitment ends in 36 months, do not offer 60-month amortization without a credible refinance plan. Use shorter amortization, cash sweep, or a step-down line of credit. For revenue-based financing and merchant cash advance, set withholdings based on base-case revenue, not the settlement uplift.

5. Stress zero renewal. Model a 20 to 40 percent revenue decline in the first 90 days after expiration. Check debt service coverage, fixed-charge coverage, and collection capacity. If the borrower cannot service debt on base revenue, the loan is a bet on renewal. Price it that way or decline.

6. Cap concentration. One studio, one regulator, or one settlement can dominate a small business. Set exposure limits by counterparty and by regulatory event. If 30 percent of a portfolio depends on the same merger condition, that is correlated risk. Correlated risk does not diversify away.

7. Monitor leading indicators. Track slate cuts, release date shifts, production starts, permit volume, payroll hours, vendor payment days, theater attendance, box office concentration, union disputes, and studio executive turnover. These metrics move before the commitment expires.

8. Covenant the risk. Require quarterly settlement-revenue reporting. Trigger reserves or step-down availability if commitment volume falls below a threshold. Require notice of any regulatory modification, renewal, or termination. Make the borrower disclose the expiration date in every renewal.

## Implications for Funders, Brokers, and ISO Networks

Funders should build a settlement-duration exposure report. Tag loans by regulatory event. Stress-test expiration quarters. For media supply chain lending, track studio concentration and release slate dependency. For healthcare, track reimbursement pilot expirations. For energy, track tax credit step-downs. Settlement-duration risk is not only Hollywood.

Brokers should package transparency. Prepare a one-page settlement revenue memo. State the buyer, commitment, expiration, renewal likelihood, and borrower replacement pipeline. Label base revenue and settlement revenue separately. Funders price clarity better than surprises. A broker who hides the expiration will lose the account when the file is declined.

ISO networks should train representatives to ask three questions on every media-adjacent file: What percent of revenue is tied to a merger or regulatory commitment. When does it end. What is the replacement pipeline. If the answers are vague, position the deal as base revenue plus a time-limited uplift, not as stable contract revenue. That protects approval rates and renewal commissions.

## Underwrite the Expiration, Not the Headline

The next credit cycle will not reward lenders for spotting the loudest headline. It will reward those who separate durable cash flow from time-boxed regulatory demand. Settlement-duration revenue can be bankable. It cannot be treated as permanent. Underwrite the expiration, not the promise. That is how funders protect capital, brokers win approvals, and ISO networks build repeatable volume in event-driven verticals.

## The headline most lenders read wrong

BJ's Wholesale is culling SKUs. Lululemon is trimming assortments. The standard read is merchandising: curate the floor, cut the tail, get back to healthy comps. That read is fine for equity analysts. It is incomplete for anyone who funds a small business whose balance sheet carries inventory.

Assortment cuts are a working capital event. When a retailer drops 15 percent of its SKUs, that decision cascades backward through a supplier base and sideways into every lender holding inventory as collateral. The question for funders, brokers, and ISO networks is not whether curating assortments is smart. It is what happens to advance rates, collateral values, and repayment capacity when the merchant two tiers down loses shelf space.

## What is actually changing

Traditional assortment strategy optimized for breadth: more choice, more chances to convert. That model worked when inventory carrying costs were cheap and consumer attention was abundant. Neither holds now.

Retailers are shifting to depth over breadth. Fewer SKUs, deeper buys, faster turns. The operative metrics are turn rate, gross margin return on inventory investment (GMROI), and open-to-buy discipline. A merchant that cuts SKUs but holds total inventory dollars flat is not shrinking. It is reallocating capital into fewer bets with higher expected sell-through.

That reallocation is where the lending story lives. Three things move at once:

1. Inventory composition gets more concentrated per SKU.
2. Supplier count shrinks and per-supplier volume rises.
3. Markdown exposure migrates from a long tail of slow movers into a smaller set of high-volume bets.

Each of these changes the risk profile of an inventory-backed line even when the headline borrowing base stays stable.

## Collateral quality is not the same as collateral quantity

Most inventory advance rates are still built on category-level assumptions. Apparel gets one rate, electronics another, seasonal goods another. That framework assumes a broad assortment behaves like a blended pool. Assortment cuts break the assumption.

When a merchant cuts from 400 SKUs to 300 and doubles down on the 300, the borrowing base may look identical on paper. The concentration is not. A single poor read on a hero SKU now represents a larger share of the collateral. If that SKU misses, markdown recovery drops faster than the blended rate implies.

Funders should ask three questions before renewing an inventory line where the borrower sells into a concentrated retail channel:

- What percentage of the borrowing base sits in the top ten SKUs?
- How does that concentration compare to the last twelve months?
- Has the retailer imposed new return-to-vendor or markdown-sharing terms as a condition of holding shelf space?

If the answers are moving, the advance rate should move with them. Concentration is a pricing input, not a footnote.

## The supplier compression problem

Assortment cuts do not distribute pain evenly. They concentrate it. A retailer culling 20 percent of SKUs typically eliminates entire vendors, not line items spread evenly across the base. A smaller supplier can lose 40 to 60 percent of its revenue in one buying cycle.

For lenders, this creates two separate exposures.

The first is direct. Any small business lender with exposure to a supplier that just lost a major retail account is now funding a business with a structurally impaired revenue base. Existing receivables may still collect, but the forward book is damaged. Renewal decisions need to be made on forward revenue, not trailing.

The second is indirect and more subtle. Suppliers that survive the cut often win more volume but with worse terms. Retailers demanding deeper buys, longer payment windows, and markdown protection push working capital stress upstream. The surviving supplier looks healthier on revenue and weaker on cash conversion. That is a classic setup for a payment default that shows up six to nine months after the order book looks strong.

Brokers and ISO networks should be screening their books for suppliers where a single retail customer represents more than 25 percent of revenue. That threshold is no longer conservative in an environment where assortment reviews happen twice a year.

## Liquidation assumptions need a rewrite

Most inventory lenders rely on historical recovery rates by category. Those rates were built during a period of broad assortments, where a distressed merchant could liquidate across a wide pool of buyers.

Concentrated assortments liquidate differently. If a merchant is holding deep positions in a narrow set of SKUs, the secondary market for those SKUs is also concentrated. Liquidators price that risk in. Recovery rates on concentrated inventory can run 10 to 20 points below the category blended rate, particularly for branded goods where channel restrictions limit where inventory can be sold.

Funders should re-run recovery assumptions on any deal where the borrower's inventory mix has shifted toward depth. Do not accept a category rate. Ask what the liquidation value looks like if the top five SKUs must move at once.

## What operators should do now

For funders:

- Add SKU concentration to the collateral monitoring pack. Require monthly top-ten SKU reporting on inventory lines above a defined threshold.
- Re-price or reduce advance rates where concentration has increased without a corresponding improvement in turn rate.
- Stress test the borrowing base against a single-SKU markdown scenario, not just a category downturn.

For brokers and ISO networks:

- Qualify borrowers on customer concentration, not just revenue and deposits. A supplier with three retail accounts and one at 40 percent is a different credit than the same supplier diversified across ten accounts.
- Treat assortment-cut announcements as a portfolio trigger. When a major retailer signals SKU reductions, pull the supplier list and identify which clients are exposed.
- Position working capital products to the suppliers that survived the cut but now carry deeper inventory and longer terms. That is a real financing need, and it is underserved by banks that only look at trailing financials.

## The underwriting takeaway

Assortment rationalization is not a retail story with a lending footnote. It is a working capital event that changes collateral concentration, supplier viability, and liquidation value at the same time. Funders who keep underwriting inventory as a blended pool will under-price the risk. Funders who underwrite at the SKU and customer level will find both protection and opportunity.

The retailers already made their call. The lending market has not caught up.

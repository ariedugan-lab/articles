## MGM and People Inc. Is a Working-Capital Signal, Not Just Casino M&A

MGM's CEO leaving the door open to a People Inc. bid is a headline for equity desks. For small business lenders, it is a working-capital signal. Casino dealmaking does not stay inside the boardroom. It moves through procurement, vendor contracts, event calendars, staffing schedules, and payment terms. Those are the exact inputs that determine whether a small business can service debt, factor invoices, or repay a merchant cash advance.

The reported sequence matters. People Inc. withdrew its $48.30-per-share proposal to buy the rest of MGM. MGM was exploring an offer for People Inc. That is not a clean acquisition. It is a contested, two-way situation with a long uncertainty window. Uncertainty is a cash-flow event for every small business that depends on casino properties.

Lenders that treat casino M&A as background noise will misprice risk. Funders that build a gaming-adjacent underwriting screen now will avoid losses and capture a vertical.

## Why Casino Ownership Uncertainty Hits Small Vendors First

A casino is not a single borrower. It is an ecosystem of small businesses: food and beverage distributors, linen and laundry services, HVAC and electrical contractors, security firms, valet and transportation operators, AV and event production shops, staffing agencies, and local retailers that depend on visitor traffic.

When a bid emerges or a merger is explored, management teams turn inward. Discretionary spending pauses. Capital projects get deferred. Procurement reviews start. Vendor consolidation becomes a synergy target. Payment terms can stretch as the operator preserves liquidity for deal costs.

The small businesses feel this before they see a revenue decline. A purchase order slows. A contract renewal gets pushed. A renovation project moves to next quarter. An event series gets postponed. For a lender relying on trailing bank deposits, those signals are invisible until the cash gap is already open.

## Four Underwriting Tests for Gaming-Adjacent Borrowers

### 1. Customer Concentration and Change-of-Control Clauses

Ask every applicant how much revenue comes from casino, racino, or gaming resort customers. If one property represents more than 20 percent of revenue, treat it as concentration risk. If it represents more than 35 percent, stress the file.

Read the master service agreement. Many contracts have change-of-control provisions. A new owner can terminate, renegotiate, or rebid the work. If the contract is not assignable, the receivable is conditional. Funders should reduce advance amounts, shorten repayment terms, or require personal guarantees and additional collateral. Brokers should flag these clauses at submission instead of letting underwriting discover them later.

### 2. Receivables Aging and Payment Terms

Casino operators often extend payables during deal negotiations. A vendor on 45-day terms can suddenly face 75-day payments. That creates a payroll and supplier gap. Pull accounts receivable aging, not just bank statements. Calculate true days sales outstanding. Compare the last 30 days to the prior six months.

If the borrower relies on invoice factoring or receivables financing, verify the debtor concentration and dispute risk. A single casino operator with slow pay can freeze the entire facility. For MCA underwriting, do not assume card sales. Many casino vendors invoice B2B. Look for large ACH deposits from casino entities and match them to invoices.

### 3. Capex and Event Pipeline Quality

Casino properties spend on renovations, shows, restaurants, and entertainment. That spending creates project revenue for small contractors and production companies. During a bid war, nonessential capex freezes. The project pipeline becomes less reliable.

Separate recurring maintenance revenue from project revenue. Recurring service contracts are higher quality. Project work is lower quality during ownership uncertainty. Lenders should apply different advance rates and terms to each. A borrower with 70 percent project revenue tied to one casino is not the same as a janitorial company with multi-property recurring contracts.

### 4. Labor and Scheduling Volatility

Casinos run 24/7. Staffing agencies, security, valet, cleaning, and transportation companies depend on occupancy and event schedules. If deal talks slow group bookings, promotions, or entertainment calendars, hours get cut. Payroll volume drops before bank deposits do.

Monitor payroll volume and shift counts. Ask for weekly hours by property. A staffing firm with falling hours at a casino customer is an early warning. Funders should tighten remittance frequency and review line utilization. ISO networks should train reps to ask about shift cancellations and schedule changes.

## What Funders Should Do Now

Build a casino-exposure flag in your CRM. Screen applications for NAICS codes tied to hospitality, food service, staffing, security, transportation, and entertainment services. Ask three questions at intake:

- What percentage of revenue comes from gaming properties?
- Who are your top two customers, and what are the contract terms?
- Do your contracts allow termination or renegotiation on a change of control?

For renewals, reduce exposure to single-property vendors. Shorten terms. Increase remittance frequency. Require monthly AR aging. For new originations, price for contract churn. A diversified gaming vendor can qualify for standard small business lending products. A concentrated project vendor should move to a higher-risk tier or be declined.

## Broker and ISO Implications

Deal flow will increase if casino M&A creates cash gaps. Vendors facing slow payables will seek working capital. But quality will bifurcate. Brokers who pre-screen for customer concentration and contract assignability will place better files. ISOs that submit blind MCA applications will see early defaults and lost renewals.

Use a tiered approach. Tier one: multi-property service providers with recurring revenue and diversified customers. Tier two: single-property vendors with recurring contracts and no change-of-control termination. Tier three: project-based vendors concentrated in one casino. Price accordingly.

There is also an opportunity. Consolidation creates spinouts, management rollups, and new procurement platforms. Lenders can target vendors that serve multiple operators. Brokers can position as gaming-supply chain specialists. ISO networks can build campaigns around casino vendor financing.

## The Hidden MCA Risk

Merchant cash advance underwriting depends on future card receivables. Many casino vendors do not process card sales. They invoice. If a funder uses a card-based model on an invoice-based business, the repayment math breaks. Use invoice-backed products or AR lines when appropriate. If you use MCA, verify ACH deposits from casino entities. Watch for stacking. A vendor waiting on slow casino payables may take multiple advances. That is a default signal, not a growth signal.

## The Bottom Line

MGM leaving the door open to a People Inc. bid is not just casino dealmaking. It is a working-capital event. The winners of the bid battle will be decided by shareholders. The cash-flow consequences will be decided by procurement teams and contract terms. Small business lenders should underwrite the contract, not the casino brand. Monitor receivables aging, customer concentration, change-of-control clauses, and payroll volume. Adjust limits and terms before the cash gap appears. Funders, brokers, and ISO networks that treat casino M&A as an underwriting signal will protect capital and open a new vertical in gaming-adjacent small business lending.
